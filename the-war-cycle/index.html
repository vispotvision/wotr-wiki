The War Cycle

Nov 17, 20266 min read

Every child in the Arrow valleys can sing you Ser Aldric and the Cart. In the fourth summer of the Marrowmere war Ser Aldric Noon walked out onto Harrow Field at sunrise in a coat of aura the orange of a kiln mouth, a Master on a lord’s retainer with a herald running ahead of him to cry his name, and before the porridge was ready he had unmade twelve hundred of the Lady of Sallowby’s spearmen, with their banners, their drums and a fair part of the hill. Camp followers on both sides climbed onto the wagons to watch. A bookie in the Sallowby baggage paid out at nine to one with tears running into his beard. The Lady’s quartermaster, a one-eyed carter’s widow named Bet Oxley, did not look up from her tally. She sent four hundred men and sixty wagons around the far side of the hill to the mills at Cobb’s Reach, where they sat down and started digging. Ser Aldric could not be in two valleys. By harvest his side had eaten its horses, by the first frost it had walked home, and in the song he is still standing on Harrow Field, unbeaten, shining like a forge, asking where everybody went.

The eleven documents in this cycle are the explanation of that song. The Divisions of the Guild Accord wrote them in the voice of people who have counted the wagons, and I, who keep these shelves, have left that voice alone and added windows only where a reader would otherwise suffocate. War is bodies, money and names. The minstrels look after the names.

A practitioner converts Essence into effect. An army converts time into ground.

Every document in this cycle is an elaboration of that distinction, and every failure in the record is a commander who did not believe it.

What the Cycle Argues

Read in order, the eleven documents make one case and it is not a flattering one.

An army cannot be beaten by a practitioner and a practitioner cannot be beaten by an army, so wars are fought over ground, and ground is held by ordinary men in numbers. Those men are chained to fodder arithmetic, and the practitioners among them are chained to geology, which means campaign routes run vein to vein and half the sieges in the record are of places nobody valued.

Most of a campaign is therefore spent sitting outside somewhere, digging, waiting, and dying of camp fever. And the reliable instrument for actually breaking an enemy is neither the battle nor the siege. It is riding through his country and burning the means by which he pays for an army.

Which puts the Accord in a specific position. It is incapable by its own founding oaths of a war of conquest: the Law of Reversal sees to that (if the Concord turns aggressor, the Lattice, the web of sworn resonance that binds the Accord, strips its whole army of power). It is also the institution whose own after-action record for stormed towns contains a consistent discrepancy between the Scribe’s account (every battle must by law be recorded by a Concord Scribe or Seer) and the returns of the surviving population.

Nine reviews. No prosecutions. The explanation is evidentiary and is technically correct.

The Missing Fifth

Sack and Contribution is the sixth of the cycle. The Practitioner Problem is the first, the Order of Battle the second, the Long March the third, and Walls the fourth.

The fifth is The Camp, cited by name in the Long March as the document describing what happens to an army’s water and to the men who drink it. It is not held in this archive.

Its absence is load-bearing. Every document in the cycle points at camp disease as the largest killer in warfare, the Order of Battle records more Legion men lost to it than to every enemy in the field, and Walls records besieging camps losing more to it than the garrison loses to everything.

The cycle’s largest cause of death is the one volume nobody has.
