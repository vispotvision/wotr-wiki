How Magic Works

How Magic Works

Nov 28, 202650 min read

In the river towns, how magic works means Dunny Thrupp and the Bridge at Aske. Dunny ran the Aske ferry, owed fourteen silver to three different people, and had one idea in the whole of his soul, which was that a knot he tied stayed tied. It came from the flood year, when one hitch had held him and his brother to a drowned alder for two nights. At haymaking he broke the nose of a lord’s nephew over a fare, and the lord put a bounty on him, and Sir Ansgar Rook came down the Aske road in gold-washed mail to collect it, a full Stage over the ferryman, his aura burning lime-kiln white, with three hundred people running behind him to watch. The bookies on the bridge had Dunny at sixty to one. Dunny stood in his boat, wet to the thighs and red in the face, threw a loop of tarred mooring line over the knight’s shoulders from thirty feet, with the other end made fast to the bridge’s middle pier, and bawled, at a volume that put up every heron on the river:

"All the World's Rope!"

The crowd howled. A fisherman fell off the rail laughing. Sir Ansgar laughed too, and charged, with the whole of his strength behind him, and the line came up tight.

It did not break. It went on not breaking, while a man who could put his shoulder through a temple door leaned on it with everything he had, and the rope sang a note that loosened teeth on both banks, and then the middle span of the Bridge at Aske came away with a groan like a ship going down and followed him up the road, oak, bookies and all. Sir Ansgar was dug out alive by evening. Dunny’s palms had burned to the tendon where the line ran through them, and he could not close a hand until the harvest. The song says he tied up the moon. The lord billed him for the bridge.

That night Dunny’s soul reported to him, in river talk and for his eyes alone, that he had climbed further in one afternoon than in eleven years of ferrying. He told the whole of the Aske Arms what it said, which nobody there could check, and they bought him drinks anyway.

[Level: 61 to 68]
[New move: All the World's Rope]
[Skill: Knots, Journeyman to Master]
[Hit: both palms burned to the tendon. Vitality 81%]

Every part of that afternoon has a name: where Dunny got the power, what law held the knot, what the shout bought him, what that block of lines was, why his hands paid for it, and why a man a full Stage up could lose to a man with a good read and a better rope. This page gives you those names, in order, and I have tried to keep it shorter than the Accord’s assessment of that rope, which runs to nine leaves and leaves out the fisherman.

A first page on magic. Each section ends with the page that goes deeper.

The Soul Crystal, and who wakes

The Soul Crystal is the soul, and the scholars mean that literally: what your grandmother called your soul has three layers, holds power and sends it out, and can be measured. Nearly everyone is born with one. In most people it sleeps for life. About one in ten wake it, nearly always as children, taught by a school, a house or a guild, or stirring early on their own. They are practitioners (people whose Crystal is awake and working), and one in ten is a great many people: you will pass them at every market, and any child can recite the sect ranks and tell you which school’s colors to avoid on a feast day. The rest are the unwoken (their Shell sealed, with no Mastery to read on any road) and touch magic only through what they buy. A sealed Crystal can be woken, but rarely, and at great cost: by a blessing, or by a Waking Draft, which is very dear and often kills or warps the child who drinks it, and the rich buy it for their children anyway. Windows

Since the soul can be measured, it reports. A window is the soul reporting its numbers to its owner, in the owner’s own tongue, and nobody else can see it. A Moto reads hers in Moto and a Kharven drover reads his in the language he swears in. It comes when called and it comes unasked, in the middle of a fight, the moment there is something to report. Who built it is the oldest quarrel in the Measurewrights’ hall (the Measurewrights being the guild that keeps the world’s tables and argues about them), and I have seen a Grandmaster throw soup over it.

Here is what Wen Talley, a tavern brawler you will meet again below, sees when he takes a normal look at himself:

[Wen Talley · Human · Level 47 · Stage III, Ascension · Path: Body]
[Vitality 290 · Ardency 560 · Gnosis 150 · Dexterity 170]
[Harmonics 120 · Resilience 140 · Tempering 130 · Dominion 110]
[Reserve: 100% ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓]
[Trait: Hold the Rafter]
[Skills: Brawling, Apprentice · Smithing, Journeyman · Drinking, Master]
[Blessing: none]

The top line carries his race, which shows because race and lineage shape what a person starts with and what comes easily, and his Path (the layer of the Crystal his growth has actually come to lean on, which nobody chooses). Below it are the Eight Primaries (his eight main stats), each the total of the eight Sub-Stats under it, which is why a brawler’s Gnosis reads poor while the one number for his right hand reads a good deal higher. Nobody’s window gives them a letter: letters are for gear and beasts. The reserve is a percentage and a bar. Traits carry no level, and skills carry one of the nine ranks, Initiate up to Paragon, all of which this page explains. A Sub-Stat (one of the sixty-four single abilities beneath the Primaries) appears when it is used or when it moves, and the full set is there to study for anyone with a free evening.

A window shows numbers, ranks, percentages, bars and Level. It has never once shown the raw figures the Accord’s instruments read, which is why assay houses still have customers, and it reports nothing about anyone else unless the owner’s perception is strong enough to read them. In a fight it comes in short blocks after the blow, for damage, skill gains and reserve, and every window in this Archive is a copy dictated afterward by its owner, so allow for vanity. Aether and Essence

Aether, which foreigners call mana, is everywhere, thick in some places and thin in others, the way air is. It is what remains of the undivided world from before the planes were raised, which is why it can carry any idea, and only a body can pull it. Every practitioner pulls it in from the environment around them, and nobody on any road, in any art or any song worth believing, has worked a grain of it they did not first pull inside. What happens to it next depends on the road (the three Paths, below), because the three roads spend three different things. A Body fighter packs it raw into the Shell (the Crystal’s outer skin) and holds it there unrefined as the Charge, shapes it there and drives it out through the Shell, and whatever fails to arrive burns in the Shell, which is a furnace that never goes cold, and comes off as heat. A Spirit worker refines it in the Soul Crystal, where it takes on the stamp of whoever refined it, and from then on it is Essence, or spiritual energy, spent through the Core, and whatever fails to arrive is paid as a hollowing in the Core and warms nobody. A Fate worker stores no Thread at all. Their reserve is Essence, refined like a Spirit worker’s, and they pull Thread down from Fate for the one working and pay for it out of that reserve, the soul’s own share spent through the Layer, and whatever fails to arrive stays behind as a knot. Fighters call the three styles Aether Manipulation, Essence Manipulation and Thread Manipulation, and for once the common names are exactly right.

Essence is the soul’s own substance, and Charge is the world’s. The Essence Core, the Crystal’s inner layer, is the center of all identity, and the Crystal is the soul, so what a Spirit worker holds is themselves, and spending it is spending yourself, which is why such a person running on empty stops being entirely the person you knew. A Body fighter spending Charge is spending the world, and what she pays is the body’s bill for handling it: heat, torn fiber and recoil. Either way the holding is the reserve (the one store a soul carries, written as a single figure whichever of the two fills it), and every working spends reserve. A Fate worker’s is Essence, and a pull, a vow and a Domain are all paid out of it.

So where you stand matters. Rich ground fills you faster and lets you put out more; thin ground starves you, and ground that has been salted or bled starves you worse. Scholars call the richness Aetheric Density. Street fighters say fat ground and lean ground, and a clever one will walk a quarrel three fields over before she lets it start. Density fills everybody. Two other kinds of ground favor two of the roads: Echo Depth, in graveyards, temples and old homes, makes a Spirit working cheaper, and Thread Weight, on battlefields and at crossroads, does the same for a Fate pull, each by a tenth to the band, the way Density does for a blow. Rich Aetheric ground has one drawback, which is that it crowds a Fate fighter’s Range and the reach of a Domain, and heavy Thread Weight stretches them. Three layers, three Paths

A soul meets the world through three layers, and every soul has all three, the unwoken included. The Aether Shell meets the Physical World: the blow, the block, the weight, the heat. The Essence Core sits on the Soul Plane and holds identity, memory and Traits. The Attraction Layer sits on the Plane of Fate and is a soul’s own share of fate: its bonds, its oaths, its claims and its odds.

Only the first two of those are places in any careful sense. The Epochs, the three sovereign powers at the top of everything, stand over the three layers: Aegor’s Physical Reality and Ashura’s Spiritual Reality are the two lower realities, where things are. Fate, under Bara, is not a reality at all. It is everything that flows, every possibility, idea and concept, anything that can happen in those two, and it sits above them as the law and construction of the world. “Plane of Fate” is a courtesy.

A Path is the layer a Crystal has learned to lean on, and there are three. The Body Path is the literal understanding and manipulation of physical reality, worked in raw Aether through the Shell: the full force bonus at contact, and low down it has to close. High up it commands matter by recognition and then by law, and its reach runs as far as there is matter to read, though it still needs existing substance and pays in heat and recoil. The Spirit Path is everything to do with spiritual reality, souls, spirits, the dead and what a thing means, worked in Essence through the Core. Its force at range is force on souls: a soul knocked out of true, dread laid on a mind, a Core cut open, none of which a breastplate covers. It has no purchase on a stone. The Shell is the only door into matter, so a Spirit worker who wants a wall moved moves it through their own Shell like anyone else, at whatever their Body Mastery will bear. What such a strike leaves is a soul wound, which sits on the window like any other wound until a mender is paid to close it. The Fate Path is the hardest road there is. Its everyday weapons are Range, Aura Pressure, presence and bonds. On the rare day its walker can pull a thread down, they move what a result depends on, a guard slips or a column arrives late, and the result turns up without having been forced, at a ruinous price and with a knot left owing, which the world collects later. Its deadliest end is the true Fate Arts. Nobody picks one. The Crystal watches how you live and recognizes it, some years after it stopped being reversible. None of the three is the better road. Each has plain limits, each has a way of beating each of the others, and that is why some people pay to walk two.

A Path stands on a sheet as Mastered (its gates are no obstacle at the person’s Stage), Walked (three parts in ten of their practice or more) or Studied (knowledge and small uses, which anyone may have of any Path, and which open no gates). One Path masters fastest. Two take about two and a half times as long, at three-quarters of the force bonus on each for life, with holes in the fighter at times and an opponent who cannot read them. Three take about six times as long, which is longer than a mortal life, and the world looks down on anyone trying. Read next: The Three Paths, then Soul Wounds and the Menders.

Wellsprings

A Wellspring represents an alchemical and a spiritual transformation and concept: Exuroth is the trial by fire that proves what a thing is made of, Judicium the truth that will not be talked round. You can see one as a source of power to pull from, and the old books count them as sixty types of Aether, each acting on the Crystal in its own way. More truly, a Wellspring is the governing idea that transforms how a soul uses Aether, a lens set over magic that was already potent. There are sixty, fixed, and nobody has ever made a sixty-first. They come down from the Titans, the great bearers of law: a Titan is the Wellspring at full scale, and each of the Sixty is one way that Titan’s law shows up in the world, carried by Aether.

The lens shapes whatever a Path works: raw Aether in the Shell on the Body, Essence in the Core on the Spirit, the claim on the Layer on Fate. The idea stays the same and the target changes, so a blow’s character comes from Wellspring and Path together. Pyreveil on the Body is heat living in the tissue, and the blow burns on contact, and what it burns is matter. On the Spirit it is thrown, and it burns what a soul means and holds, a grief, a grudge, an attachment, and leaves the coat unscorched. On Fate it is a claim that burns its way through, and what it burns is chances, what a thing could have been. Each Wellspring also has a home plane, named by its kind, which is never a lock: Wild for the Physical World, Spirit for the Soul Plane, Fate for the Plane of Fate, and Medium for all three. At home a lens gives a little at first and after years of mastery can double the effect. Away from home it costs more and reaches half again at most: less of each spend arrives, on top of whatever the ground and their Mastery are already taking, and the lost share is paid in the road’s own coin. A Spirit Wellspring gives a fighter no fire to throw on any Path; it shows in what a person can bear, know, keep or let go.

A soul gets one at a Core in a dungeon, from a school, from a Draft or a blessing, or by birth. A Core is a site where a Wellspring’s Aether pools, the greatest concentration there is (no relation to the Essence Core inside the Crystal, a distinction the street has never once respected). Most Cores lie in dungeons, and the rest are said to lie out in vast territories. A worked Core is a dungeon somebody cleared and now holds: it recharges, it has keepers, and its keepers can close it to you.

What a lens does, it does by its idea. A practitioner harmonizes with one (tunes the Crystal to it), and from then on works a transformation on themselves: the idea reshapes what they spend and, slowly, who they are, and the world gives way as far as that soul is truly changed. The ordinary world keeps the habits it settled in the Physical Epochs, so stones still fall and fire still eats wood; magic never overturns them, it only argues with them, and a weak soul argues with a pebble while a great one argues with a mountain range. Every idea has its own operation at the bench, its own price and its own flaw, the thing it cannot hold, and a working fails exactly where that flaw is. What burns in the crucible burns in the one who tends it.

The Measurewrights, the guild that measures for a living, have a second account, and it is physics. In their books each Wellspring runs on one law of natural philosophy, heat or motion or growth, and their tables tell you exactly what a lens buys. Every number in those tables stands: the guild is often right about how much and never about why, and the confessors and the benches hold the truer reading. A Measurewright will defend the account to the death all the same, and tell you what is wrong with yours. Read next: The Sixty Wellsprings.

Traits

A Trait (a standing law in the soul) forms through lived experience: something a person survives hardens into a rule the world accepts as true. Nobody can teach you one. Sleeping souls carry Traits too, as a knack: the fisherman the cold cannot touch. Waking arms them. The Primary Trait (the first and deepest, carried from birth and shaped by first imprint and the life lived) sets the direction for everything that follows.

A Trait carries no level of its own. It works as deep as its owner’s Mastery of the lead road (below Glory, where Mastery is not yet read, it works at its base), so it grows as the life does, and a Release and a Domain are the same Trait escalating. The mark a Trait leaves on the body follows that road too. The old books gave Traits levels of their own, and some old windows still printed one. Skills, techniques and the kit

Every practitioner has skills: swordwork, tracking, haggling, brewing Drafts, cutting runes, chanting, and anything else a person does until they get better at it. A skill rises with use and shows on the sheet as one of the nine ranks, the same words a Stage’s rank uses: Initiate, Apprentice, Journeyman, Adept, Expert, Master, Grandmaster, Archmaster, Paragon. A trade can climb all the way to Paragon with no Stage behind it, the way a baker of fifty years can, while a fighting skill never ranks above its owner’s own Stage rank: a Journeyman’s sword stops at Journeyman, an Expert’s at Expert, and only a Paragon’s reaches Paragon. Anyone can level the ones hands alone can do, baking and pouring Drafts included, while cutting runes, firing tags, chanting and speaking a working want a woken Crystal.

A technique (a named, repeatable working with a stated cost) is a named thing done with a skill and a Trait. Taught techniques come from a school; derived techniques are found alone; a signature is one person’s own art. A technique shows no level on the sheet. It jumps when its skill gains a rank and a fight pushes it past its old limit, gaining power, reach, speed or a lower cost; when it jumps, it takes a bigger name, and the old name stays in use for the smaller move. Fighters shout a technique’s name as it fires: the shout costs a beat (the moment it takes, in which an opponent can act) and buys a surge of power.

A fighter’s whole kit is one wild concept you can explain in a breath, knots or thunder or debt, with a growing list of loud named moves under it. They call those names in their own tongue, and anyone may reach for a word like World, God or Infinite. The room laughs until it works, and living up to the name becomes the rest of that person’s life. Heralds cry the winners, minstrels get the details wrong, lords post the bounties and bookies take the odds, and all of it changes what you pay at an inn and who comes looking for you.

Every working (a deliberate act of magic) is written in Parun (the glyph grammar all magic uses) and set into something. Spoken, it happens now and the body pays. Cut into stone, steel or paper, it outlives its maker. Poured into a bottle, anyone can drink it, woken or not. Sung, it takes many voices and holds while they sing. These are ways of doing the work and the trades that grew up around them, open to anyone who will put in the years, though all but the pouring want a woken Crystal. The cutters’ guilds still rank themselves highest and call speakers mouths, the pourers have words for the cutters, and the crowds cheer the fighters anyway. Most of the magic you will meet this week is small, and somebody is doing it with their hands for money. The hedge-worker at the fair will charm your ladder straight for two coppers. A ward-cutter will cut your doorstep against rats, and charge double if she dislikes your dog. Guild craftsmen shout their marks across the workshop and swear the contraption has never once gone off by itself. The lamp-wardens keep a street lit at a penny a lamp a night, and when they struck over the penny last winter all of Fish Row went to bed at sundown, with results the midwives are still remarking on. All of them can overcharge, and all of them can say no. Read next: Who Pays for the Lamp: The Living Crafts.

One working, start to finish

Wen Talley’s Primary Trait is heat, carried from birth and first shown when he was a forge-boy who held a burning rafter off his brother and lived. From it he learned a technique, Hot Iron, which drives that law into whatever his hand strikes. In a tavern brawl he shouts it, “Hot Iron!”, spoken and paid for in the body. Wen is a Body fighter, so what answers the shout is the Charge in his Shell, raw Aether that was never refined, taking the shape of his heat and going out through the knuckles. His fist glows the smoky, clouded orange of a low-Stage aura; the man’s jaw blisters where it lands, and the taproom smells of singed beard. The punch costs a sliver of his reserve, a few seconds of everything he has, and the heat runs back up his own arm, so after four or five he cannot close the hand. His soul has the report ready before the man hits the floor:

[Reserve: 96% ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓░]
[Hit: right hand scorched by its own heat. Vitality 96%]
[Skill: Brawling, Apprentice to Journeyman]

That is magic: a law from the soul, a learned shape, a skill that carries it, a price in reserve, and a limit the body feels.

What it all costs

Reserve spent is gone until it is pulled back, by rest, from the ground, at a Core, or out of a fight Draft. A registered card (the record an assay house or guild keeps on a practitioner) writes a working’s drain as how long it could run from a full reserve. As the reserve falls, the body shows it: at half, the breath shortens and the aura dims; at a quarter, a tremor and a thinning color; below a tenth comes Starvation, when the aura flickers and the Trait stutters. Each road adds signs of its own on top of those, so you can read a person’s Path by watching them flag. A Body fighter runs hot: the heat-haze thickens on the skin at half, steam comes off the shoulders with cramps at a quarter, and below a tenth the Shell starts drawing its Charge out of the flesh and the skin splits in fine lines. A Spirit worker runs hollow: the colors near them go flat, then comes a cold and a slipped word, and below a tenth, since what is running out is the self, the Core begins to fray: a name will not come, then a year goes missing. A Fate worker runs unlucky: small luck curdles nearby, then things start going wrong around them, and at the last their own name sits wrong in other mouths. Their reserve is Essence, so under the bad luck the Core frays as a Spirit worker’s does. The halls call the first Charge Starvation and the other two Essence Starvation. Pushing past the Crystal’s safe ceiling cracks it, and cracks that build up become permanent. Beyond reserve there is money, the oath sworn at the gate, the knot that every pull on a thread leaves owing, and, in a Draft bought from the wrong source, someone else’s convictions mixed into your own. A big art also charges a price out of its own idea, which is how a man whose whole law is rope comes to lose the skin of his hands to it. Stages, Levels and the numbers

The whole climb goes in one sentence, and the Measurewrights have spent three centuries failing to improve on it: your soul climbs Stages, and each Stage asks one thing of you; your life climbs Level, by everything hard and real you have done; your hands climb skills, by use. Everything else is read from those three.

A Stage (one of sixteen steps of the soul’s refinement, from Murmuring to Apex) is a full remaking of the Crystal, and everyone says it as a rank: Stage I is an Initiate, II an Apprentice, III a Journeyman, IV an Adept, V to VII an Expert, VIII to X a Master, XI and XII a Grandmaster, XIII and XIV an Archmaster, XV and XVI a Paragon. “A Journeyman, Ascension, close to Flourishing” is how a careful hall says it; the street says “a Journeyman” and means the same. Tempering melts the Crystal down and rebuilds each layer in its own coin, the layer the soul leans on first and hardest: the Shell comes out able to pack and hold more raw Charge, the Core’s Essence comes out more finely refined, and the Layer’s claim comes out firmer. The body gains Traits, and every stat’s ceiling rises with it. The stone’s look follows the Stage: Clouded to Adept, Faceted through the Experts, Fire through the Masters, Flawless for the Grandmasters, Starred for the Archmasters, Absolute at the top.

Level is the life. It moves by anything hard and real a person does: danger overcome above all, and killing something stronger moves it more than a hundred weak kills, so crews argue over who got the kill the way other people argue over a will; but a flood night held, a plague nursed through or fifty years at a trade move it too, and a baker can stand at Level 140 without once having been in danger. A Stage opens when two things are true: its Catalyst (the one deed that Stage asks, met in the lead road’s terms) has been met, and the soul’s Level can hold it. Stage II holds at Level 5, III at 15, IV at 30, V at 101, VIII at 201, and so on up to 500 at Revelation, the Stages of one rank group sitting close to its floor. Meet the Catalyst early, or buy it in a Draft, and the Crystal is Overgrown: power spills, lamps lean toward the soul, and no Release will come until Level catches up. Grinding to the top of a rank group is ordinary, but a soul that runs past it, over Level 100 below Expert, over 200 below Master, and so on up, with no Catalyst met, builds pressure instead, as Residual Strain, which shows in the room and can crack the Crystal; it is also how a high-Level commoner sometimes wakes late, when the pressure finally breaks the seal.

Each practitioner has eight main stats (Vitality for the body’s endurance, Ardency for raw output, Gnosis for perception, Dexterity for speed, Harmonics for pulling power in, Resilience for withstanding it, Tempering for growth, Dominion for presence and authority), each the total of the eight Sub-Stats under it. A contest compares the numbers of the stat in use, under the gap in Stages: a stat a full step ahead on the Measurewrights’ scale wins that exchange outright, two steps ahead overwhelms, three leaves the weaker side only trying to survive, and between near numbers skill, ground and the read decide. The scale has letters, F up to EX+, and the halls use them for things: a blade, a missile, a beast, a bounty. Nobody calls a person by a letter; a Measurewright who wants to be rude says a man “hits like a Grade A beast” and lets him work it out. Your life spends most of your stat points by what you do with it, which mostly means what your lead layer does, and a few are yours to place at each Stage, in a grant that leans toward your road: Ardency, Vitality, Dexterity and Tempering for the Body, Gnosis and Harmonics for the Spirit, Dominion and Resilience for Fate. Mastery and the class

From Glory, Stage VI, an assay can read a soul’s Mastery: how well it works its own road’s substance, one rung for each Path, I to V, and Ω for the Paragons. A window writes it short, Body IV · Spirit II. Nobody trains it as a thing apart. The Stage sets its ceiling (I at Glory, II at Refraction, III at Transcendence and Invocation, IV at Realization and Dissonance, V from Emanation to Zenith, Ω only at the top) and Level places the soul under that ceiling, so it is read off the life, and a bought Stage raises the ceiling and leaves the soul where its life put it. Mastery decides yield (η), how much of what a person spends arrives as effect, as a distillation has its yield, and how much is lost: on the Body it burns in the Shell’s furnace and roars off as heat and noise, so a fighter at Body I loses about a third of every spend and you will hear her coming. Each road’s people kept their own old words for the rungs, and you will still hear them: on the Body a Muridic, Harmonic, Resonant, Luminous or Radiant fighter; on the Spirit a Listener, Answerer, Confessor, Shepherd or Ferryman; on Fate one Touched, Drawn, a Spinner, a Knotwright or a Weaver. The old books counted them as three separate ladders, the Aether, Essence and Thread Classes, and they are one table now. Any rung can be held Inverted, a feared and wrong way of working it that the old books counted as a sixth rung of its own. Fate climbs with Level like the others, but without a grip, which is a Work of a bloodline or a blessing, it stops at II, and most Fate souls live there; and the great bloodlines’ own instruments open their road early, reading rung I from Stage III, the one exception to the rule that nothing is read below Glory; its price is not lost yield but the knot every pull leaves owing. Which road’s Mastery reads a working depends on what it lands on: matter reads Body, a soul or a meaning reads Spirit, a condition reads Fate.

At Glory, with Mastery I, the soul also offers a named class, one that fits how the person has lived. The offer comes through the lead layer, so it tastes of the road, with surprises allowed. Ten years of holding a gate may bring Doorward; a life of running a crew’s books under fire may bring Quartermaster. A class is a name, never a number. It evolves each time the lead Mastery rises, each evolution bringing one move that fits it and a lean on where life spends the stat points, into forms some share with thousands and some hold alone. The one wild concept stays the person’s own: the class is what the soul made of your life, and the concept is what you made of the class. Read next: Classes and Mastery.

How power grows

Practitioners train by pulling at Wellspring sites or on rich ground, by sparring and real fights, and by seclusion before a Threshold (the moment of remaking). The old roads to a Catalyst are crisis in a fight, seclusion, and tribulation, when a Wellspring’s Aether tests the practitioner directly. Each Stage makes one demand, and it is met three ways, in the terms of the layer a soul leans on: in the flesh and in matter for the Body, in a soul or a meaning for the Spirit, in a bond, an oath or the odds for Fate. A soul on two roads meets its lead road’s. Seclusion is kept on the road’s own ground, Aetheric Density for the Body, Echo Depth for the Spirit and Thread Weight for Fate. A breakthrough is a visible transformation, heat and shaken ground on the Body, a valley gone quiet on the Spirit, luck turning for miles on Fate; a failed one leaves slag (a remaking that set wrong). A lived one passes the Great Work in its own road’s terms, the four stages the benches name nigredo, albedo, citrinitas and rubedo: the soul broken down, purified, woken and fixed.

There is also a road with a price list. Drafts and blessings can supply a Catalyst, and Stages can simply be bought, so the wealthy are stronger because they are wealthy, and the great houses hoard the top Drafts to keep it that way. A breakthrough Draft is poured by road: on a Salt base for a Body soul, on Core stock for a Spirit one, and on the Layer reagent for a Fate one, which is the dearest of the three and unlawful everywhere. A purse can buy the Catalyst for any Stage, though at Zenith a Hollow Throne must still recognize the buyer, so up there money buys the attempt and not the seat. The poor rise by dungeon luck, guild patronage and blessings, and a commoner who has come up holding a monster core is the thing a great house fears most.

A bought Stage opens the door and no more. Level still has to be lived, so the buyer stands Overgrown until it is, and a Release (below) must still be found and mastered by living its own Catalyst, and sects teach what money cannot buy: releases, Domains, techniques, skills and the rites for their Wellspring. A rich heir at Stage IX may have no Release at all while a poor Body fighter at the same Stage has all three, and the brackets sell a great many tickets on that difference. Most of this happens in schools, which run like football clubs, with colors, songs, grudges going back four generations and a fighting style you can spot at fifty paces, and you should think carefully before wearing the wrong sash into the wrong tavern. Read next: Training and Sects, then The Ladder and the Draft.

Monsters, cores and dungeons

Monsters live and move across the whole of the Inner World, and plain beasts range more widely still. The Scourge of Hell, a wasteland in the middle of the continent, is the one area known for extremely dangerous ones, and a bad spring anywhere will put something on your road. A monster gains Levels by killing, its own kind included, and changes form at its own thresholds. It sits on the same Stage and Level scale as you do, and unlike you it carries a Grade, and a strong enough perception can read it, while a weak one gets question marks:

[Read: Marsh Drake · Grade B · Level ??? · Flux ??]

Monsters are known and bountied by Grade, E up to EX, the same letters the Accord’s Commissions (its hunting contracts) carry, and the Accord sets which title may take which Grade of bounty; a famous one earns a name, an epithet and a bounty. Underneath the letter a beast has a Stage, a Level and a lean on one Path like anyone, so it leans on a room with Pressure exactly as a practitioner of that height would, and it has Mastery on its road. A famous one can show a Release. None takes a class. Its hide, bone and scale make gear. Its core is what the crew came for. Stone and parts are read from the beast’s own Stage and Level on the coil, and the Grade stays the shorthand for danger and price, since most beasts line up with it. Old hunters still say T4 for a Grade B drake; the nine Threat Tiers were the Accord’s reckoning before the letters.

A monster’s core is in a way its Soul Crystal, worth more the stronger the monster. An alchemist renders it into Essence Drafts used for tempering, at the risk of Identity Contamination (the beast’s appetites coming along with its power). Monsters walk the roads too. How a monster lives sets its Path, and the kind of its Wellspring only leans it, so a thing that kills by weight and hide is a Body monster wherever it was spawned. The road decides what the core renders to. Every stone is mostly Core. A Body monster’s, the common kind, has its packed Charge set into it and parts out heaviest in Salt ash, which the bench pours as Salts to pack a Shell; the rarer Spirit monsters give the richest Core stock; and a Fate monster, rarest and most feared, is the only one whose stone grows a skin of Layer, shaved off as a reagent that changes hands only on the black market. Swallowed raw, it gives everything at once, and most who try it die or come out wrong, and people keep trying it because the alternative is being poor. A Crystal cut from a dead practitioner makes the strongest Draft of all, which is the worst crime in every realm and the most profitable, and the reason the alchemists’ top Grade of stock (Index Grade IV, no relation to Body IV) has never balanced its books.

Most cores of the other kind, the Wellspring Cores, lie at the bottom of dungeons. A dungeon grows down in floors around its Core, more dangerous as you go, with something at the heart guarding it. Kill that and the place is cleared; leave it and it grows. It is alive and learning: it moves its galleries, breeds what kills crews best and pays attention to who keeps coming back, and scholars argue whether it thinks. Read next: Dungeons, then The Bestiary.

Reinforcement

A practitioner’s stats live in their body as a passive base. Reinforcement (power from the reserve driven through the body to push it further) adds an active layer for a cost in reserve. It shows as a coat of their own aura, thickest where they push. Only a coat worn through the Shell hardens a body, at the wearer’s Body Mastery. A Spirit worker’s coat wards the soul, and a Fate worker’s is the claim worn as presence, Pressure and a room that settles around them. Gathering it into one fist leaves the rest of the body less protected. Asleep, drugged or taken by surprise, they keep the base and lose the coat.

The coat is the most famous art in the world, and nobody can agree what to call it. Common soldiers say wearing your color. Kharven riders call it the hide. The Moto say matoi (the wearing) and hold that a coat anyone can see is a coat badly worn; the Mahuo say gabot (armor) and hold that a coat nobody can see has never been hit. Read next: Reinforcement.

Drafts, runes and tags

A Draft (a working poured into a substance and bottled) is how most of the world touches magic. Advancement Drafts bring a soul up to a Stage’s minimum and help it set cleanly, and the best of them supply the Catalyst as well. Essence Drafts, most of them rendered from monster cores, feed the tempering itself. Fight Drafts are drunk mid-fight: each costs a beat, refills reserve and leaves a crash, and each follows the road of whoever drinks it. Core stock feeds a Spirit worker’s Core with Essence that nobody has stamped yet, and the crash is the Crystal stamping it afterward, all at once. A Body fighter’s fight Draft is a Salt, raw Aether in a fraction that packs straight into the Shell: it goes down like swallowing a burn and leaves a crash of cramps and fever. There is no fight Draft for Fate: a Fate worker’s reserve is Essence, and Core stock refills it. Each Draft is graded by the Stage it serves, the price climbs about threefold a Stage, and counterfeits are common. Read next: The Ladder and the Draft.

Cheap runed gear is everywhere; the great pieces are old and cannot be made again, and people kill for the ones that came off the battlefields of the Great Spirit War. Fighters carry prepared tags (short workings cut on paper, hide or foil, set off with a touch), and a few bond a weapon over years until it takes part in their release. A Body fighter fires a tag and bonds gear with her own Charge. Runes cut into a living body are a rare and dangerous art. Read next: Runes and Tags.

Gear: Grade and Rarity

An item carries two ratings. Grade says how much power it has, on the same scale the Measurewrights keep under a fighter’s numbers, though only things wear the letter. Rarity says how scarce it is: Common, Uncommon, Rare, Very Rare, Legendary, Mythic, Unique. The old words you will still see punched into metal (Marked, Proofed, Tempered, Instrument, Hallowed, Storied, Found, Numinous, Proscribed) survive as the Accord’s stamps. An Appraisal skill and an assay instrument read an item, and a good forger beats a weak appraiser, which has paid for several very fine houses. Monster parts make the best gear going. A bonded piece grows with its owner, and everyone else’s stays what it is.

Plain gear does its own work and no more. Worked gear (runed, Draft-tempered or made of monster parts) also multiplies its wearer, one whole Primary at a time, and the window shows it: [Worn: Ashjaw Helm · Rare · Grade B], [Gnosis ×1.15], [Shell draw: light]. The draw is what it takes out of the reserve to stay lit, and it goes by the piece: armor and weapons draw on the Shell, soul-wards and charms on the Core, oath-rings and luck-tokens on the Layer, all from the one reserve. The trades are real (heavy plate may read Vitality ×1.3 and Dexterity ×0.85), all of a person’s gear together lifts any one Primary by half at most, and a piece above its wearer’s Stage works at half strength or fights them. The other roads have gear of their own: soul-wards, charms and reliquaries for the Spirit, oath-rings, luck-tokens and knot-cutters for Fate.

A Master Work does what its Grade and material should not be able to. It is made once, at the end of a lifetime, always Unique or Mythic, and named, and each area of the world and each trade has its own. A Draft can be one: a Fate Essence Draft is extremely hard to make, and there are people who would burn a city for a single dose. Read next: Grade and Rarity.

The Guild Accord and its nine titles

The Guild Accord is a coalition of many kinds of guild across many nations: adventurers, merchants, craftsmen, alchemists, healers, couriers and more. Halls sign on for shared rank, enforceable contracts and passage, and the crowns tolerate it and fear it. Its nine titles are the same in every guild: Initiate, Apprentice, Journeyman, Adept, Expert, Master, Grandmaster, Archmaster, Paragon. A title grades trust in that trade. A merchant may deal up to it, and it caps the Rarity he may sell. A craftsman may make up to it, and it caps the Grade she may forge. An adventurer may take Commissions up to the Grade the Accord allows that title. Titles are earned by commissions, and fighting guilds ask besides that the holder stand at the title’s own rank in Stage, so a Journeyman’s token in a fighting hall wants Stage III; trade titles ask no Stage at all, and a Grandmaster merchant may not be able to throw a punch. The words are the same nine a fighter’s rank uses, and people mix the two up the way people do: Master Venn at the forge may be an Apprentice on the road. The title travels on a silver token, and forging one is a great crime, which tells you how many forgers there are. Read next: The Standing and the Title, then The Guild Accord.

Auras and Pressure

Every woken aura shows its own hue from the first Stage, clouded and smoky low down and clearing as the Stage rises. The Wellspring a person has tempered on longest sets that hue (Pyreveil ember-orange, Judicium arc-blue, and every other of the Sixty its own), and it brightens as they push. Any other Wellsprings add texture and sound, and a class or a big Trait adds a shape. Where the aura sits is the road’s: on the skin like heat-haze for a Body fighter, a half-step off the body for a Spirit worker, with the shadows falling wrong around it, and hardly anywhere for a Fate worker, around whom dust lies still and dropped coins land face up. Pressure (the weight a stronger soul puts on a room) is felt first in the room, then in bodies: one Stage up bends attention, two brings sweat and nausea, three drops people where they stand. Only then does the window report it, as a line telling you what it has taken from you. A Grandmaster merchant with no fighting Stage has no Pressure at all, whatever his token says.

Pressure and sensing (reading another soul’s strength without using your eyes) are shared arts as famous as the coat, and the arguments about them have ended friendships and at least one marriage. A tavern in the south says a man is leaning on the room; Kharven call it the low sky; the Moto call it atsu, the press; the lineage halls say wēiyā, awe pressing down, and teach it as manners. For sensing, Kharven ask if you can smell a man’s stack, the Moto speak of kehai, the sign of someone there, and the Mahuo say nunchi, which their grandmothers also use for knowing when to leave the table. The law under the argument is short. A stronger Gnosis Perception reads a weaker Resilience Ward, and your window prints what it found. Everything else is an estimate said aloud, and people can hide it or fake it. Read next: The Color of Essence.

Releases and Domains

A release (a practitioner’s Trait let off its safety limit on purpose, in a shape their Crystal has learned to hold) is a full transformation: the body changes, the aura flares, a sound comes, and power rises. How many a soul may find is set by its lead Path. A Body soul has three, the First from Stage IV, the Second from VII and the Third from IX. A Spirit soul has two, from IV and VII, each deeper. A Fate soul has one, the One, which opens at Refraction, Stage VII, deeper than any First, and counts as its Second. A second road walked beside the lead flavors a soul’s Releases and brings none of its own. Each Release has a Catalyst of its own, a deed in the road’s terms (a Body fighter’s First asks for the body broken and refusing to fall; a Spirit worker’s, the self said aloud, and true), and none comes to an Overgrown soul; How Power Grows gives all six. Each must be found and mastered by living it, whatever the Stage cost. A fighter may call one aloud for the full rise or go silent for a weaker one. Every release runs on a clock and ends on its own when the reserve runs low. When it is called the window fills, and a timer bar starts to drain:

[FIRST RELEASE: KILN SKIN]
[Blows carry forge heat. Heat workings strike at the top of their reach.]
[Timer ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓]

What a release acts on follows the road: a Release transforms the flesh and the blow on the Body, the line and its reach on the Spirit, the claim and the Pressure on Fate. Far up every road waits a Realm, reached late and by each road’s own door, the Body’s through its Third, the Spirit’s through its Second grown whole, Fate’s through the One; the exact doors are still in study. All of a fighter’s own boosts together stack to two steps on the Measurewrights’ scale over the sustainable figure on their card, and only outside help goes past that. A Path’s force bonus and a Wellspring’s lens work inside that figure and never count. Read next: Release Forms, then Releases and Domains by Path.

A Domain (a space where a practitioner’s inner law outranks the world’s) first shows as a seed: at the Threshold into Stage VII a person’s own Trait may flicker out into one, once, with no release behind it. A Second Release, or a Fate soul’s One, later lets a seed be called and held, and from Stage VIII it stands as a true Domain, grown by living and called aloud, forming as it is named. Anyone at Stage VIII can have one, their own concept made real, and fame, rivalries and bounties follow it, though a person who bought the Stage must go and find that release first. The Stage grants a Domain and no number ever does. Trait, release and Domain are one idea escalating: the idea, then the body transformed by it, then the world obeying it. Every Domain is a claim made by the Attraction Layer, so it needs no grip on any thread, and what it rules follows the road: on the Body it rules matter, and everyone can see it; on the Spirit it rules meanings, and the room looks exactly as it did; on Fate it rules conditions. When two fields meet they clash by Stage first, a field two Stages up overwriting the lower; otherwise Dominion holds the ground, and a sharp read can flip it. Read next: Domains.

Vows

A vow (a condition a person binds on themselves, spoken aloud) is small Fate, and it is anyone’s to make, with no Stage, school or witness required. Spoken, it shows in the speaker’s window with the bargain and its terms, and its price shows on the body at once. A vow spends the soul’s own share, which is part of the reserve, so it drains like any working and refills the same way, and a pull reaches above it, which is why a fishwife who has never held a thread can swear one that holds, and why a vow kept leaves no knot behind it. The narrower the condition and the heavier the forfeit, the more it buys, and what it buys counts toward the two-step cap on self-boosts. A broken vow lands at once by its own terms, and repeated denial of the truth behind it makes a Shear Break (a Trait turning on the bearer who denied it). Munahi, the twenty-ninth of February, is the day the law sleeps and Fate does not: no instrument binds, and a vow spoken on it still holds. Read next: Vows and Oaths.

Fate and blessings

The Plane of Fate is the control of Attraction Force and Obsession Force and the workings of reality itself. Attraction Force is what makes a name, a vow or a bond take hold in the world; Obsession Force is the same current when it refuses to let go. Thread is Attraction Force pulled down for a working and released. Obsession is Thread held by force, which is what keeps a corrupted working fed, and it leaves a heavier knot. To work Fate is to manipulate probabilities: to reach outside what reality already knows and control the fate, the predestiny, of something, and all spirits and all physical things have fates to control. It can attach to oaths and is more closely tied to blessings. Real Fate Arts are rare and mostly run in bloodlines, some high-Stage masters reach them late along the Fate Path, and most people only ever touch Fate from the outside, through a blessing or a Master Work Draft. They are considered incredibly overpowered, and people who hold them tend to be invited to things.

A vow and a blessing are the small uses. People also fight and wage wars with Fate, and it is the hardest thing anyone does. It is the law and construction of the world, the Archons and the great principles they embody hold sovereignty there as they do on the planes below, and nobody can reason their way into it, because reasoning is made of the thing it would be used against. Most of what a Fate fighter does in most fights is plain: the room is heavy before they arrive, their reach is long, and everyone sworn to them fights better. When one of them does pull, you see them set, the eyes gone still and the breath held on a count that is not the count of the exchange, and then your back foot comes down on the one wet stone and the blade is already there. It is an ordinary cut and an ordinary wound, and the one who moved it is bleeding from the nose and has perhaps half of what they walked in with. It often fails. Landed or failed, it leaves a knot: the world collects on every pull, later and in kind, so that a strap of the puller’s own gives next week or a friend’s sound plan goes wrong, and serious Fate users keep a book of what they owe. Moving a whole field, weather, panic, the hour a column arrives, is a life’s masterwork at high Stage or a bloodline’s art, and it is why each side’s first job in a war is to find the other’s Fate users. It moves only what already has a way of happening, so a sound strap stays sound, the price rises with how far the result stands from likely, and it can be contested, Dominion’s Fate Sub-Stat pushing and the target’s Resilience Persistence and Continuity pushing back. Another Fate user can lean the other way, a kit or a soul sound enough gives it nothing to move, and anyone who reaches the fighter before the result arrives has an ordinary body in front of them.

A blessing grants a power out of a person’s natural reach, even in Level. It need not go through Fate, though most do. Higher spirits, Archons, Epochs and people of higher Temperance give them, because they are spiritually stronger, and a great giver spends almost nothing doing it. Lesser spirits and high-Stage people give most of the world’s blessings; Epochs and Archons act rarely, and when they do it shakes the world. A blessing shows on the sheet as its own line, and that line can lie, because the giver wrote it:

[Blessing: The Road Remembers You]

A curse is often a blessing with terms you did not read, or from a giver who does not care what you want, and nobody can tell at first, and some are both. Those who hold Hataraki, the Moto’s governing power, can take a blessing back or remove it, which is one of the reasons people are so afraid of them. It takes two Works or two Moto, since a Work is an inherited grip on one function. Read next: Blessings and Curses, then The Tell and the Knot and The Three Paths.
