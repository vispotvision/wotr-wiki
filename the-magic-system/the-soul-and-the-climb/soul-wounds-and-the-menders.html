Soul Wounds and the Menders

Soul Wounds and the Menders

Nov 17, 202617 min read

What a Spirit strike does once it is past the Shell, how the hurt shows, how long it stays, who closes it and for how much, and what the Spirit road does to its own walkers when they overspend

“He had not a mark on him. That was how we knew it was bad.”

A ringside surgeon at the Sealhall Lantern Brackets, giving evidence in the matter of the purse.

Gorm Hadrick, called the Anvil, went into the third round of the Lantern Brackets at Sealhall in a harness of blued Marrowhold plate with brass rivets the size of thumbnails, behind a Durability the slates had chalked at A, and he had not been put down in six years. Across forty paces of raked sand stood Sister Odalys Wren of the Quiet Bell, a thin Confessor in a saffron habit with ink on her cuffs, whom he outweighed by a second person. The crowd had come to see how long she could run.

She did not run. She raised two fingers, said one word in the temple tongue, and something the color of old honey crossed the sand between them faster than the eye liked. It touched nothing. The plate did not ring, the rivets did not start, the sand under his boots lay as it was raked. Gorm Hadrick took one more step, stopped, and sat down in the ring like a man who has remembered he left a pot on the fire. He was not bleeding. His aura, which had been a good loud forge orange, had gone the color of weak tea, and when his second ran out and asked him what day it was he gave the right answer and then could not say what his second’s name was, which was Tobb, and Tobb was his brother.

His window had it before the surgeons did:

[Hit: Core struck out of true. Soul wound, deep.]
[Lost: the color orange. Taste down a Grade. Tobb, gone thin.]
[Stays until mended.]

The plate was sold that winter to pay the mender. It had never been touched, and it fetched full price.

That is a soul wound, and it is the reason every hitter in the four quarters who has fought a thrower once keeps a charm on a cord and a mender’s name in his purse. The rest of this page is the law of it.

I. The Door Past the Shell

Armor answers a blow. Durability answers a blow. Both belong to the Aether Shell, the soul’s outer layer, the one that meets the Physical World, and a strike from the Spirit Path is not going to the Physical World at all.

A Spirit fighter spends Essence (spiritual energy: Aether refined in the Crystal and stamped with the person who refined it), authored in the Essence Core and sent out through the Core. What leaves her is a say about a soul, carried on her own substance, and it lands on the layer it was made in: the other person’s Core. Spirit force is force on souls. It does not dent bronze, lift a stone or scorch a coat, and a Spirit fighter who wants a wall moved has to move it through her own Shell like anyone else, at whatever her Aether Class will bear. The Shell is the only door into matter. The Core is the door into people, and a breastplate does not cover it.

What stands in the way is the target’s own soul. Integrity is how hard the Core is to cut, Anchoring is how firmly it sits in true, and Ward answers a read rather than a blow. A hitter with an A in Durability and a D in Integrity is a castle with the gate open, and throwers can tell at a glance, which is most of why they look so calm.

A Spirit strike does one of three things, and the surgeons have learned to ask which.

The strikeWhat it doesHow it reads on the day
Knocked out of trueThe soul is shoved off its seat in the body, the way a blow shifts a rider in the saddle. Nothing is cut. Will and limb stop agreeing for a moment, and a working half-made comes apart in the handA stagger with no blow behind it, a lag between meaning to move and moving, a dropped guard. Usually shallow
Dread laid on a mindA feeling is set in the Core with the striker’s stamp on it, and the mind takes it for its own. Dread is the common one, because it is the cheapest to author, and grief, shame and homesickness have all been thrown in angerThe knees go before the reason does. The victim can tell you there is nothing to fear and cannot make his legs agree. Shallow if it lifts with the fight, deep if it does not
A Core woundThe Core itself is cut: identity, memory or a Trait’s seat opened, and something leaks or is lostColor goes out of the aura and out of the world, a sense dulls, a memory thins. Deep by nature

Anything that works on souls can give one. A Spirit Path fighter is the usual source, and a spirit, a lingering dead thing or a beast of the Soul Plane will do it just as well and with less ceremony. A Body Path fighter cannot, however hard she hits, because matter has no purchase on a soul, and the hitters who claim otherwise are describing a concussion.

II. How It Shows

A soul wound is a status line, like any wound, and it stays until someone treats it. It sits on the window beside the broken collarbone and the missing ear, in the owner’s own tongue, and it says what has been lost.

[Hit: dread laid, shallow. Knees unsteady while it holds.]

[Hit: Core cut, deep.]
[Lost: green, the smell of rain. Your mother's voice, gone thin.]
[Stays until mended.]

From outside there is no blood and no bruise, and the tells are quieter than a body’s. There are three, and a good second knows them all.

  • Lost color. The victim’s aura fades toward gray at the place it was struck, and the world fades for the victim to match. It is rarely all color. It is one, or a family of them: Gorm Hadrick lost orange, which was his own hue, and for a season his forge fire looked to him like dishwater. People notice it first in their food.
  • A dulled sense. Taste, smell, the feel of cloth under the fingers, the pitch of a known voice. The organ is whole. A physician will test the ear and find it sound, and the patient will tell you music has gone flat.
  • A memory gone thin. The memory is still there, and that is the cruelty of it. The victim knows he has a brother, and knows the name if told it, and it will not stay. A thin memory has no weight: it does not come when called, it carries no feeling, and it reads to its owner like something he was told about a stranger.

The body follows on. A soul out of true makes a clumsy hand, a deep wound makes a poor sleeper, and a fighter carrying one works at a loss: a working authored in a cut Core arrives ragged, and Traits seated near the wound stutter. None of this is heat, and none of it is fever. A surgeon who bleeds a soul-struck man is treating the wrong layer and should be stopped.

The pale place. Anyone with Gnosis Analysis worth the name can read a soul wound directly, as a tear or a dislocation in the Core, and can say how deep. A healed one still reads. It leaves a pale place, a patch of Core with the color washed out of it, and it stays for life the way a scar stays. Assayers note it on a slip without being asked. Opponents with the stat to see it note it too, for their own reasons, as section III explains.

III. How Long It Lasts

DepthLeft aloneMended
Shallow (knocked out of true, a dread that lifts, a nick)Closes by itself in days, a week or two at the outside, with rest and no spendingA sitting or two, and the fighter is back in the yard
Deep (a cut Core, a dread that has set, a loss on the window)Does not close. It spreads slowly, and the thin memory gets thinnerSeasons. A course of sittings through one season at the least, often two
SetFor lifeFor life. A mender can make it bearable and cannot make it go

Two things set a wound, and both are common.

Left past a season. A deep wound untreated for a full season sets where it lies. The Core closes around the loss the way bone knits around a break nobody splinted, and what was thin is then gone: the color, the sense or the memory is no longer missing, because there is no longer a place for it. Most set wounds in the four quarters belong to people who could not pay in the first month and had the money by the fourth.

Struck twice in the same place. A second wound on an open one, or on the pale place a healed one left, sets at once, on the day, and no mender alive has undone it. This is why a pale place is worth knowing about, and why a certain kind of thrower pays an assayer for the reading on an opponent before a bout. The halls call that scouting. The menders have another word for it.

Tam Corrow of the Hallenfeld barges took a Core wound from a drowned thing under Chainbridge at nineteen, lost the pitch of voices, paid a mender through one hard summer and had it back, with a pale place left of center to remember it by. Eleven years later a rival who had bought his slip put a second stroke on the same spot in a tavern yard. Corrow is a Stage VII Riverblade with a good name on the water, and he has not heard a tune since. He can tell you a fiddle is playing. He says it sounds like someone describing one.

Mending draws on the patient. A soul wound closes with the patient’s own Essence, re-stamped across the gap, since nobody else’s will take. So a fighter under a mender’s care is a fighter spending reserve on being whole, and one who goes back into the ring half-closed is spending from a purse with a hole in it. Menders refuse to sit a patient who is still flagging, and the good ones refuse a patient who fights between sittings.

IV. The Menders

Soul-mending is a trade, with a bench, a fee and a reputation to lose, and it has nothing to do with the miracle sellers at the fair. A mender is a Spirit Path practitioner, an Answerer at the least for shallow work and a Confessor or better for anything deep, since the work is reaching into a willing soul and finding what is lodged or torn there. Temples keep them, the great houses retain them, the fighting halls have one on a stipend and complain about the stipend, and a working town of any size has a mender’s door with a lamp over it, usually near the physician’s and never on good terms with him.

What a sitting is. The patient sits. The mender reads the wound, sets the soul back in true if it has been shifted, and then has the patient do the long part, which is to tell the thin thing over: the brother’s name and the brother’s face and the day they stole the pears, again and again, while the mender holds the edges of the cut together so that what the patient says has somewhere to stay. It is slow, it is dull, and it leaves both of them gray and cold. A mender cannot put back what is gone. She can close a wound around what is left, stop it spreading, and keep a deep one from setting.

What it costs. The prices below are the Guild-city rate, set against a physician’s visit at 1 to 3 silver and a surgeon’s operation at 5 to 40 gold on What Things Cost. The fee is quoted before the first sitting and paid before the second.

ItemPriceNote

Who goes without. The arithmetic is the same as for a broken leg. A rich fighter takes a deep wound on a Tuesday and is under a Confessor’s hands by Wednesday, and by harvest has a pale place and a story. A poor one waits to see whether it will close by itself, and a deep wound never does, and the season runs out. The unwoken take soul wounds too, from haunted ground and from fights they were standing too near, and most of what a village calls a changed man, a woman who came back from the fen wrong or a child who stopped laughing is a set wound nobody had the silver to read.

A warning from the bench. There is a brisk trade in “restoring” a set wound, sold to the families of the well-off. What is sold is a second stamp laid over the place by someone else’s hand. It takes, for a while, and the patient is delighted, and what he has got back is somebody else’s memory of pears. The Guild fines for it. The temples have a harsher word.

V. The Second Case

Lady Maren Aldous of the Banner Houses took a laid dread at the Assize of Greywater, from a rival’s hired Answerer in the gallery, in the middle of her own testimony. It was meant to be shallow, a moment’s disgrace in front of the bench. It did not lift. Her window read dread laid, deep, the color blue went out of her banners as far as she could see them, and for nine weeks she could not climb a stair without her hands shaking, and knew perfectly well there was nothing on the stair.

Her house had a Shepherd under retainer. He sat her four times a week through the autumn, at a fee the house has never disclosed, and by first frost she was back before the same bench, where she won. The pale place is still there, low on the Core, and her enemies have paid to know exactly where. She has worn a reliquary of worked silver over it ever since, changes menders every year so that no one of them knows the whole of her, and is said to be the only woman in the Midmarch who fences with her back to the gallery wall.

The rival’s Answerer was fined eight gold for a working thrown in a court. The Lady considered this the most insulting part.

VI. Flagging on the Spirit Road

The wounds above are done to a soul by somebody else. The Spirit Path also does a quieter version to its own walkers, every time they overspend, and a watcher who knows the signs can tell a Spirit fighter from across a yard by how she tires.

Every practitioner shows the shared tells as the reserve falls: at half the breath shortens and the aura dims, at a quarter comes tremor and a thinning color, at a tenth the aura flickers and the Trait stutters. On top of those, each road flags in its own way, because each spends a different thing. A Body fighter is working raw Aether through the flesh, and what she loses comes off as heat. A Spirit fighter is spending Essence, which is to say herself, and what she loses is paid in the Core as a hollowing. It is never heat. She does not sweat and she does not steam. She goes cold.

ReserveWhat a Spirit fighter shows, on top of the shared tells
HalfThe colors near her go flat. Cloth, banners and painted shields within a few paces look washed, and she sees it worse than anyone
A quarterA hollow cold comes off her, with no frost in it, and she slips a word: the wrong name for a thing, a sentence that ends somewhere else
A tenthNames and memories slip. She knows the face in front of her and cannot find what to call it, and the working she is holding starts to forget what it was for
MaxedA memory gone for good, and a faint. She wakes not knowing what she has lost, which is the nature of the loss, and someone who loves her has to tell her

The last row is a soul wound in everything but the name, self-dealt, and it sets on the day because there is nothing left to mend it with. Old Spirit masters keep a book in their own hand for this reason, and reading it back is said to be the worst hour of the year.

For comparison, and so that the other roads are not mistaken for it: a flagging Body fighter runs hot, with heat-haze thickening on the skin, then steam off the shoulders and cramps, then the skin splitting in fine lines as the Shell cracks, and at the end heat stroke and burst vessels. A flagging Fate fighter looks well, and the trouble is around her: small luck curdles nearby, things begin going wrong within reach, her own name sits wrong in other mouths, and at the end every knot she owes comes due at once. The Three Paths sets the three side by side.

Cost. A shallow wound costs days and a few silver. A deep one costs a season, a year’s wages or a house’s indifference, and a pale place for life. A set one costs whatever it took, for good.

Counter. Integrity and Anchoring, raised honestly, and Ward against the read that finds the weak place. A worked soul-ward. Distance closed before the working is authored, since a Spirit fighter caught out of position has an ordinary body. And a mender’s name, known before the bout and paid before the season is out.
