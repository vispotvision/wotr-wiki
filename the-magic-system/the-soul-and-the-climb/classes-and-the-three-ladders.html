Classes and the Three Ladders

Classes and the Three Ladders

Nov 17, 202644 min read

“Your soul has been watching you for years, and at Stage VI it tells you what it thinks. Bring a drink.”

Advice to new Experts, chalked on the wall of the assay house at Waxgate, and renewed by somebody every time the wall is painted.

Every army in the Hundred Crowns has marched through the Painted Rivers at least twice, and every one of them has used the ford at Threeways, because it runs knee-deep on a good day between banks thick with yellow flag, and because there is a perfectly good ferry beside it that nobody wearing a crown has ever paid for. Bess Tolliver grew up on that ferry. At Glory, Stage VI, her window (the soul’s private report of its own numbers) offered her Pole-Hand, as it has offered every ferry child who lived that long. A rung later it offered her Riverblade, which is what any ferry town produces when you hit people with a long wet pole for ten years and then someone gives you an edge. She was one of a few thousand. A rung after that she came out a Floodbreaker, one of a few hundred, and the quartermasters of the Midmarch had started putting her name in their letters.

On the morning the levies of Pennycrown came down to Threeways, eleven hundred spears in yellow and blue, four carts of bunting and a king who owed her father nineteen crossings, she walked out to the middle of the ford with her skirts tied up, stood knee-deep in bright green water, laid a blade flat across the current, and let the whole valley have it.

"Nothing! Crosses!"

The first rank laughed and walked in. Water presses on whatever is under it according to how much of it is piled above, density times gravity times depth, which is why divers go home with their ears bleeding. Bess had Abython, the Abyssal Pulse, for a lens, which teaches a soul to match the pressure of the deep by teaching it exactly what that pressure is, and her years on the Body Path had taught her that a thing you can read in matter you can also tell to matter. She told that one stretch of river how deep it was. It looked exactly as it had. It was still knee-deep to the eye, and it bore down on those men’s shins with the weight of the sea at its deepest sounding. A body can stand a great deal of weight pressed evenly all over it, which is how the deep fish live, and it cannot stand that weight from the knee down with nothing above to push back. Their greaves folded like wet pasteboard, the blood and the soft meat went up inside their legs, the knees burst, and the front rank knelt in a foot and a half of sunny water and could not be pulled out by their friends, who stepped in to try. The king of Pennycrown paid all nineteen crossings that afternoon, in coin, by messenger, with an apology, and went home by the long road.

Her window arrived that night:

[Threshold reached: Aether IV, Luminous]
[Your life has earned:]
  The Unforded
[Choose.]

There was one name on it, and it told her to choose anyway. No one else in the Archives has ever held that class. The art charges in its own coin, as the big ones do: nothing crosses, and the river has never made an exception for the woman who said so. She has not set foot over running water since, her mother lives on the east bank, and for nineteen years the people of Threeways have listened to the two of them conduct a family quarrel across two hundred yards of green river at the top of their lungs. Tavern rankings put the Unforded among the most frightening classes in the North. She is, by every account, furious.

Three things happened to Bess Tolliver at those thresholds, and they are most of the subject of this page. Her soul named her. The name changed as she lived. And the idea she carried from the first day to the last, that nothing gets across, stayed hers alone. The rest of the page is the ladder she was climbing while it happened, and the two others beside it that she never set foot on.

What a Class Is

Taverns run two things together that the assayers keep apart: the Class, and the ladders.

A Class is what the soul makes of a life. It is a name, offered at thresholds, fitted to how the person has lived, and it arrives on the window in the owner’s own tongue. It gives abilities, shapes growth and evolves by how it is lived, each time the person’s leading ladder rises. It has no ladder of its own. No coil has ever read one. A class name is known to its owner, to anyone with Gnosis Perception (the stat of sensing what a soul is) strong enough to read it off them, and to everyone else exactly as far as the owner cares to tell it, which is why half the Wallbreakers in the Midmarch are something else.

The ladders are what the instruments read. There are three, one to each layer of the Crystal, and they measure how strong a person’s reach is through that layer. Aether Class is the Aether Shell (the Crystal’s outer layer, which turns inner power into outward force) and their influence on the physical world. Essence Class is the Essence Core and their influence on spiritual reality. Thread Class is the Attraction Layer and how much of Fate they can pull down and hold. From Glory each ladder sets the yield (η: the share of each spend that arrives as intended effect, as a distillation has its yield) for workings through its own layer. A practitioner stands in the coil, the brass assay instrument, and the clerk writes Aether III Resonant on the slip, and for a second fee will write the other two, which on most slips is a waste of a second fee.

The Archives file the full law of the Shell under Tier Grade, Bands & the Aether Shell, with its anomalies, and in Fracture of Worlds, Part Seventeen. Every soul has all three layers, whichever Path it leans on, and each ladder can be read on anyone: the Aether rung of a Fate Path auditor, the Thread rung of a Body Path brawler. What follows is first about the name and what people do once they have one, and then about the ladders.

The Offer

When. The first offer comes at Glory, Stage VI, where the ladders first read on the coil. Stages I through V are unclassed, and the unwoken have no class at all: a baker’s window shows her Level, her trade skills and her health, and waking opens the rest of it without yet naming her. After Glory, the offer comes again each time the person’s leading ladder climbs a rung, whichever ladder that is. A Fate-led soul’s class evolves on whichever of its three ladders stands highest, since its Thread rung waits on grip and decades and may never move, which is how a Fate Path fighter with no grip on anything still gets a new name to complain about.

What arrives. A short block, in the owner’s tongue, with one name or several:

[Threshold reached: Aether II, Harmonic]
[Your life has earned:]
  Riverblade
  Toll-Breaker
  Oath-Hand
[Choose.]

That one belonged to a ferryman’s son at Chainbridge who chose wrong, and knew it about a year later. The three names were all true of him. He had fought for the river, he had cheated the chain, and he had sworn to a lord’s steward, and his soul laid out the three roads his life could honestly be said to have taken. Most people get two or three. A soul that has only ever done one thing gets one thing, and a soul with a false account of itself finds the window declining to share that account, which has embarrassed a number of paladins.

Which way it leans. The offer comes through the layer the soul leans on, and it has that layer’s accent. A Body soul hears mostly names of work, weight and matter: Pole-Hand, Wallbreaker, Winter Porter. A Spirit soul hears names of souls, meanings and things kept: Auditor, Bone-Reader, Calls Them In. A Fate soul hears names of bonds, debts and odds: Oath-Hand, Contract Hand, Turncoat. It is a lean, and the soul allows itself surprises. Body-led ferrymen have been offered Oath-Hand, the Archives hold a Spirit Path abbess whose window said Wallbreaker and would not be argued with, and the surprise is usually the truest name on the list.

What it cannot be. A class is never picked off a list of what you would like. A duke’s son who dreams of being a Stormblade and has spent his life being fitted for coats will be offered something about coats. An offer left unanswered keeps the old name at the new rung and goes on waiting, and a few stubborn people have carried a first-rung name up three rungs out of spite. Once taken, the name stays until the next rung. Nobody has ever handed one back, sold one or had one cut out, though the fair-stall trade in promises to do so is brisk.

The bought Stage. A Stage can be bought, as The Ladder and the Draft explains at length, and a Shell that is not its owner’s lead layer can climb on a purse along with the rest, by a paid-for Infusion or down the back stairs of the Forge Rites, two of the four roads on the Shell’s page. A Body-led soul’s Aether rung is another matter. It climbs by working Charge, and nobody has yet found a way to pay someone else to do that. A bought Stage opens a ladder’s ceiling and leaves the rung where it was (the table is under The Three Ladders, below), so what that purse pays for is one dangerous step at a time. The offer cannot be bought with it, because the offer reads the life. House heirs who take Glory in a quiet room with a physician standing by have opened their windows to Well-Attended, Second Son and Patient of Many Doctors. Great families pay good silver to keep those names quiet. Commoners with Gnosis to spare pay good silver to find them out.

What a Class Gives

The name carries real goods. In rough order of how often they turn up:

  • Skills. A class usually comes with a skill or two already started, shown on the sheet with the rest and rising by use from there. A Riverblade wakes up with Wet Footing and Long Measure on the window and an unreasonable confidence on planks. See Skills and Trait Levels.
  • An ability of the class’s own. A small lawful trick that fits the name, listed on the window when gained, the kind of thing a person might have spent years failing to learn. It runs on the owner’s reserve at the owner’s yield (yield) and costs what it costs.
  • An easier road. Certain kinds of move come quicker, and the teachers notice. A Wallbreaker’s elbows learn in a month what a Bookkeeper’s elbows will never learn.
  • A lean in growth. Life spends most of a person’s stat points by what their lead layer does, and a class leans on that: what the name is about grows a little faster, and what it ignores a little slower. Every Wallbreaker in the four quarters has roughly the same shoulders.
  • A shape in the aura. The Wellspring a person has tempered on longest sets their base color, any others add texture and sound, and a class or a big Trait adds a shape, which a good eye can spot at fifty paces. People read strangers off it in every market on the continent, or think they can:

Cherry-red, temper-blue at the edges, smelling of quench oil: an Exuroth lens. A Riverblade’s shape, running in a flowing band around the shoulders.

“Fire school, river town, and he’s hiding a limp.”

The gain shows when it lands:

[Class: Wick-Trimmer]
[Aether III · Essence Ø · Thread Ø]
[Gained: Clean Thread. A flame held to a bootlace's width keeps all its heat.]

What a class never gives is power out of nothing. It does not add a Grade, it does not lift the two-Grade cap on boosts, it opens no Release and it builds no Domain. Those are the person’s to find. The yield comes from the ladders, by the clarity of each layer, and the name merely sits beside them.

How It Evolves

A class grows at each rung of the leading ladder into a form chosen from what you have actually done. When that ladder climbs, the old name is offered back changed, or offered back in pieces, according to how it was lived. Two Auditors going up from the second rung can come out as a Reckoner and a Debt Collector, and they will not be speaking by then. Bess Tolliver’s record is the one every guild hall tells:

Her kit is still one idea: nothing crosses.

There is no tree. Every few years somebody at a fair sells a beautifully inked chart of what a Wallbreaker becomes, with arrows, and guild halls have been known to hang them. The charts describe what happened to the last few people and predict nothing. The Archives hold eleven Wallbreakers who became Siegebreakers and one who became Gentle Hands, after a very bad year, at a rung and a yield that the eleven would have killed for. Parents plan around the charts regardless, and it costs them.

You cannot drill for one. Sects have tried, for centuries, because a known class is an easy thing to put on a recruiting bill. What a sect can do is give its students the same life, the same drills, the same enemies and the same bad food, and the classes do cluster: a school’s colors and a school’s class names come to march together, and a style you can spot at fifty paces gets a name you can guess. The one who goes over the wall to see her dying father comes out of the next threshold with something else, and that one is usually the student the elders start watching.

Conquest has never moved a ladder. A Body-led soul’s Aether rung climbs by working Charge through the Shell, anyone else’s Shell climbs through refinement, by the four roads filed on its own page, and the Core and the Layer are no more impressed by a good day’s killing. Conquest has moved a great many names.

Common Classes and Classes of One

Common classes are famous. A class shared by thousands has songs, slang, a reputation in the bookies’ mouths and a joke the other classes tell about it. Crews advertise for them: wanted, one Wallbreaker, one Ferryman, share of the core. Taverns rank them, the rankings disagree, and fights over the rankings have closed more taverns than fire. A commoner who comes out a Riverblade has a trade anywhere there is water.

Rare classes are a calling card. A few hundred Floodbreakers means that every one of them has heard of the others. Lords hire them by the name. Rivals look for them on purpose.

Classes of one are feared. Some names have been held by exactly one person in the history of the Archives, and those people tend to be the ones the heralds cry and the bounty boards carry. The name is the first thing anyone wants to know about them, and the last thing they can check. Children know the great ones the way they know the names of monsters, and a false claim to one is a good way to be challenged by someone who knows the real holder.

There is one complication the Measurewrights enjoy. A one-person class is one person’s life, so no teacher can prepare you for it, no chart lists what it gives, and no one can warn you what it costs. The Unforded found out about the river by trying to go home.

By People and Region

Peoples tend toward classes and Traits of their own, because a class fits how a life is lived and a place decides a great deal of that. These are tendencies and never locks. There is a Marrowkin Riverblade working the barges below Chainbridge, and he is sick of hearing about it. The names below are gathered from assay-house gossip, sect rolls and three lawsuits, and each arrives on the window in its owner’s own language, so the Common forms are what foreigners call them.

Moves, and the One Concept That Stays Yours

A kit has one wild concept, the thing you can explain in a breath. The concept is the person’s own, and no class has ever supplied one. A class is what the soul says about a life. A concept is what the person decided to do about it. The class gets no vote: a Stage VII Auditor may well fight with soup.

What the class does is shape the moves, the way a trade shapes a hand. Take two Riverblades out of the same ferry town, with the same footing and the same long measure on the sheet, the same way of keeping the blade low and letting the wet plank do half the work.

  • Bess Tolliver’s idea is that nothing crosses. Her Riverblade moves are all cuts laid across a line: Wake Cut, a flat stroke that leaves a seam of pressure standing in the air behind it, and whoever walks through it finds out.
  • Old Latch of Chainbridge’s idea is doors. His Riverblade moves are all cuts that open something: Latch-Lift goes in low at the same angle Bess uses and leaves a neat hinged flap in a man’s mail, his ribs, or on one celebrated evening his brother-in-law’s strongroom.

Same class, same stance, same aura-shape, and a bookie who has seen both will quote you different odds. The class is why they look alike. The concept is why they fight nothing alike.

As the class evolves the moves get new bones, and the name of a move gets bigger at each jump, with the old name staying in use. Techniques themselves show no level on any window.

Trait, Release and Domain are that same concept escalating: the idea, the transformed body, and the world obeying it. A class stays in its own lane through all of that. It shows in how the body carries the Release and in the shape around the aura, and the song writers, who cannot tell any of this apart, will credit the class regardless.

The Three Ladders

Every name above sits beside a ladder, and the ladder is what decides how much of a practitioner’s power gets out of them. A Crystal has three layers and each has a ladder of its own, running Ø to Ω, read from Glory. They print on one line:

[Aether IV · Essence II · Thread Ø]

That line belongs to a hitter with a very clean Shell and some schooling in souls, and like nearly everyone alive she has never held a thread.

Each ladder sets the yield for workings through its own layer, and each layer works a different thing. The Core authors everything, and the layer it lands through sets the rung. A blow by hand, or a burn that lives in the tissue, is raw Aether (mana, in plain words): drawn in, packed unrefined in the Shell as the Charge, shaped there by the Wellspring’s lens and driven out through the Shell. None of it is ever refined into Essence, and it reads the Aether rung. A working authored in the Core is paid in Essence (spiritual energy: Aether refined in the Crystal and stamped with its owner), spent through the Core, and reads the Essence rung. A pull takes hold of Thread, drawn down through the Attraction Layer and paid for out of the puller’s Essence, and reads the Thread rung.

Fighters name the three styles for what they spend: Aether Manipulation, Essence Manipulation and Thread Manipulation. The halls used to wince at the first of those, and it is now the literal truth, since a Body fighter is handling the world’s own Aether with her Shell and nothing else. Measurewright Odo Farrant of the Waxgate assay house, who has grumbled about bracket slang for forty years, has merely changed grumbles. His present one concerns the young hitters who hear “Aether Manipulation” and conclude that power which is not theirs must be free. It is not theirs and it is not free. Spending Essence is spending yourself, and spending Charge is spending the world, and the body pays for the handling in heat, torn fiber and recoil, to the last copper. Farrant keeps a jar of teeth on his desk from people who thought they could skip the body’s bill. The Three Paths has the whole list of which layer reads what, trade by trade. One person can be clean in one layer and roaring in another, so a yield on a slip is only ever a yield for something. The name changes; the share that arrives follows the layer. A Riverblade at Aether II leaks what a Bookkeeper at Aether II leaks, whatever the songs say.

The one table: Stage against rung

The Stage sets a ladder’s ceiling, and practice moves you up to it. A Stage says how high a road may go. It has never carried anyone there. A bought Stage opens the ceiling and not the rung, which is how a rich Stage VIII can stand under a roof marked Radiant and read Aether I, and why the fighting halls’ field average at that Stage sits so far below what the Stage allows. Which rung you stand on follows your road.

  • The ladder of your leading Path climbs toward its ceiling as you practice, and your Class evolves when it does. For a Body-led soul that practice is working Charge, and for a Spirit-led one it is work on souls and meanings.
  • A Fate-led soul is the exception on one point only. Its Fate Path Stage climbs with the soul’s Stage like any Path’s, and the Fate gates open with it, but its Thread rung waits on grip and decades, so its Class evolves on whichever of its three ladders stands highest.
  • The ladder of a Walked second Path runs a rung behind until that Path is mastered.
  • A Studied ladder runs two rungs behind the lead and stops at II, or at I for Thread.
  • A ladder on a road you have never touched stays at the bottom. The Shell takes its first rung at Glory in any woken soul, because everything a person does in the world has to leave by it. The Core stays Silent until someone has used it for something, and the Layer stays Unthreaded unless something extraordinary happens.

The Stages in the table are a ceiling and the rungs below are what people have done under it, so a Stage XI on Aether II is common and nobody thinks less of her until she leaks on them. A rung on a sealed card that sits above the ladder for its Stage stands as recorded: a Baptism or an Infusion from above can reach past a ceiling, the coil does not argue, and the Measurewrights write the word anomaly beside it and charge extra. The Soul Crystal Tier is a separate reading again: what the Crystal as a whole has grown into, read by Stage, and it lags a bought Stage until Level fills it, as the Shell’s page sets out.

Aether Class: the Shell

How strong your influence on the physical world is. Its mechanism is clarity: how cleanly the Shell works raw Aether, the Charge packed in it, into matter, force and heat. Bess Tolliver climbed this one. It is the ladder the old registers mean when they say “Class” with a numeral and nothing else, since for a long while it was the only one anybody paid to have read.

Essence Class: the Core

How strong your influence on spiritual reality is: souls, spirits, the dead, what a thing means. A wall will tell you nothing about Essence Class, and its great holders have often been people you could knock down with a loaf.

For eleven years the Pennycrown Yellows drilled in the Bellmarsh. They had died there to a man in a single afternoon, four hundred spears in yellow and blue, and nobody had told them. Reed-cutters heard the sergeant’s count at dawn, the fen villages sickened with a homesickness that had no home in it, and two bishops and a Sancta Lux exorcist in scarlet went out in turn with bell and banner to order the dead to lie down, and came back hoarse. Then the villages took up a collection and sent for Dorrit Quill, a wide, cheerful laundress from Tallow Row in a mustard-yellow apron, on the Spirit Path these thirty years, whose slip read Essence III and Aether I, and who by her own account could not crack a walnut at the first try. She walked out into the marsh with a basket of new bread and stood at the end of their line. She commanded nobody. She called the company roll, in the voice of a woman calling children in to supper, all four hundred names, which had taken her a year to collect out of the parish books, and four hundred voices no living ear could quite hear answered here. Then she turned round and walked home, and they fell in behind her. It took nineteen days. Every market on the road went quiet as she came through with the dogs lying flat and a cold wind at her back, though not a reed bent and not a cup spilled from one end of that road to the other. At each village a few of the Yellows fell out of the file and went in at their own doors, and that night someone in that house slept. Her window arrived on the sixth day, somewhere south of Tollgarth:

What a Ferryman does, and the one thing she does not. The rung has five works, and the temples list them in this order. She guides: a soul that has lost the road is walked to it and across. She questions: the dead will answer her, truthfully as far as they know, for as long as she can hold the crossing open. She lays to rest: what lingers is set down on the far side and stays there. She carries a message, one way or the other, word for word, and is paid well by widows and badly by heirs. And she binds a lingering dead: a shade that will not go and cannot be let loose is tied to a place, a thing or a term, until someone can do better by it. Every one of those moves a soul or a word across the Veil. None of them moves a life back. The dead stay dead, and a Ferryman who is asked will say so before she names her fee, which is how you tell her from the other kind. Anything closer to a return than a message is no Ferryman’s work at all: it is a blessing of the sort that shakes the world, from a giver who can afford it, and by every account on file it comes back as a curse. Soul hurts short of death are another trade’s business, set out in Soul Wounds and the Menders.

An Essence Ferryman has nothing to do with the Ferryman on a crew’s bill, who is a class and works a boat. Everyone concerned has complained.

Thread Class: the Layer

How much of Fate you can pull down and hold. This is the steep one. Most souls stay Ø for life, a great many Grandmasters among them, and it costs them nothing they would notice. A Fate Path fighter’s everyday weapons, Range, Aura Pressure, presence and bonds, work at any rung, and anyone can keep a vow, because a vow binds a person to themselves and pulls nothing down: a vow spends the soul’s own share, and a pull reaches above it. The share is part of the reserve, so a vow drains it like any working, spent through the Layer, and it refills the same way. The ladder measures one thing only, which is what happens when you reach up.

What a Fate soul holds is Essence. It refines Aether in the Crystal as a Spirit soul does, and it pays its pulls from that. Thread is never stored. It is pulled down for one working and is gone when the working is, which is why no window has a Thread bar, why Core stock refills a Fate worker as it does a Spirit one, and why a Fate fighter who has run herself dry is hollow and cold as well as unlucky.

It opens no earlier than Glory unless a bloodline or a blessing gives a grip, which is why a Moto child can read Thread I at an age when other children are reading their sums, and why the rest of the world dislikes hearing about it. Study takes anyone as far as I and no further. Thread has no Stage climb. It moves by grip, from blood or a blessing, plus decades of use, and a sworn hitter who has kept every vow she ever made reads I or II at the very most. Blood opens the ladder early and never lifts its cap: a Work of Hataraki is an inherited grip on one function, which lowers the chance of failure and has never lowered the price. A personal grip, found by nobody’s teaching, can be won once. And its numeral gives a yield two rungs below the same numeral on the other ladders: a Thread III Spinner loses what an Aether I brawler loses, and I and II sit under the bottom of the other ladders’ table altogether: about a tenth of what is spent on a pull arrives at Touched, and about a fifth at Drawn. The pulls work. They cost a huge share of the reserve for what they move, and they often fail. That is the whole reason a pull low down costs what it costs. No drill helps. Everything you understand is made of the thing you are trying to move, as The Three Paths explains at more length than anyone enjoys.

Pell Oakshot, called the Long Odds, gave twenty-three years to the Fate Path and was known from Sealhall to the Wend as the dullest duelist on the circuit, a thin woman in a plum-colored coat who stood still, talked too much and lost on points. In the final at the Saltgate Cod Fair she drew Anders Holm, a Stage above her and a head taller, in a gilt harness his cousin had mended cheap, and for four exchanges he broke things on her while the crowd threw herring. Then she went still in a different way. Her breath held on a count that was not the count of the exchange, and the ring went slightly too tidy, and people along the rope stopped chewing without knowing why. Holm’s left greave strap, which had been going for a month, let go as his weight came onto it, his foot slid a hand’s width on the wet boards, and her cut, an ordinary cut, was already there, and it went in under his arm. She fell over a moment after he did. This is what she was reading, face down:

[Pull: landed · Thread II, Drawn]
[Essence: 6% ▓░░░░░░░░░]
[Hit: both eyes bleeding. Sight down a Grade.]
[Knot: one, small. Owed within days.]

The bookies paid at forty to one and watched her for a repeat for the rest of her career, which ran eleven more years. It never came, a personal grip being a thing a soul wins once. Asked at the Fair how she had done it, she said that if she knew, it would not have worked.

Where the lost share goes depends on the layer, and the three are not alike. What the Shell loses burns in its own furnace, and shows as heat on the skin and in the air: the Shell is the athanor, the furnace that never goes cold, and that heat is the furnace burning, never power thrown away. What the Core loses is paid in the Core as a hollowing, a cold and a flattening of color, and never as heat. What a pull loses stays on the Layer as a debt, the knot, which the world collects later.

The jump from II to III is the largest single gain in fidelity on a ladder, which is why an Essence III Auditor walked the Barn to death around the Harl Cross market. The full account of that night is on the Shell’s page, where the yield table by Stage also lives, with the rule for which to read: a read rung overrides the Stage’s figure. And a good figure on the Thread column buys clean delivery and never an easy pull. A Thread V Weaver still pays in years, in blood and sometimes in everything, because what she is spending against is the distance of the result from likely, and no ladder has ever shortened that.

What a pull costs

A pull is priced by rung against distance from likely, as a share of the reserve, which for a Fate worker is Essence. The Measurewrights keep this table in pencil, and the people on it do not fill out forms. Read it for one result in one fight. A Work moves the failure on that table and leaves the share alone. A vow appears nowhere on it, because a vow spends the soul’s own share and reaches for nothing above it. The table is also only the first of two bills. Every pull on it, landed or failed, leaves a knot that the world collects afterward, sized by the same distance from likely, and no Work has ever shrunk one. How to see a pull coming, how long the warning runs and what the knot does are set out in The Tell and the Knot. And what lies past the right-hand column is the Stage XII soul emptied to the floor with nothing to show for it, which the Archives hold on file several times over.

What People Do About It

They brag. The class is the first thing on a fighter’s lips and the first thing on a slate, chalked beside Stage and Path and color. It costs nothing to claim, and the coil cannot contradict it.

They lie. Only a stronger Gnosis Perception against a weaker Resilience Ward can read the name, and everything else is an estimate said aloud. Every crew has met a Stormblade whose aura had a suspiciously ferry-shaped band about it. A sworn statement of your class before a proper court is another matter, because an oath is small Fate, and Windows and the Sheet sets out what it costs.

They hire by it. A class on a commission bill is shorthand for what a person will be good at on the worst day of the job. Merchants pay more for an honest Contract Hand than for a Stage over her.

They get embarrassed by it. The name arrives on the window at the moment of greatest pride, and it is usually a remark about the owner’s character. Coward’s Patience has held the Cutwall stair for twenty-six years and answers to it.

They argue about what it proves. Measurewrights will tell you for free that the offer proves the Continuum (the whole lawful order of reality) reads biography, and for a fee that it proves no such thing. The priests of half a dozen faiths claim the name as a calling and the other half as an accusation. The Measurewrights’ hall has produced four monographs on whether a class names a person’s life or nudges it, and the only settled finding is that Wallbreakers have stopped reading them.
