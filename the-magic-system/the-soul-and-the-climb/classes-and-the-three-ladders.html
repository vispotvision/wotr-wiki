Classes and Mastery

Classes and Mastery

Nov 28, 202644 min read

New to the climb? Begin with How Power Grows: the soul climbs Stages, the life climbs Level, the hands climb skills. This page is the two things read off them that people talk about most, the Class the soul offers and the Mastery that says how far a soul’s reach runs on each road. The older registers filed it as Classes and the Three Ladders.

“Your soul has been watching you for years, and at Stage VI it tells you what it thinks. Bring a drink.”

Advice to new Experts, chalked on the wall of the assay house at Waxgate, and renewed by somebody every time the wall is painted.

Every army in the Hundred Crowns has marched through the Painted Rivers at least twice, and every one of them has used the ford at Threeways, because it runs knee-deep on a good day between banks thick with yellow flag, and because there is a perfectly good ferry beside it that nobody wearing a crown has ever paid for. Bess Tolliver grew up on that ferry. At Glory, Stage VI, her window (the soul’s private report of its own numbers) offered her Pole-Hand, as it has offered every ferry child who lived that long. A rung of Mastery later it offered her Riverblade, which is what any ferry town produces when you hit people with a long wet pole for ten years and then someone gives you an edge. She was one of a few thousand. A rung after that she came out a Floodbreaker, one of a few hundred, and the quartermasters of the Midmarch had started putting her name in their letters.

On the morning the levies of Pennycrown came down to Threeways, eleven hundred spears in yellow and blue, four carts of baggage and a king who owed her father nineteen crossings, she walked out to the middle of the ford with her skirts tied up, stood knee-deep in bright green water, laid a blade flat across the current, and let the whole valley have it.

"Nothing crosses."

The first rank came on at the march and walked in. Water presses on whatever is under it according to how much of it is piled above, density times gravity times depth, which is why divers go home with their ears bleeding. Bess had Abython, the Abyssal Pulse, for a lens, which teaches a soul to match the pressure of the deep by teaching it exactly what that pressure is, and her years on the Body Path had taught her that a thing you can read in matter you can also tell to matter. She told that one stretch of river how deep it was. It looked exactly as it had. It was still knee-deep to the eye, and it bore down on those men’s shins with the weight of the sea at its deepest sounding. A body can stand a great deal of weight pressed evenly all over it, which is how the deep fish live, and it cannot stand that weight from the knee down with nothing above to push back. Their greaves folded like wet pasteboard, the blood and the soft meat went up inside their legs, the knees burst, and the front rank knelt in a foot and a half of sunny water and could not be pulled out by their friends, who stepped in to try. The king of Pennycrown sent the nineteen crossings that afternoon, in coin, by a herald under a white branch, and took his host home by the long road with the front rank carried on hurdles.

Her window arrived that night:

[Threshold reached: Body IV, Luminous]
[Your life has earned:]
  The Unforded
[Choose.]

There was one name on it, and it told her to choose anyway. No one else in the Archives has ever held that class. The art charges in its own coin, as the big ones do: nothing crosses, and the river has never made an exception for the woman who said so. She has not set foot over running water since, her mother lives on the east bank, and for nineteen years the two of them have spoken across two hundred yards of green river or not at all. Tavern rankings put the Unforded among the most frightening classes in the North. Her mother is old now, and all of Threeways knows that when she is buried Bess will stand on the west bank for it.

Three things happened to Bess Tolliver at those thresholds, and they are most of the subject of this page. Her soul named her. The name changed as she lived. And the idea she carried from the first day to the last, that nothing gets across, stayed hers alone. The rest of the page is the Mastery her Level was reading off while it happened, and the same rungs on the two roads she never set foot on.

What a Class Is

Taverns run two things together that the assayers keep apart: the Class, and Mastery.

A Class is what the soul makes of a life. It is a name, never a number, offered at thresholds, fitted to how the person has lived, and it arrives on the window in the owner’s own tongue. It is first offered at Glory, with Mastery I, and it evolves when the lead Mastery rises. Each evolution brings one move that fits the new name and a lean on where life spends the person’s stat points. It has no rung of its own. No coil has ever read one. A class name is known to its owner, to anyone with Gnosis Perception (the stat of sensing what a soul is) strong enough to read it off them, and to everyone else exactly as far as the owner cares to tell it, which is why half the Wallbreakers in the Midmarch are something else.

Mastery is what the instruments read. It is one rung for each Path a soul walks, and it measures how strong a person’s reach is through that Path’s layer. Body Mastery is the Aether Shell (the Crystal’s outer layer, which turns inner power into outward force) and their influence on the physical world. Spirit Mastery is the Essence Core and their influence on spiritual reality. Fate Mastery is the Attraction Layer and how much of Fate they can pull down and hold. From Glory each sets the yield (η: the share of each spend that arrives as intended effect, as a distillation has its yield) for workings through its own layer. A practitioner stands in the coil, the brass assay instrument, and the clerk writes Body III on the slip, and the old clerks add Resonant out of habit, and for a second fee will read the roads the soul has only walked or studied, which on most slips is a waste of a second fee.

The Archives file the full law of the Shell under Grades, Ranks and the Aether Shell, with its anomalies, and in Fracture of Worlds, Part Seventeen. Every soul has all three layers, whichever Path it leans on, and Mastery is read on every road the soul walks, Mastered, Walked or Studied: the Body rung of a Fate Path auditor who studied the Shell, the Spirit rung of a Body Path brawler who walks the Core besides. A road a soul has never set foot on reads nothing. What follows is first about the name and what people do once they have one, and then about Mastery.

The Offer

When. The first offer comes at Glory, Stage VI, with the first rung of Mastery, where the coil first reads one. Stages I through V are unclassed, and the unwoken have no class at all: a baker’s window shows her Level, her trade skills and her health, and waking opens the rest of it without yet naming her. After Glory, the offer comes again each time the lead road’s Mastery climbs a rung. A Fate-led soul with no grip on the thread stops at the second rung, and its class stops with it, which is how a Fate Path fighter with no grip on anything comes to carry the same name for forty years and complain about it the whole time.

What arrives. A short block, in the owner’s tongue, with one name or several:

[Threshold reached: Body II, Harmonic]
[Your life has earned:]
  Riverblade
  Toll-Breaker
  Oath-Hand
[Choose.]

That one belonged to a ferryman’s son at Chainbridge who chose wrong, and knew it about a year later. The three names were all true of him. He had fought for the river, he had cheated the chain, and he had sworn to a lord’s steward, and his soul laid out the three roads his life could honestly be said to have taken. Most people get two or three. A soul that has only ever done one thing gets one thing, and a soul with a false account of itself finds the window declining to share that account, which has embarrassed a number of paladins.

Which way it leans. The offer comes through the layer the soul leans on, and it has that layer’s accent. A Body soul hears mostly names of work, weight and matter: Pole-Hand, Wallbreaker, Winter Porter. A Spirit soul hears names of souls, meanings and things kept: Auditor, Bone-Reader, Calls Them In. A Fate soul hears names of bonds, debts and odds: Oath-Hand, Contract Hand, Turncoat. It is a lean, and the soul allows itself surprises. Body-led ferrymen have been offered Oath-Hand, the Archives hold a Spirit Path abbess whose window said Wallbreaker and would not be argued with, and the surprise is usually the truest name on the list.

What it cannot be. A class is never picked off a list of what you would like. A duke’s son who dreams of being a Stormblade and has spent his life at his father’s elbow will be offered a name for the life he has had, and it will not be Stormblade. An offer left unanswered keeps the old name at the new rung and goes on waiting, and a few stubborn people have carried a first-rung name up three rungs out of spite, giving up the moves the soul had for them. Once taken, the name stays until the next rung. Nobody has ever handed one back, sold one or had one cut out, though the fair-stall trade in promises to do so is brisk.

The bought Stage. A Stage can be bought, as The Ladder and the Draft explains at length, and Mastery cannot. It is read from Level under the Stage’s ceiling, a bought Stage opens the ceiling and leaves the Level where it was (the table is under Mastery, below), and a Stage held above its Level runs Overgrown, its Mastery reading low or not at all. The offer cannot be bought either, because the offer reads the life. House heirs who take Glory in a quiet room with a physician standing by have opened their windows to Well-Attended, Second Son and Kept Indoors. Great families pay good silver to keep those names quiet. Commoners with Gnosis to spare pay good silver to find them out.

What a Class Gives

The name carries real goods. In rough order of how often they turn up:

  • One move. Each offer brings one move that fits the name, a small lawful trick listed on the window when gained, the kind of thing a person might have spent years failing to learn. It runs on the owner’s reserve at the owner’s yield and costs what it costs, and it often arrives with the skill it runs on already started: a Riverblade wakes up with Wet Footing and Long Measure on the window and a sure foot on planks. See Skills and Traits.
  • An easier road. Certain kinds of move come quicker, and the teachers notice. A Wallbreaker’s elbows learn in a month what a Bookkeeper’s elbows will never learn.
  • A lean in growth. Life spends most of a person’s stat points by what their lead layer does, and a class leans on that: what the name is about grows a little faster, and what it ignores a little slower. Every Wallbreaker in the four quarters has roughly the same shoulders.
  • A shape in the aura. The Wellspring a person has tempered on longest sets their base color, any others add texture and sound, and a class or a big Trait adds a shape, which a good eye can spot at fifty paces. People read strangers off it in every market on the continent, or think they can:

Cherry-red, temper-blue at the edges, smelling of quench oil: an Exuroth lens. A Riverblade’s shape, running in a flowing band around the shoulders.

“Fire school, river town, and he’s hiding a limp.”

The gain shows when it lands:

[Class: Wick-Trimmer]
[Body III]
[Gained: Clean Thread. A flame held to a bootlace's width keeps all its heat.]

What a class never gives is power out of nothing. It adds no step on the scale, it does not lift the two-step cap on boosts, it opens no Release and it builds no Domain. Those are the person’s to find. The yield comes from Mastery, by the clarity of each layer, and the name merely sits beside it.

How It Evolves

A class grows at each rung of the lead road’s Mastery into a form chosen from what you have actually done. When that rung climbs, the old name is offered back changed, or offered back in pieces, according to how it was lived. Two Auditors going up from the second rung can come out as a Reckoner and a Debt Collector, and they will not be speaking by then. Bess Tolliver’s record is the one every guild hall tells:

Her kit is still one idea: nothing crosses.

There is no tree. Every few years somebody at a fair sells a beautifully inked chart of what a Wallbreaker becomes, with arrows, and guild halls have been known to hang them. The charts describe what happened to the last few people and predict nothing. The Archives hold eleven Wallbreakers who became Siegebreakers and one who became Gentle Hands, after a very bad year, at a rung and a yield that the eleven would have killed for. Parents plan around the charts regardless, and it costs them.

You cannot drill for one. Sects have tried, for centuries, because a known class is an easy thing to put on a recruiting bill. What a sect can do is give its students the same life, the same drills, the same enemies and the same bad food, and the classes do cluster: a school’s colors and a school’s class names come to march together, and a style you can spot at fifty paces gets a name you can guess. The one who goes over the wall to see her dying father comes out of the next threshold with something else, and that one is usually the student the elders start watching.

Conquest has never chosen a name. A good day’s killing is Level like any hard thing, and Level moves the rung, so a famous fight reaches the class in the end, by the long way round, as the Shell’s page sets out. What the new name is, the soul decides from the whole life. Conquest has moved a great many names, and named none of them.

Common Classes and Classes of One

Common classes are famous. A class shared by thousands has songs, slang, a reputation in the bookies’ mouths and a joke the other classes tell about it. Crews advertise for them: wanted, one Wallbreaker, one Ferryman, share of the core. Taverns rank them, the rankings disagree, and fights over the rankings have closed more taverns than fire. A commoner who comes out a Riverblade has a trade anywhere there is water.

Rare classes are a calling card. A few hundred Floodbreakers means that every one of them has heard of the others. Lords hire them by the name. Rivals look for them on purpose.

Classes of one are feared. Some names have been held by exactly one person in the history of the Archives, and those people tend to be the ones the heralds cry and the bounty boards carry. The name is the first thing anyone wants to know about them, and the last thing they can check. Children know the great ones the way they know the names of monsters, and a false claim to one is a good way to be challenged by someone who knows the real holder.

There is one complication the Measurewrights enjoy. A one-person class is one person’s life, so no teacher can prepare you for it, no chart lists what it gives, and no one can warn you what it costs. The Unforded found out about the river by trying to go home.

By People and Region

Peoples tend toward classes and Traits of their own, because a class fits how a life is lived and a place decides a great deal of that. These are tendencies and never locks. There is a Marrowkin Riverblade working the barges below Chainbridge, and he is sick of hearing about it. The names below are gathered from assay-house gossip, sect rolls and three lawsuits, and each arrives on the window in its owner’s own language, so the Common forms are what foreigners call them.

Moves, and the One Concept That Stays Yours

A kit has one wild concept, the thing you can explain in a breath. The concept is the person’s own, and no class has ever supplied one. A class is what the soul says about a life. A concept is what the person decided to do about it. The class gets no vote: a Stage VII Auditor may well fight with soup.

What the class does is shape the moves, the way a trade shapes a hand. Take two Riverblades out of the same ferry town, with the same footing and the same long measure on the sheet, the same way of keeping the blade low and letting the wet plank do half the work.

  • Bess Tolliver’s idea is that nothing crosses. Her Riverblade moves are all cuts laid across a line: Wake Cut, a flat stroke that leaves a seam of pressure standing in the air behind it, and whoever walks through it finds out.
  • Old Latch of Chainbridge’s idea is doors. His Riverblade moves are all cuts that open something: Latch-Lift goes in low at the same angle Bess uses and leaves a neat hinged flap in a man’s mail, his ribs, or on one celebrated evening his brother-in-law’s strongroom.

Same class, same stance, same aura-shape, and a bookie who has seen both will quote you different odds. The class is why they look alike. The concept is why they fight nothing alike.

As the class evolves the moves get new bones, and the name of a move gets bigger at each jump, with the old name staying in use. Techniques themselves show no level on any window.

Trait, Release and Domain are that same concept escalating: the idea, the transformed body, and the world obeying it. A class stays in its own lane through all of that. It shows in how the body carries the Release and in the shape around the aura, and the song writers, who cannot tell any of this apart, will credit the class regardless.

Mastery

Every name above sits beside a rung, and the rung is what decides how much of a practitioner’s power gets out of them. Mastery is how well a soul works its road’s own substance: one rung for each Path a person walks, numbered I to V, with Ω above them for the very few. The slip prints it in one line:

[Body IV · Spirit II]

That line belongs to a hitter with a very clean Shell and some schooling in souls, and like nearly everyone alive she has never walked the Fate road at all, so the slip says nothing of it.

Each road’s rung sets the yield for workings through its own layer, and each layer works a different thing. The Core authors everything, and the layer it lands through sets the rung. A blow by hand, or a burn that lives in the tissue, is raw Aether (mana, in plain words): drawn in, packed unrefined in the Shell as the Charge, shaped there by the Wellspring’s lens and driven out through the Shell. None of it is ever refined into Essence, and it reads Body Mastery. A working authored in the Core is paid in Essence (spiritual energy: Aether refined in the Crystal and stamped with its owner), spent through the Core, and reads Spirit Mastery. A pull takes hold of Thread, drawn down through the Attraction Layer and paid for out of the puller’s Essence, and reads Fate Mastery.

Fighters name the three styles for what they spend: Aether Manipulation, Essence Manipulation and Thread Manipulation. The halls used to wince at the first of those, and it is now the literal truth, since a Body fighter is handling the world’s own Aether with her Shell and nothing else. Measurewright Odo Farrant of the Waxgate assay house, who has grumbled about bracket slang for forty years, has merely changed grumbles. His present one concerns the young hitters who hear “Aether Manipulation” and conclude that power which is not theirs must be free. It is not theirs and it is not free. Spending Essence is spending yourself, and spending Charge is spending the world, and the body pays for the handling in heat, torn fiber and recoil, to the last copper. Farrant keeps a jar of teeth on his desk from people who thought they could skip the body’s bill. The Three Paths has the whole list of which layer reads what, trade by trade. One person can be clean in one layer and roaring in another, so a yield on a slip is only ever a yield for something. The name changes; the share that arrives follows the layer. A Riverblade at Body II leaks what a Bookkeeper at Body II leaks, whatever the songs say.

The one table: Stage against rung

Mastery is never tracked. The Stage sets the ceiling, and the Level places the soul under it. A Stage says how high a road may go. It has never carried anyone there, and neither has practice on its own: the rung follows Level, and Level follows everything hard and real a soul has done. A bought Stage opens the ceiling and leaves the Level where it was, which is how a rich Stage XII can stand under a roof marked Radiant, Overgrown, and read three rungs short of it, or nothing at all. The roof rises a rung at a time up the whole climb, so the numeral means something at every height, and a soul fresh across a Stage reads a rung under its new roof until its Level grows into the Stage. A new Refraction soul at 130 reads I and reaches II at 150; a new Transcendent at 201 reads II and reaches III at 215; a new Realization soul at 230 reads III and reaches IV at 260; a new Emanation soul at 320 reads IV and reaches V at 340.

The early grip. One exception breaks the silence below Glory. A soul born to one of the Epochs’ lines and holding that line’s instrument, a Moto with a Work of Hataraki, a Zettari with Matla, a Yukari with Null-Light, reads rung I on that instrument’s road from Ascension, the third Stage, and only rung I until Glory. The blood opens one ladder early, and the assay houses keep a separate book for those children.

Inverted. Any rung can be held Inverted, a feared and wrong way of working it, in which the soul takes where it ought to give and empties where it ought to fill. It is a mark beside the rung and never a rung of its own: Spirit IV Inverted. Most souls never see it.

Which rung you stand on follows your road.

  • The Mastery of your leading Path reads from your Level, up to the Stage’s ceiling, and your Class evolves when it rises.
  • A Walked second Path, thirty parts in a hundred of the practice or more, reads a rung behind until that Path is mastered.
  • A Studied Path reads two rungs behind the lead and stops at II.
  • A road you have never walked or studied reads nothing.
  • Fate reads like the others, with one hard limit, set out under Fate Mastery below.

The Stages in the table are a ceiling and the Level places a soul under it, so a Stage XI who reads Body II on a road she only studied is common, and nobody thinks less of her until she leaks on them. Mastery is read from Level for everyone, and the Archives keep no list of people who stand above it. Where an old record shows someone working past the rung their Level gives, that was an overreach: done once, in that moment, at a price, and the record stands as the night they reached past themselves, never as a rung they hold. The Measurewrights still charge extra to write it up. The Crystal’s look is a separate reading again: how the whole stone looks, following the Stage, as the Shell’s page sets out.

Body Mastery: the Shell

How strong your influence on the physical world is. Its mechanism is clarity: how cleanly the Shell works raw Aether, the Charge packed in it, into matter, force and heat. Bess Tolliver climbed this one. It is the rung the old registers mean when they say “Class” with a numeral and nothing else, since for a long while it was the only one anybody paid to have read.

Spirit Mastery: the Core

How strong your influence on spiritual reality is: souls, spirits, the dead, what a thing means. A wall will tell you nothing about Spirit Mastery, and its great holders have often been people you could knock down with a loaf.

For eleven years the Pennycrown Yellows drilled in the Bellmarsh. They had died there to a man in a single afternoon, four hundred spears in yellow and blue, and nobody had told them. Reed-cutters heard the sergeant’s count at dawn, the fen villages sickened with a homesickness that had no home in it, and two bishops and a Sancta Lux exorcist in scarlet went out in turn with bell and banner to order the dead to lie down, and came back hoarse. Then the villages took up a collection and sent for Dorrit Quill, a wide, cheerful laundress from Tallow Row in a mustard-yellow apron, on the Spirit Path these thirty years, whose slip read Spirit III, and who by her own account could not crack a walnut at the first try. She walked out into the marsh with a basket of new bread and stood at the end of their line. She commanded nobody. She called the company roll, in the voice of a woman calling children in to supper, all four hundred names, which had taken her a year to collect out of the parish books, and four hundred voices no living ear could quite hear answered here. Then she turned round and walked home, and they fell in behind her. It took nineteen days. Every market on the road went quiet as she came through with the dogs lying flat and a cold wind at her back, though not a reed bent and not a cup spilled from one end of that road to the other. At each village a few of the Yellows fell out of the file and went in at their own doors, and that night someone in that house slept. Her window arrived on the sixth day, somewhere south of Tollgarth:

What a Ferryman does, and the one thing she does not. The rung has five works, and the temples list them in this order. She guides: a soul that has lost the road is walked to it and across. She questions: the dead will answer her, truthfully as far as they know, for as long as she can hold the crossing open. She lays to rest: what lingers is set down on the far side and stays there. She carries a message, one way or the other, word for word, and is paid well by widows and badly by heirs. And she binds a lingering dead: a shade that will not go and cannot be let loose is tied to a place, a thing or a term, until someone can do better by it. Every one of those moves a soul or a word across the Veil. None of them moves a life back. The dead stay dead, and a Ferryman who is asked will say so before she names her fee, which is how you tell her from the other kind. Anything closer to a return than a message is no Ferryman’s work at all: it is a blessing of the sort that shakes the world, from a giver who can afford it, and by every account on file it comes back as a curse. Soul hurts short of death are another trade’s business, set out in Soul Wounds and the Menders.

A Spirit Ferryman has nothing to do with the Ferryman on a crew’s bill, who is a class and works a boat. Everyone concerned has complained.

Fate Mastery: the Layer

How much of Fate you can pull down and hold. This is the hard one. A Fate Path fighter’s everyday weapons, Range, Aura Pressure, presence and bonds, work at any rung, and anyone can keep a vow, because a vow binds a person to themselves and pulls nothing down: a vow spends the soul’s own share, and a pull reaches above it. The share is part of the reserve, so a vow drains it like any working, spent through the Layer, and it refills the same way. The rung measures one thing only, which is what happens when you reach up.

What a Fate soul holds is Essence. It refines Aether in the Crystal as a Spirit soul does, and it pays its pulls from that. Thread is never stored. It is pulled down for one working and is gone when the working is, which is why no window has a Thread bar, why Core stock refills a Fate worker as it does a Spirit one, and why a Fate fighter who has run herself dry is hollow and cold as well as unlucky.

Fate climbs with Level like the other two roads, and its grip sets the ceiling. Without a grip on the thread a Fate soul stops at II, however long it lives and however many vows it keeps. A grip is a Work or a blessing. A Work of Hataraki is an inherited grip on one function, which lowers the chance of failure and has never lowered the price; a blessing that grants a grip lifts the ceiling the same way. Either lifts it to the Stage’s own and never past it. Blood alone is not a grip, and the cadet lines of the great Fate houses, who have the blood and were never given a Work, stop at II like anyone else and are bitter about it in every generation. A holder of an Epoch’s Work reads Fate I from Ascension, the early grip, which is why a Moto child can read the first rung at an age when other children are reading their sums, and why the rest of the world dislikes hearing about it. Fate’s yield reads from the same table as everyone else’s, and no drill helps the reading: everything you understand is made of the thing you are trying to move, as The Three Paths explains at more length than anyone enjoys. Fate’s price is not the yield. It is the share a pull costs, below, and the knot after it.

Pell Oakshot, called the Long Odds, gave twenty-three years to the Fate Path and was known from Sealhall to the Wend as the dullest duelist on the circuit, a thin woman in a plum-colored coat who stood still, talked too much and lost on points. In the final at the Saltgate Cod Fair she drew Anders Holm, a Stage above her and a head taller, in a gilt harness his cousin had mended cheap, and for four exchanges he broke things on her while the crowd bayed for the finish. Then she went still in a different way. Her breath held on a count that was not the count of the exchange, and the ring went slightly too tidy, and people along the rope stopped chewing without knowing why. Holm’s left greave strap, which had been going for a month, let go as his weight came onto it, his foot slid a hand’s width on the wet boards, and her cut, an ordinary cut, was already there, and it went in under his arm. She fell over a moment after he did. This is what she was reading, face down:

[Pull: landed · Fate II, Drawn]
[Essence: 6% ▓░░░░░░░░░]
[Hit: both eyes bleeding. Sight dimmed by half.]
[Knot: one, small. Owed within days.]

The bookies paid at forty to one and watched her for a repeat for the rest of her career, which ran eleven more years. It never came. She had no grip, and a lean that size from a soul without one is a thing that lands once, if ever. Asked at the Fair how she had done it, she said that if she knew, it would not have worked.

Where the lost share goes depends on the layer, and the three are not alike. What the Shell loses burns in its own furnace, and shows as heat on the skin and in the air: the Shell is the athanor, the furnace that never goes cold, and that heat is the furnace burning, never power thrown away. What the Core loses is paid in the Core as a hollowing, a cold and a flattening of color, and never as heat. What a pull loses stays on the Layer as a debt, the knot, which the world collects later.

The jump from II to III is the largest single gain in fidelity on any road, which is why a Spirit III Auditor walked the Barn to death around the Harl Cross market. The full account of that night is on the Shell’s page, where the field average by rank also lives, with the rule for which to read: a read rung overrides the rank’s figure. And a good figure on the Fate column buys clean delivery and never an easy pull. A Fate V Weaver still pays in years, in blood and sometimes in everything, because what she is spending against is the distance of the result from likely, and no rung has ever shortened that.

A registered card’s own price for its own pulls governs its holder, as a card’s own yield does. A Work moves the failure on that table and leaves the share alone. A vow appears nowhere on it, because a vow spends the soul’s own share and reaches for nothing above it. The table is also only the first of two bills. Every pull on it, landed or failed, leaves a knot that the world collects afterward, sized by the same distance from likely, and no Work has ever shrunk one. How to see a pull coming, how long the warning runs and what the knot does are set out in The Tell and the Knot. And what lies past the right-hand column is the Stage XII soul emptied to the floor with nothing to show for it, which the Archives hold on file several times over.

The older registers kept these rungs as three ladders, Aether Class, Essence Class and Thread Class, Ø to Ω, each with a sixth lateral rung; an old slip’s Aether III is Body III now, and its sixth rungs, Voidic, Sin-Eater and Severer, are the Inverted mark.

What People Do About It

They brag. The class is the first thing on a fighter’s lips and the first thing on a slate, chalked beside Stage and Path and color. It costs nothing to claim, and the coil cannot contradict it.

They lie. Only a stronger Gnosis Perception against a weaker Resilience Ward can read the name, and everything else is an estimate said aloud. Every crew has met a Stormblade whose aura had a suspiciously ferry-shaped band about it. A sworn statement of your class before a proper court is another matter, because an oath is small Fate, and Windows and the Sheet sets out what it costs.

They hire by it. A class on a commission bill is shorthand for what a person will be good at on the worst day of the job. Merchants pay more for an honest Contract Hand than for a Stage over her.

They get embarrassed by it. The name arrives on the window at the moment of greatest pride, and it is usually a remark about the owner’s character. Coward’s Patience has held the Cutwall stair for twenty-six years and answers to it.

They argue about what it proves. Measurewrights will tell you for free that the offer proves the Continuum (the whole lawful order of reality) reads biography, and for a fee that it proves no such thing. The priests of half a dozen faiths claim the name as a calling and the other half as an accusation. The Measurewrights’ hall has produced four monographs on whether a class names a person’s life or nudges it, and the only settled finding is that Wallbreakers have stopped reading them.
