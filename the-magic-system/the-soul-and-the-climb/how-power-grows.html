The Climb as the Archives Now Reckon It: Stage, Level and the Hands

“The soul climbs, the life piles up, the hands learn. Everything else is somebody reading one of the three.”

Chalked over the door of the assay house at Waxgate, where the old wall of figures used to be. The clerks painted the figures out themselves and say they sleep better.

In the autumn lists at Harl Cross, in the year the Wendle locks were rebuilt, the house of Stanmere sent its youngest son to the sand in a coat of blued plate with gilt at every rivet and a long sword the Appraisers’ stall had priced aloud that morning as Grade A and Very Rare. Leofric Stanmere was nineteen. He had been bought to Glory, the sixth Stage, by four breakthrough Drafts in four quiet rooms, and he was an Expert by rank on every slate in the Cross. When he walked out, the lamp flames along the rail leaned toward him like grass in a wind, and the unwoken in the cheap stands bent at the knee without meaning to, which is what an Expert does to people who have never woken. The bookies made him one to nine.

His opponent was Elswyth Ashby, the lock-keeper at Wendle, an Adept, Stage IV, in a leather jerkin her mother had worn and a quarterstaff with an iron shoe. She had held the Wendle gates through three flood nights while the river tried to take the town, and she had pulled eleven people out of the race with a boathook on the worst of them. Everyone who drank in the Cross knew two things about her. The water in the lock would not lie flat when she stood near it, and had not for a year. And she had never once been offered a Stage, by a Draft or a sect or anybody else.

Stanmere’s first blow put her through the barrier and into the stands. The second she took across the staff and the forearms, and the staff held, and the forearms did not, and she did not fall. Witnesses on the east side swear the sand under her went dark and wet, as if the river had come up through it, and that the heat came off her in a wave they felt on their faces. Then she stood up straight out of the wreck of the rail, and she was Splintering, the fifth Stage, and an Expert like him, and he had never in his life been hit by anyone who meant it.

She broke his sword hand at the wrist on the next exchange and his collarbone on the one after. He had the weight of a Glory soul behind every swing, and his blows were very heavy, and they went where a boy who had trained for three years in a walled garden sent them. His Crystal spilled power into the air every time he moved and none of it would gather into anything he could use. Hers had been ready for a year and had only been waiting for a reason.

The Archives keep the slate from that day under glass. Three marks are chalked beside each name: the Stage, the Level and the staff or the sword. The bookies at Harl Cross had read the first one. This page is about the other two, and about why the first one is still the one everybody says.

The One Sentence

The soul climbs Stages, and each Stage asks one thing of it. The life climbs Level, by everything hard and real a person has done. The hands climb skills, by use. Everything else is read from those three.

That is the whole of the reckoning, and the Archives would like it chalked over every hall door in the four quarters, as the assay house at Waxgate has done. Older books carried a dozen more measurements than this, each with its own ladder and its own clerk, and the halls spent a generation discovering that every one of them was a way of reading one of these three badly.

  • Stage is the soul. It is what the Crystal has become, crossing by crossing, and it is the thing that matters and the thing people say.
  • Level is the life. It is the count of what a person has lived through and done, and it is what lets a Stage hold.
  • Skills are the hands. They rise by doing, and they are what a fight is actually made of.

Leofric Stanmere had a great deal of the first and very little of the other two. Elswyth Ashby had the other two in full and was owed the first.

Stage, Said as Rank

There are sixteen Stages, and nobody outside a hall says their names in ordinary talk. What people say is the rank, one of nine words that every child in the four quarters knows, and the Stage name is the precise term for when precision is wanted: “a Journeyman, Ascension, and close to Flourishing.”

The Crystal’s look is how the stone looks to anyone who can see a soul, in the jewelers’ words: Clouded through the four lowest Stages, Faceted through the Expert years, then Fire, Flawless, Starred and Absolute. It follows the Stage. It is a description, and nobody climbs it.

Lenses. A soul holds one home lens, its first Wellspring, through the Adept years. From Expert it can safely harmonize one more at each rank, so a Master holds three and a Paragon six. A Clouded soul may try for a second lens early, and some do, and the menders see the tears. The base hue a person’s aura shows is the lens they have tempered on longest.

The same words serve two worlds. A fighter’s rank is their Stage. A tradesman’s rank is the Accord title for their trade, earned by the work, as The Tiered Path sets out. So a Master smith may be an Apprentice by Stage, and Master Venn at the Waxgate forge, who makes swords for Grandmasters, is an Apprentice the moment he steps onto the road. People mix the two up constantly, and a fair share of the Accord’s lawsuits begin with a merchant who thought he was hiring one kind of Master and got the other.

The Sixteen Stages gives every Stage in full: its story, what it is from inside, what it looks like and what the folk call it.

What Opens a Stage

A Stage opens when two things are true at once. Its Catalyst has been met, and the soul’s Level can hold it.

The Catalyst is one deed to each Stage, and the Crystal asks it in the terms of the Path the soul leans on: in the flesh and in matter for a Body soul, in a soul or a meaning for a Spirit one, in a bond, an oath or the odds for a Fate one. It comes by crisis, by a long guarded seclusion, by tribulation, or it is handed over in a breakthrough Draft or a blessing. Stage II’s Catalyst is the first lens itself: a Wellspring’s raw Aether, touched under pressure, which is how most people learn which Wellspring they belong to. The Sixteen Stages carries all three forms of every Catalyst, floor by floor.

A Stage is rare, and it is paid for. The Level climbs all year, and the hands get better every week a person works them; that is the climb anyone can watch. A Stage is another thing. Most fighters cross only a handful in a lifetime, and the great ones cross perhaps one in a hard year, sometimes none in five. Every road to it costs blood. A crisis leaves its wound, a seclusion is months of something going wrong in a locked room, and a tribulation takes somebody standing too near. Ask anyone who has crossed what it cost them, and they will tell you, or they will change the subject, and both are answers.

The hold. Each Stage is held from a Level, and below that Level the Crystal has opened a door it cannot yet fill. The Stages of one rank hold close to its floor, so a soul that has crossed into a new rank soon has the Level for the next Stage or two of it.

Buying is the door and nothing more. A breakthrough Draft or a blessing will supply any Catalyst on the ladder, and the Crystal goes to the melt and comes out a Stage higher, in a quiet room, with nobody for miles the wiser. The Level is still owed. So is the Mastery, and so is every Release. A purse can open the door. Whatever comes after it has to be lived.

Overgrown. A Stage held under its Level leaves the Crystal Overgrown. Power spills out of it with nowhere to go: lamps lean toward the person, tempers stain the air around them, their Mastery reads low or not at all, and no Release will come, whatever they do, until Level catches up. It makes no difference whether the Stage was bought by a great house or met early by a prodigy who walked into the right disaster at fourteen. The heir and the prodigy are the same case, and the halls can spot either one from across a yard. Leofric Stanmere was Overgrown, and every lamp at Harl Cross said so before he drew.

Residual Strain. The opposite case is worse to stand beside. A soul may climb a long way in Level inside its rank, and that is ordinary: most fighters grind up toward the top of their group for years, and nobody thinks the worse of them. The trouble begins at the old gates. When Level passes the top of the soul’s group with no Catalyst to the next Stage, the pressure builds in the Crystal with nowhere to go:

The veteran stuck at the gate is the strange one, and the dangerous one. The Strain shows in the room: water that will not lie flat, dogs that will not settle, a cold or a heat the season does not explain. Held long enough, it can fracture the Crystal. It can also break a seal that was never broken, and this is the most important thing the Archives have learned about it: Strain can wake the unwoken. A commoner who has lived hard enough for long enough builds pressure in a sealed soul until something cracks it open, and late wakings, in a widow of fifty or a drover of sixty, are a known thing in every quarter, and are not always a happy one.

Any real work moves Level. Danger overcome moves it most, and beating something stronger than you most of all, which is why crews still argue over who landed the last blow. But a flood night held moves it, and a plague nursed through, and fifty years at a trade. A baker can stand at Level 140 and never have been in danger in her life. There is one Level, and a lifetime’s work counts toward everything Level is read for once the soul wakes. Elswyth Ashby’s Level was flood nights and boathooks, and the Crystal did not care that none of it had been done with a sword.

Mastery: How Far the Reach Runs

Every soul works its Path’s own substance, and Mastery is how well it does. There is one rung for each Path a person walks, numbered I to V, with Ω above them for the very few. Nobody tracks it. The Stage sets how high it may go, and the Level says where under that ceiling the soul stands.

The ceilings. Below Glory, Mastery is not read at all, and the soul works its road by blows, the coat and the Stage’s own figure. The roof then rises a rung at a time up the whole climb, so that the numeral means something at every height: at Glory the ceiling is I; at Refraction, II; at Transcendence and Invocation, III; at Realization and Dissonance, IV; from Emanation through Zenith, V; and Ω only at Revelation and Apex.

The early grip. There is one exception to the silence below Glory. A soul born to one of the Epochs’ lines and holding that line’s instrument (a Moto with a Work of Hataraki, a Zettari with Matla, a Yukari with Null-Light) reads rung I on that instrument’s road from Ascension, the third Stage. The blood opens one ladder early, and the assay houses keep a separate book for those children.

The rungs by Level. A soul reads I from Glory’s hold at Level 115, II from 150, III from 215, IV from 260 and V from 340, each no higher than its Stage’s ceiling allows. A soul fresh across a Stage therefore reads a rung under its new roof until its Level grows into the Stage: a new Refraction soul at 130 reads I and reaches II at 150, a new Transcendent at 201 reads II and reaches III at 215, a new Realization soul at 230 reads III and reaches IV at 260, and a new Emanation soul at 320 reads IV and reaches V at 340.

Shown above the rung. Mastery is read from Level for everyone, and the Archives keep no list of people who stand above it. When a soul is seen working past the rung its Level gives, that was an overreach: done once, in that moment, at a price, and the record stands as the night they reached past themselves, never as a rung they hold.

The folk names. The card says Body IV, and so does the assay slip. The people of each road keep their own word for the rung, from the ladders the halls used to chalk:

A hitter in a Hallenfeld tavern will tell you she is Luminous, and a mender in a Sum-gol temple will tell you the old woman upstairs is a Shepherd, and both mean the fourth rung.

Inverted. Any rung can be held Inverted, a feared and wrong way of working it, in which the soul takes where it ought to give and empties where it ought to fill. It is a mark beside the rung, never a rung of its own: Spirit IV Inverted. The halls do not teach it. Some people arrive at it anyway, and the ones who do are written about.

Fate’s grip. Fate climbs with Level like the other two roads, with one hard limit: without a grip on the thread, a Fate soul stops at II, however long it lives. A grip is a Work or a blessing. Blood alone is not one, and the cadet lines of the great Fate houses, who have the blood and were never given a Work, stop at II like anyone else and are bitter about it in every generation. A grip lifts the ceiling to the Stage’s own, and a blessing that grants one lifts it that far and no further. Fate’s yield reads from the same table as everyone else’s. Its price is the knot, which the world collects later, as The Tell and the Knot explains.

Two Paths. A Path on the card is Mastered, Walked or Studied. A Walked Path, thirty parts in a hundred of the practice or more, opens its gates a Stage late and reads a rung behind the lead until it is mastered. A Studied Path reads two rungs behind and stops at II. All of it reads from Level like the rest, and The Three Paths gives the gates.

No Letter on a Person

The halls used to give people letters. They have stopped, and the Archives were glad to see it go.

Grades are for things. A sword has a Grade and a Rarity, and an Appraiser will tell you both for a fee. So does a crossbow bolt, a ballista shaft, a beast and a bounty. A person has none, aloud or anywhere else. Stanmere’s sword was Grade A. Stanmere was an Expert who had bought his way there, and that is all anyone at the Cross now says of him.

The card keeps the numbers. Every one of a person’s sixty-four Sub-Stats is still on the card, with the points spent on them, and each of the eight Primaries is their total. When two people contest a thing, the numbers are compared under the Stage-gap rules, and the assayers keep the old scale underneath for their arithmetic: a stat a full step ahead on it wins its exchange outright, and a self-boost, whatever its source, stacks no more than two steps over what the card can sustain. Only outside help goes past that. Nobody says the scale aloud of a person, and a clerk who forgets is not thanked for it.

Gear multiplies a whole Primary, all eight of its Sub-Stats together, and all the gear a person wears together lifts any one Primary by half again at the most. A piece above its wearer’s Stage works at half, or fights them. The piece itself still fights at its own Grade and Rarity.

Beasts keep a Stage, a Level and a lean toward one Path underneath, the same as anyone, and are known and bountied by Grade. The Accord says which title may take which Grade of bounty, and every board from the coast to the passes posts the Grade beside the price. The beasts themselves are in The Bestiary.

Pressure on the unwoken reads by the fighter’s rank. An Initiate or an Apprentice brings dread into a room. A Journeyman brings weight, so that people feel it in their shoulders. An Expert bends their knees, as the cheap stands at Harl Cross found out.

The Hands

Skills read as the nine ranks: Initiate, Apprentice, Journeyman, Adept, Expert, Master, Grandmaster, Archmaster, Paragon, the same words as a Stage’s rank and an Accord title. “A Journeyman by Stage and a Master at the anvil” means exactly what it says, and a good deal of the world’s trouble comes from people who only heard the first half.

Trades go anywhere. A trade skill can reach Paragon with no Stage behind it at all. There is a bread-oven in Stannvaard that has been a pilgrimage for forty years, kept by a woman who never woke, and her Baking reads Paragon.

Combat is capped by Stage. A fighting skill cannot rank above its owner’s own Stage rank: a Master’s sword stops at Master, and an Expert’s at Expert.

A trade skill still feeds the Body road. At Journeyman or better with a material, a Body fighter works one rung further into that material than their Mastery would otherwise reach, and never past their ceiling. The mason who fights is a different animal from the soldier who does.

Techniques jump when two things happen together: their skill gains a rank, and a fight pushes them past their old limit. A jump gives one of four things, power, reach, speed or a lower cost, and a bigger name, and the old name stays in use.

Traits have no levels. A Trait reads at the lead Path’s Mastery, and its Release and Domain are the same idea escalating. Below Glory, where Mastery is not read, a Trait works at its base. Skills and Trait Levels holds the older account.

Class: The Name the Soul Offers

A Class is a name, never a number, that the soul offers for how the person has lived. It is first offered at Glory, with Mastery I, and it evolves when the lead Mastery rises. Each evolution brings one move that fits the new name and a lean on where life will spend the person’s stat points from then on. The one wild idea at the heart of a person’s power, their Trait, stays their own through all of it. Bess Tolliver of Threeways, who was Pole-Hand and Riverblade and Floodbreaker before she was anything worse, is the story everyone tells, and Classes and the Three Ladders tells it.

Leofric Stanmere, at Level 74 and Glory, had been offered nothing. His Mastery did not read. The soul offers a name for a life, and he had not had one yet.

Releases, by Path

A Release is the soul’s own law let off its limit in a shape found and mastered by fighting, and the Path decides how many a soul can have. Each one is deeper than the last, and each has its own Catalyst.

  • The Body has three. The First comes of the body broken, refusing to fall: beaten past what it can take, by something that means it, and still standing. The Second comes of a material known so well it obeys at range: one stone or steel or water read to the grain until it answers across a yard of air. The Third comes of the body remade around a wound: a new wound, taken in the fight that asks for it, with the Wellspring helping to build the body again around the break.
  • The Spirit has two. The First comes of the self said aloud, and true, declared to the face of someone who denies it. The Second comes of a meaning borne for someone else, the sin-eater’s office: a grief or a guilt that another has set down, carried whole.
  • Fate has one, the One, at Refraction, and it comes of a claim staked on the soul’s own life, and kept: a claim over something outside the self, the claimant’s life forfeit if it fails, held through a whole contest. A claim kept by pulling a thread does not count, because a pull is not a claim. The One is deeper than any First and counts as a Second: it opens the Domain and powers it.

Every Release Catalyst counts only from the Release’s own Stage, must be met against something that means it, and cannot be bought. If a deed would answer both a Release and the next Stage, the Stage takes it. One met while the Crystal is Overgrown prints on the stone and stays locked until Level holds.

Two roads, one count. A soul walking two Paths has the Releases of its lead Path, and no more. The Walked road colors them, so that a Body soul who walks the Spirit road throws a First Release with meaning in it, and it brings none of its own.

Domains stand as they always have. A seed may flicker once at Refraction as the Catalyst that carries a soul into that Stage. Anyone at Transcendence may have a Domain. Only a Second Release, or Fate’s One, can call one up and hold it. Domains has the whole of it.

The Realm is the far end of the climb, and every road reaches it late, by its own door. The crossing into Invocation still carries a first flicker of one, once, on every road. The door itself opens no earlier than Realization, and never for a bought Stage:

A Body Realm cannot grip, so whatever lifts or pulls away parts it. A Spirit Realm gives nothing to hold to what means nothing. A Fate Realm falls the moment its holder breaks a word of their own. Release Forms and Releases and Domains by Path give the forms in full.

Drafts, Blessings, Sects and the Accord

Drafts. A tempering Draft gives Level, at the risk of Identity Contamination, which is what happens when a dead monster’s core goes into a living soul and some of the monster comes with it. A breakthrough Draft gives a Catalyst. A fight Draft refills the reserve and leaves a crash that pays for it. All of them are priced about threefold a Stage, and counterfeits are common. The Ladder and the Draft has the trade.

Blessings. A blessing can grant Level, a Catalyst, or both. A Stage blessed in above its Level runs Overgrown like any other.

Sects teach the road to the Catalysts. They put their students into the danger and the truths that meet the Stage and Release Catalysts, teach their techniques and their style, and keep the lore of which lens and which Path suit whom. It is the one thing in the climb a purse cannot buy outright, and the sects know precisely what they are selling when a duke’s daughter turns up at the gate at Glory asking to be hit.

The Accord. Accord titles are earned by commissions. A fighting guild also asks the title’s own rank in Stage, so a Journeyman’s title needs Stage III behind it. A trade guild asks none.

How a Card Reads Now

An assay slip, or a card in the Archives, carries the reckoning in two lines and a third for the roads:

Stage VIII Transcendence · Master (Fire) Level 238 · Refined · Floodbreaker Body VIII Mastered, III · Spirit VII Walked 35%, II · Lenses: home + 2

The first line is the Stage, its rank and the Crystal’s look. The second is the Level, the Crystal’s state (Refined when it is sound, Overgrown when it spills, Fractured when it has broken) and the Class. The third is each Path the soul walks, with how far that Path has opened, how it is held and its Mastery, and last the lenses it carries.

Old Words

Old books and old people use a good many words the reckoning above has set aside. They are worth knowing, because most of the world still says them. Where an old card in the Archives reads otherwise than this page, the reckoning wins. Whatever its holder was seen to do still happened, and where it ran past the rung their Level gives, the Archives file it as an overreach on the night it was done.

  • Tier of Standing. The older registers numbered the nine ranks as Tiers, so that a Master was a “Tier Six” on a hall token. The number is gone and the word is kept.
  • The Bands. Mortal Foundation, Awakened, Sovereign, Mythic and Absolute were five blocks of a hundred Levels, each with a gate. People still call a Master-rank fighter “a Sovereign” in the North. The edges of the old Bands survive as the hold Levels of Splintering, Transcendence, Dissonance and Principality, 101, 201, 301 and 401, and the old gates are where Residual Strain begins.
  • The class ladders. Aether Class, Essence Class and Thread Class were three ladders, one to each layer of the Crystal, that the coils used to read. They are Mastery now, and their rung names are the folk names in the table above. Their sixth rungs, Voidic, Hollow and Severer, were the old way of saying Inverted.
  • The Clear Crystal. The jewelers once had a Clear look between Faceted and Fire. A Refraction soul reads Faceted now, and an old slip that says Clear means the same.
  • Trait levels. A Trait used to carry a level of its own. Now it reads at the lead Path’s Mastery, and an old card’s Trait level is a guess at the same thing.
  • The T-tiers. Beasts were once graded T1 to T9. The bounty boards dropped the tiers and post the Grade, and old hunters still say “a T5” of anything that killed someone they knew.
  • The craft ranks. Skills were once read on six craft ranks of their own, Novice, Apprentice, Journeyman, Master, Grandmaster and Legend, and before that in the fighters’ letters. They read on the nine ranks now, and an old letter crosses plainly: Hollow, F and E are Initiate, D Apprentice, C Journeyman, B Adept, A Expert, S and SS Master, SSS Grandmaster, X and EX Archmaster, and EX+ Paragon. Old masters still call a lifetime’s craft “a Legend’s hand”.
  • A person’s Grade. “An A-Grade fighter” was ordinary talk a generation ago and is still heard in the dockside rings. The letter belongs to the sword now. The scale under it is still there, in the assayers’ arithmetic, where nobody has to hear it.
