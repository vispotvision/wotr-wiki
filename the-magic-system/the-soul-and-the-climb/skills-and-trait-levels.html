Skills and Traits

Skills and Traits

Nov 28, 202623 min read

“Fifty years at one thing will make anyone frightening. It will not, as a rule, make them alive afterward.”

Guild surgeon’s note, Tollgarth, in the margin of an invoice

Marta Kell had kept the block on Tollgarth’s Tripe Row for fifty-one years, under a striped red awning, with sausages in ropes overhead and a yellow cat on the scale. She was a wide old woman in a wet apron who had never woken a Crystal in her life. Her window held a Level you could count on your fingers and one line that made Measurewrights take their hats off. When Captain Gorr Ashtooth of the Iron Mandate came down the Row collecting for his company, a Stage V hobgoblin in a black coat and a scale shirt, carrying a mace the size of a churn, he told her the levy had doubled. He leaned across her block to take a ham by way of first installment, and he did not bother to run his aura down that arm, since she was a fat old butcher and he had done this on four hundred streets.

"Hold still, love."

She had a boning knife with a blade worn thin as a willow leaf and the arm of a woman of seventy, and she brought nothing to that moment you could put on a force table. Her Ardency read nothing worth writing down. She had also taken apart something over two hundred thousand animals, and her hand knew where a shoulder comes undone the way your tongue knows your teeth. The knife went in through the gap at the armhole of the scale shirt, along the top of the arm bone, and round the ball of the joint, where the capsule (the sleeve of gristle that holds a joint together) can be opened by anyone who knows its seam with no more force than it takes to joint a chicken. It took her about as long as it took him to look surprised. Then the arm slid off onto her block, ham and all, with the mace still in its fist, and the blood came out of him in a hot red rope that went clear across the Row and wet the sausages.

He hit her with the other hand. It went through the awning post, the back wall and the wall of the tannery behind that, with her in front of it.

She lost. She lost the fight, the shop, four teeth and the use of a hip. Captain Ashtooth, coat flaring to the socket and white as lard, walked to the end of the Row on his own feet and has been Ashtooth Red-Sleeve in every song from there to Undaar-Gorr. And her window, when she could read it, in a bed at the guild surgeon’s, went like this:

[Hit: left hip broken. Four teeth gone. Ribs uncounted. Health 31%]
[Level: 9 to 23]
[Skill: Butchery, Paragon, 97%]

She keeps a new stall now on the same Row. The Iron Mandate’s collectors no longer walk it.

Everything about that afternoon sits on this page. A trade skill can go as high as the ranks go with no Stage under it, and it can do things force cannot. It still leaves you standing in front of a Stage V with a boning knife, and the rank decides how well you do what you do, while the stats decide what happens next. How the whole climb fits together, Stage, Level and the hands, is set out in How Power Grows.

The Things That Rise

Five things are counted over a life. Four of them rise by roads of their own, the fifth rides on another, and the window reports each differently.

What risesBy whatShown asHas a ceiling
LevelAny real work, danger overcome most of allA number, 1 to 500 and past it at Paragon500 below Stage XV; Residual Strain past the old gates without a Catalyst
StatsMostly what your life spends, by what your lead layer does, a few placed by you at each StageNumbers, never a letter; a Sub-Stat when used or when it movesThe Stage’s ceiling

TraitNothing of its own: it reads at the lead Path’s MasteryNo number at allThe Mastery ceiling of the Stage
TechniqueIts skill gaining a rank, and a fight or a scene that pushes it past its old limitNo level at all; a bigger name when it jumpsNone

The class has its own page, and it hands you skills and shapes moves on top of all this.

Skills

A skill is one thing a person does and has got better at by doing it. Swordwork, haggling, tracking, brewing Drafts, cutting runes, folding tags, chanting, laying out the dead, lambing. Every practitioner has them, and so does everybody else, since a baker has a Baking skill and a little sheet. Anyone can level most of them, and everyone can pour or bake; cutting a rune, folding a tag and chanting want a woken Crystal. There is no gate of birth or Stage on picking up a skill beyond that, which has made a great many teachers unhappy.

A skill rises with use and shows on the sheet on the nine ranks: Initiate, Apprentice, Journeyman, Adept, Expert, Master, Grandmaster, Archmaster, Paragon, the same words a Stage’s rank and an Accord title use. Between ranks the window gives a percentage when something moves it. Work done under real weight counts for more than work done idly, the way danger moves Level: a hundred sparring cuts shift a Swordwork line a little, and one cut that decides whether you see supper shifts it a good deal more, which is why sect elders put their students in real danger and call it teaching.

[Skill rising: Footwork, Journeyman, 91%]

[Skill: Measure, Initiate to Apprentice]

The names are the soul’s and not yours. Your window files what you actually do, in your own tongue, and it does not flatter. A shepherd is told he has Lambing, Shearing and Laying-Out, the washing and dressing of the dead, because he buried his own in a hard winter. A veteran finds Bearing Blows on the sheet above his Swordwork, and a duke’s son finds Obedience, and is surprised how high it has climbed.

What a sheet of them looks like

A normal look gives you the skills in play. A long look gives you all of them, and most people’s are longer than they would have guessed.

An adventurer on an Accord commission, thirty-one and doing well:

[Skills]
[Hammerwork Expert · Wet Footing Journeyman · Tracking Journeyman]
[Bearing Blows Journeyman · Bargaining Apprentice · Knots Apprentice]
[Riding Journeyman · Field Dressing Apprentice · Reading a Bill Journeyman]

A Draft-pourer in a sect’s hall, who has fought twice in her life, and badly:

[Skills]
[Pouring Master · Rendering Journeyman · Herb-Lore Journeyman · Ledger Journeyman]
[Bottle-Cutting Apprentice · Sums Journeyman · Deference Journeyman]
[Knife Initiate]

A second son of a great house, Stage VIII by his family’s purse:

[Skills]
[Riding Journeyman · Courtly Address Journeyman · Dancing Journeyman · Fencing, Salon Apprentice]
[Obedience Journeyman · Dice Master]

Marta Kell’s, before Tripe Row got interesting:

[Level 9 · Health: sound]
[Skills: Butchery Paragon · Sums Apprentice · Curing Master · Haggling Journeyman]

What a skill does and does not do

A skill’s rank says how well the thing gets done. It puts no force behind it. The stat in use decides a contest, by its numbers, and the force tables on Grades, Ranks and the Aether Shell read stats. Brawling at Journeyman behind an arm with nothing in it is a beautifully placed punch from someone who cannot hurt you. Hammerwork at Apprentice behind an Ardency the assayers whistle at is a barn door falling on the wrong house.

Where skill earns its keep is everywhere the stats tie and everywhere they are not asked. Where two fighters’ numbers sit within a step of each other, exchanges are settled by technique quality, tactics and position, and a Master by Stage who is a Master with the sword makes a Master who never drilled look like a child. It also decides what can be built: techniques are named things done with skills, so an art built on a skill you do not have is a shout and a surgeon’s bill.

Fifty years at one thing can make a Paragon of a trade with no Stage to speak of. The Stage’s ceiling binds Sub-Stats and caps the fighting skills, and it has never bound a trade. These mundane masters are real, they are rare, and they are frightening: a mason who can tell by leaning on it which stone in a keep comes out first, or a barber who has shaved a duke every morning for forty years and has never once been searched. They usually still lose a fight. Sensible lords keep them fed and on the right side of the wall.

The ranks, read by ear. Nothing on file fixes how long a rank takes, and a person with a good teacher and a bad year goes faster. A rough guide, as the guild halls talk about it:

RankReads as
InitiateYou have started and someone has noticed
ApprenticeUseful at home, and then someone would pay you for it
JourneymanA living, in a town with some competition
AdeptThe name people give when asked who does this round here
ExpertKnown across a province, and you have opinions about the others
MasterStudents travel to you
GrandmasterRivals write letters
ArchmasterYour work is argued over in halls you have never visited
ParagonA lifetime with nothing else in it; fifty years will do it. The Archives list three claims to something past it, and the three claimants disagree

The older sheets gave skills the fighters’ letters, and a later generation read them on six craft ranks of their own, Novice, Apprentice, Journeyman, Master, Grandmaster and Legend, now retired. An old letter reads across plainly: Hollow, F and E are an Initiate, D an Apprentice, C a Journeyman, B an Adept, A an Expert, S and SS a Master, SSS a Grandmaster, X and EX an Archmaster, and EX+ a Paragon.

The cap on fighting skills. A trade is free to climb as high as a life will take it, to Paragon with no Stage behind it. A fighting skill is not: the hands cannot learn to fight past what the soul behind them can carry, so a combat skill never ranks above its owner’s own Stage rank. A Master’s sword stops at Master, and an Expert’s at Expert.

Skills and trades

Drafts, runes, tags and chants are skills and trades like any other, open to anyone who will put in the years, though all but the pouring want a woken Crystal. Any soldier may learn to pour. Any woken pourer may learn to cut a seal. A trade is what happens when someone does a skill well enough for money, and a guild is what happens when enough of them would like to keep the money. The old quarrels between the pourers’ halls and the cutters’ halls are guild feuds now, and they are carried on with the same vigor as ever.

A skill is what you can do, and a title is what you are trusted to do. The nine guild titles, Initiate through Paragon, grade trust in a trade. A smith’s title caps the Grade of work she may lawfully make, and a merchant’s caps the Rarity he may sell. The two drift apart all the time, and the gap is the business of half the market. A Paragon of the anvil with a Journeyman’s token sells under the counter. A Grandmaster’s son who is a Journeyman at Smithing has apprentices who hide their work from him. See The Standing and the Title.

Trade skills on the Body Path

A trade skill is knowledge of a material, and the Body Path is the working of materials, so the one feeds the other. The schools teach the Body road as the Material Force, a ladder of five rungs: matter commanded as an object, then through contact, then through recognition, then through law, and at the far end as a whole system. A soul’s Body Mastery says which rung a fighter stands on. On a material that a Body fighter’s trade skill knows at Journeyman or better, they work one rung up, so that contact reaches recognition on that material and no other. A smith reads steel deeper than her Shell has any right to, a mason reads stone, and a butcher reads joints. It never carries anyone past the ceiling their Stage sets for Mastery, so a fighter already at the ceiling gets nothing from it but a neater cut.

Marta Kell is the case, and she shows which half of it needs no Crystal. Her Shell was sealed and held no Charge, so she worked no Aether on Tripe Row and had no rung to stand on. What she had was the knowing, at Paragon, and a hand that recognized a shoulder so completely that a thin knife and an old woman’s arm were enough to open it. Her sister’s grandson, Pell Kell, did eleven years at her block before a company took him, and his window reads Butchery at Journeyman. He is a Stage VII on the Body Path whose Shell reads Body II, a rung short of what his Stage allows until his Level reaches 185, so against a wall or a breastplate he must lay a hand on a thing to command it, like anyone on the second rung. On meat and joint he works at recognition. He does not need to find the seam of a shoulder, because he knows it across a yard the way his great-aunt knew it under her thumb, and the men who have sparred with him say it is like fighting someone who has already read the bill of fare. The Iron Mandate now asks recruits what their fathers did for a living, and writes the answer down.

When a technique jumps

Techniques show no level. A technique is a named thing done with a skill, and its strength is that skill’s rank, shaped by the Trait under it: the Trait sets what the technique can do, and the skill sets how well it is done. A fire-law under Swordwork at Journeyman is a competent burning cut. The same law under Swordwork at Master is the same cut, placed where nothing can be done about it.

A technique jumps when two things have both happened. The skill it runs on gains a rank, and a fight or a scene pushes the technique past its old limit. Neither is enough alone. A rank gained in the practice yard leaves every name on the sheet where it was, and a desperate night with no new rank under it is only a desperate night. Sect elders know this, which is the other reason they put students in danger.

A jump gives exactly one of four things: power, reach, speed or a lower cost. It may also add a condition, and the larger jumps usually do. The technique takes a bigger name, and the old name stays in use for the smaller version, which is why a veteran’s sheet reads like a family tree. Dob Harrow, a ferry-guard on the Ward river, threw Short Right Hand for nine years. His Brawling went from Apprentice to Journeyman in the spring, and that summer a river pirate stood a full stride outside his arm with a boathook. The window notes it and moves on:

[Skill: Brawling, Apprentice to Journeyman]
[Short Right Hand is now THE RIGHT HAND OF THE RIVER WARD]
[Gained: reach · Condition: both feet planted]
[Short Right Hand: kept]

Traits

A Trait is a standing law a soul carries, formed by what it survived, and The Trait System gives the whole of its law: the Four Reflections, the four offices and the ways it goes wrong. All of that stands. What this section adds is how loud it runs, and the answer is that a Trait has no number of its own. It reads at the Mastery of its bearer’s lead Path, so it grows as that Mastery grows, and its Release and Domain are the same law escalating. Ibbet Lowe’s Trait kept a street’s linen clean for forty-one years, and on one afternoon it took all the blood out of a knight in a white surcoat, and those were the same law at two amplitudes.

[Trait: It Comes Up White]
[Reads at: its base]

How it grows. Below Glory, where Mastery is not read, a Trait works at its base, and its base can be a frightening thing in a soul that has lived hard. From Glory it reads at the lead road’s rung, and it climbs when that rung does. Four things feed the climb, all of them by way of Level and the hands:

  • Use. Lean on the law and the life piles up, and the life is what Level counts. A Body fighter’s Trait is driven out through the Shell, a Spirit worker’s worked through the Core on souls and meanings, a Fate worker’s on bonds, oaths and odds, and all of it is real work.
  • The rest of the climb. A fighter who has spent thirty years leaning on one law carries it far higher than a prodigy who has merely had it longer, because thirty years of leaning is a great deal of Level, and the skill the law runs through has had thirty years to rank.
  • Refinement. A tempering course feeds Level, Salts for a law that runs through the Shell and Core stock for one that runs through the Core, and a teacher can show a practitioner where the law has more room than they have been giving it.
  • A Threshold. Tempering melts the Crystal down and rebuilds each layer, the lead layer first, and a Stage that lifts the Mastery ceiling lifts the Trait’s with it.

The mark a growing Trait leaves on the body follows the Path as well, dense and warm on the Body road, light and still on the Spirit, and next to nothing on Fate, where it shows in the street around the bearer. The Trait System has it under What a Trait Does to the Body.

[Level: 214 to 215]
[Spirit: III]
[Trait: Look Twice, now read at III]

[STAGE VII: REFRACTION]
[Bank the Fire: ceiling lifts to II]

What a rung buys. Each rung presses harder on the Trait’s four offices: a steeper Bias on the stats it touches, more that it Permits, a stronger seed in the melt and a higher Ceiling. It also buys a heavier Rigidity Effect, since the more absolute a law becomes, the less room its bearer has to contradict it. A Trait read at the fifth rung is a magnificent thing to have and an awful thing to disagree with.

How the Bias shows on the sheet. It depends on whether the law is always running. An always-on Trait shifts the stat itself, and the window simply shows the shifted figure, with no mark on it to say where the number came from. A porter whose Trait is Carries What Is Given reads Fortitude 210 every hour of his life, and only an assay with the Trait damped would show the 160 underneath. A situational Trait shows conditionally, with the plain figure first and the lifted one after it, and the condition named. Ibbet Lowe’s law runs only while there is something to be washed out:

[Absorption 140 · 190 while It Comes Up White holds]

The line changes the moment the condition does, and a fighter who has been leaning on the second figure learns a good deal from watching it go back to the first. Both kinds count on the two-step cap on boosts, so a soul whose Trait already lifts a stat a full step on the assayers’ scale has one step of room left there for a vow, a Release or a Draft.

Before waking. The great majority of the world’s Traits sit in bodies that will never be screened, refining by the Weathering, the slow hardening along the way a soul is used. A commoner’s window shows only Level, trade skills and health, so the law sits unlisted and goes on working. Ibbet Lowe’s window read Washing and nothing else for forty-one years. Waking opens the rest, and a Trait turns up already worn deep into the soul, sometimes far deeper than anyone in the room expected, to the alarm of everyone in it.

Monsters have them too. A Moor Tyrant’s Pack Hunter reads at the beast’s own Mastery like anybody else’s, and it is usually higher than you would like. See The Bestiary.

The older sheets gave every Trait a level of its own, a number that rose with use, and an old card’s Trait level is a guess at the same thing the lead road’s Mastery now says plainly.

Stat Points, and Placing Them at a Stage

Life spends most of your stat points. A few are yours. Every Level brings an allotment, and every Stage’s Threshold (the crisis that refines a soul to its next rung) brings a larger one. Most of it lands where the pressure went, by what you actually do, which for a practitioner means by what the lead layer does, and a farrier’s points do not wander into Feint because he would have liked them to. That is why a cobbler’s son and a duke’s son at the same Level read so differently, and why a sheet read properly is a biography.

The law, unchanged from Fracture of Worlds, Part Three. Points go on Sub-Stats only. A Primary is the total of its eight Sub-Stats, and the assayers read its mean (Dominion’s over seven, since Throne stands outside its total).

LevelsPoints per LevelConcentration cap per Sub-Stat, per level-up
1 to 100125

201 to 300188

A Threshold grants the Stage number multiplied by 100 in points, so Stage VI awards 600 and Stage XIII awards 1,300, landing on the same terms. No Sub-Stat may pass the ceiling of the current Stage. Points refused by a ceiling are not lost: they sit as pressure until a Threshold enlarges the vessel.

The road leans the grant. A soul’s Path is the layer it leans on, the lead layer does most of the work in a life, and so it does most of the spending. Level by Level the points follow what that layer was doing. At a Threshold the lean is plainer still, because the melt rebuilds the lead layer first and the large grant lands while it is hottest.

Lead PathThe Threshold grant leans towardBecause
BodyArdency, Vitality, Dexterity and TemperingThe Shell hits, bears, moves and holds its Charge
SpiritGnosis and HarmonicsThe Core reads, and tunes itself to what it works
FateDominion and ResilienceThe Layer claims, and keeps its own

It is a lean and no more. The points still go on Sub-Stats, the caps and ceilings stand, and a life lived against its road will drag the grant after it: a Spirit worker who has spent a Stage being hit comes out of the melt with more Vitality than her tutors ordered. A soul on two Paths takes the lean of its lead Path, and the second road gets what its share of the practice has earned.

A farrier’s Level. Odd Brannick shoes carthorses at Ostler’s Cross, and the week a gray mare put him through the side of his own forge he went from Level 14 to Level 15. That is under Level 100, so twelve points, and his life spent all twelve without asking him. Five went on Fortitude, which is the most one Sub-Stat can take from a single Level that low, because he had just been kicked through a wall. Four went on Grapple, for nineteen years of holding up the hind leg of something that did not want it held. Three went on Threshold, for the kick. Nothing went to Feint, or to anything else he might have chosen, and he will not be asked for an opinion until a Stage gives him a bar to place.

What the owner sees. None of that arithmetic appears on a window. Your soul tells you which Sub-Stats moved, and it gives you what is left to place as a bar:

[Threshold passed: Stage VI, Glory]
[Your life has spent it: Fortitude, Threshold, Flux]
[Points to place: ▓▓░░░░░░░░]
[Place where?]

You place by thinking it. The window answers with whichever Sub-Stat moved:

[Reflex: rising]
[Points to place: ▓░░░░░░░░░]

[Ward: at ceiling. Refused. Held as pressure.]

Between Stages it has nothing to say on the subject. Jory the gaff-man’s window said it flatly the morning after his first real fight: Points to place: none until your next Stage.

The placing is the whole of the owner’s say, so people argue about it for years. The handful you steer is small beside what your life has spent, and it is the only part you get to aim. The main schools of thought, shouted in any sect refectory:

  • Feed the peak. Put it where you already fight. A contested exchange is decided by the Sub-Stat in use, and a full step ahead on the assayers’ scale wins it outright, so the next step on your best Sub-Stat wins fights. This is what most fighters do and what most crews want them to do.
  • Patch the hole. Your life will never spend on what it never does, so the placed points are your one way to fix what it left out. A brawler with a Ward the assayers would not bother writing down dies the first time he meets a mind-worker. Old teachers who have buried students say this one.
  • Pay the register. Institutions measure the mean and fights are decided by the peak, so a point in a neglected Sub-Stat lifts the Primary an assessor prints. A house with a marriage to arrange or a title to secure will instruct its heirs accordingly, and the heir will look magnificent on paper until he meets someone who has read that paper.
  • Save the gate. The Path gates and the Resonant Pairs ask for a figure on particular Sub-Stats. Someone who knows where they mean to go can place toward a door years ahead. Most people who think they know this are wrong.

A bought Stage pays out too, and the Threshold points land on the same terms, most of them where the life has been spending itself, leaning toward the lead Path’s Primaries like any other grant. A second son who takes Transcendence in a quiet room receives a very fine helping of Finesse, Economy and whatever his years of obedience have trained, with his bar to place on top of it. This is what the family’s tutors are for, and why the great houses employ men whose entire job is to stand behind an heir at the moment of a Draft and tell him where to put it.

Race and lineage set where you start and what comes easily: they show on the sheet and shape base stats, so the same placed point lands on a different number for a Marrowkin and a Serpentkin. They are tendencies you can argue with, at some length, for a lifetime.
