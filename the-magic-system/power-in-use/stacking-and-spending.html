Stacking and Spending

Stacking and Spending

Nov 17, 202616 min read

How power-ups add together, and how a practitioner’s reserve shows as it runs down. For the whole system in brief, see How Magic Works.

“Two layers is a gamble veterans take. Four is a story told about the dead.” The Guild’s own rule of thumb, and Cullen Fair’s favorite thing to shout at it.

Sigrun Ox-Hand told the whole of Cullen Fair that she would lift the miller’s ox with the cart still hitched to it and the miller still in the cart, and the fair gave forty to one against. She was Stage V (fifth on the soul’s sixteen-step ladder), a drover’s daughter tempered on Pyreveil, a Wellspring of heat, and half the county had come to see her lose. She gathered her coat (her own aura, laid over the body) into her shoulders until they glowed a smoky ember-orange through her shirt. She swore aloud, with the priest as witness, that she would not set that ox down until the sun went behind the church, and she had picked her moment, since the sun was already sitting on the roof ridge and a sun takes about four minutes to get behind a roof. She drank a lifting Draft (a bottled working that pushes a stat) with her teeth and spat the cork at the bookie. Then she got under the axle and bawled HEARTH-BACK at the sky, a surge within the band for one beat’s cost, and up it all came, ox, cart, miller and the miller’s opinions, with half the county howling and the bookie sitting down in a puddle.

That was four layers. For the first minute the crowd cheered the steam coming off her. In the second the blood started, from the nose, from the corners of the eyes, and with the last red sliver of the sun still showing the orange began to go, bright lines running through it like the crazing on an old glaze, and everyone in the crowd with a day’s training had stopped cheering and started shouting at the sun. She held it to the minute, because a vow is a vow.

They heard the aura go at the far end of the horse lines. The ox, I am pleased to report, landed well. She lived, she collected six hundred gold at her bedside from a very quiet man, and for the rest of her days she could not light a pipe without her hand jumping. Asked whether it had been worth it, she bought the fair.

Everything she did is on this page, in order.

What it is

A practitioner’s own power-ups stack on one another, up to a hard cap of two Grades (letter bands of force, F through EX+) above the sustainable Grade on their registered card, the level their Soul Crystal (the soul itself, which stores and sends out their power) can hold safely. That Grade is read bare, with every worked piece off and dark; gear multiplies on top of it under a limit of its own, and the two Grades are counted from the bare letter. A boost held for the moment may pass the Stage’s own ceiling lawfully inside that cap; only a lasting forced stat takes the fracture rate the tables give for it. A Grade held up for years by a blessing is the exception that proves the rule, because the giver carries the load until they let go, and on the day they let go it lands on you. Every layer the Crystal runs at once adds risk of a fracture, and every second of it spends reserve (the one store of power a soul carries, held as Charge by a Body fighter and as Essence by a Spirit one or a Fate one, since a Fate soul refines what it draws exactly as a Spirit soul does and pays its pulls out of that, Thread being pulled per working and never stored), which the body shows on a fixed ladder of tells.

The stack line. Every registered card (the record a guild or assay house keeps on a practitioner) carries one: each layer the person can run, what it adds, and where they reach the cap. An assessor reads it the way an engine-master reads the marks on a trebuchet’s arm. A Stage bought with Drafts or given by a blessing comes with a gap in that line, because a bought Stage brings no Release, so a rich heir at Stage VII may have a whole rung of the table above he cannot yet run. Assessors look for that gap first and say so loudly at dinner.

How it looks

Running low. The body tells on the reserve, and the ladder is the same for everyone:

Reserve leftThe tells
HalfThe breath shortens and stays short between exchanges. The aura dims, as if a hand had cupped the lamp.
A quarterA tremor in the hands and the knees. The color thins: the hue washes toward gray and the edges of the aura fray.
A tenthThe aura flickers on and off like a lamp on its last spoonful of oil. Traits (the soul’s standing laws) stutter: a working comes out late, or half, or not at all. Below this is Starvation, where stats sag and the Crystal’s outer layer degrades.

Maxing out. A Crystal pushed to its ceiling and past it shows heat first, a fever-flush and steam off wet clothes, then blood: from the nose, the corners of the eyes, the gums. Last, the aura cracks. Bright fault-lines run through the color like crazed glass, and every trained eye in the room knows what they are.

The window. Everything on this ladder the room reads off the body, the fighter also reads off their own window, the soul reporting its numbers to its owner in their own tongue and to nobody else. It shows Grades, percentages, bars and levels. A Release or a Domain fills it with the name and what changed, and a timer bar drains:

[FIRST RELEASE: THE LONG YOKE]
[Ardency: top of band]
[Reserve: 47% ▓▓▓▓▓░░░░░]
[Holds: ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓░░░]

The body tells first and the window confirms after. Plenty of fighters have died reading the bar instead of the man in front of them.

Below the tenth. Starving is not only a matter of stats, and what goes after the stats follows the road. A Spirit worker’s Essence is the soul’s own stamped substance, so past that last rung the Core starts to fray: a name will not come, a year goes missing, a veteran stands in a burning yard trying to think whose quarrel this was. It comes back with the reserve, most of it. A Body fighter has spent the world and not himself, and keeps his names; his Shell, with no Charge left to work, starts drawing on the flesh itself, and the skin splits in fine lines where it was taken. A Fate fighter’s reserve is Essence too, so her Core frays like the Spirit worker’s, and her own road’s tells come on top of it: the luck around her curdles, then her name sits wrong in other mouths, and at the bottom every knot she owes falls due together. The table under What it costs has all three roads.

The lost share. Every working loses a share of what it spends, and how much is set by the practitioner’s yield (η, the share of spending that arrives as effect, as a distillation has its yield), which from Glory reads off the class ladder of whichever layer the working goes out through. What the lost share becomes follows the layer too. Through the Shell it burns in the Shell’s own furnace, the athanor that never goes cold, and comes out as heat, noise and light, so a novice’s Body working is loud and hot: a roar, a blast of warm air, light spilling everywhere. Through the Core none of it is heat: it is paid as a hollowing, and a careless Spirit worker goes gray and cold with a dry collar. Through the Attraction Layer it becomes the knot. A master’s is quiet on any road. The same force arrives with almost nothing around it, and the quiet is often the first thing that tells an opponent what they are facing.

What it costs

  • Fracture risk, per layer. One layer run inside its limit is safe. Each further layer stacked at the same time raises the chance of a Crystal Fracture Event (a crack in the Crystal, healed slowly or never). Two layers is a gamble veterans take. Four is a story told about the dead.
  • The cap is absolute. Nothing of the practitioner’s own lifts them past two Grades over sustainable. A Third Release already stands at the cap for its effect, so nothing else can be stacked on that effect while it holds. Gear sits outside this count: a worked piece multiplies the Sub-Stat underneath, under its own limit of ×1.5 from all gear together, and it can carry the shown letter up a band by doing so. The two Grades are still counted from the bare Grade, the one read with the gear off, so a fine helm buys a better number and never a higher ceiling for the fighter’s own layers.
  • Drain, written as time from full. A card states how long a working holds if started on a full reserve, before the reserve falls to a tenth: “holds six minutes from full”. Two workings at once burn both clocks together. A working started at half reserve has a little under half its time left, because the clock runs to the tenth. The exact EU, AU/s and yield behind that line stay on the card and out of the window.
  • Gear draws. A worked piece is lit out of the wearer’s reserve for as long as it is worn in earnest, a Shell draw the window grades light, steady or heavy. A heavy piece spends like a working held the whole fight, and a fighter in three of them reaches half before the first exchange.
  • Fight Drafts refill. A fight Draft (a bottle made to be drunk mid-fight) puts power back into the reserve, and it follows the road of whoever drinks it. Each costs a beat to drink and leaves a crash when it wears off. A Spirit worker’s is Core stock: the Essence arrives unstamped, and the crash (the shakes, a heavy head, and a sleep that will not be refused) is the Crystal stamping it as its own after the fact, all at once, with nobody home to help. A Body fighter’s is a Salt: raw Aether in a fraction that packs straight into the Shell. It goes down like swallowing a burn, and its crash is cramps and fever. A Fate fighter has no bottle of her own, whatever the man at the back of the market says: her reserve is Essence, so she drinks Core stock and takes the Spirit crash with it, and nothing in a bottle puts Thread in the hand. There is no rule against drinking several; there is only the price, which climbs about threefold for each Stage (step on the soul’s sixteen-step ladder) the Draft serves, and the counterfeits.
  • The road’s own bill. The shared tells above are the same on everyone. On top of them each road tires in its own way, because each is spending a different thing: a Body fighter spends the world and the body pays for the handling, a Spirit worker spends herself, and a Fate fighter pays for each pull in Essence and then again in luck that has to be made up later.

A hitter runs hot and cracks, a thrower runs hollow, and a Fate fighter runs unlucky, so a watcher can name the road by how its walker flags. A pull on a thread carries one more price that no bar shows: landed or failed, it leaves a knot, which the world collects later on the puller or on what is hers. The Three Paths has the roads, Soul Wounds and the Menders the Spirit bill in full, and The Tell and the Knot the knot.

How it is beaten

  • Read the ladder. The shared tells are the same on everyone. A short breath and a dimming aura means half; when the color flickers, close. The road’s own tells say what is running out: steam means a body about to crack, a wrong name means a soul about to slip, and a bench leg giving under a bystander means it is time to move your chair.
  • Strip a layer. Break the focus that holds a release, make a vow-sworn fighter choose between the vow and his life, knock the weapon that carries his coat. Each layer lost is a Grade lost.
  • Make them stack. A fighter at the cap who is losing has one move left, another layer, and it is the one most likely to crack him.
  • Make the gear eat. A fighter in heavy worked plate is paying for it by the breath. Draw the fight out and the pieces go dark one at a time, each taking its multiplier with it.
  • Strike the drink. A fight Draft costs a beat with the neck of a bottle at the lips. Break the belt of bottles before the fight and the reserve is all they have.
  • Wait for the crash. A fighter who drank to finish the fight and did not finish it is about to be worse than when they started.
  • Listen. A loud working means a low yield, and for the same blow delivered, a loud fighter runs dry first.

Example

Hob Garrow, a Stage VI caravan guard tempered on Monolithion (the Enduring Monument, a Wellspring of stone), has a stack line that reads: coat, focused (+1); vow, no step back (to the top of the band); IRON GRIP, called (surge); cap at +2. Against a Stage VI raider on a pass road he focuses his coat into his right fist and swears his vow aloud: he will not take a step back. That is two layers, a Grade up and the top of the band above it, and the risk is real.

Two minutes in, his breath is short and his deep amber aura has dimmed: half. The raider sees it and starts circling. Hob drinks a fight Draft with his free hand, a Salt that goes down like a swallowed coal, a beat with his guard down, and takes a cut along the jaw for it. His reserve comes back up. At a quarter again his hands shake and the amber washes gray, and the bar in his window says what his hands already told him. He calls IRON GRIP as he swings, the surge cracks the raider’s shoulder, and the fight is done. An hour later the crash lands, the cramps first and the fever after, and Hob sweats it out across two sacks of meal while the wagons roll on without him.
