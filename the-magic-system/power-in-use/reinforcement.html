Reinforcement

Reinforcement

Nov 28, 202615 min read

How a practitioner’s body holds its power, and how they push it further. For the whole system in brief, see How Magic Works.

“Gather it where the blow is. Then pray there was only the one.” Copybook line, set for novices in most lineage halls and ignored by all of them.

The minstrels call it The Bell and the Fork, it runs to forty verses, and about nine of them happened. At the Bell Feast in Ninefold Market, Gao Baoshan of the Nine-Ladle Hall, a cook by trade and an Expert, Stage VI (sixth on the soul’s sixteen-step ladder), by every account, bet his master’s broth recipe against a rival hall that he could take the Evening Bell of the Temple of Clear Water on his forehead, dropped from its own tower. The whole of Ninefold Market came, and the roofs sold out. He gathered everything. Every scrap of the ochre aura on his body ran up his neck and heaped on his brow, until he wore a lump of amber light the size of a goose egg and the rest of him stood bare as a plucked bird, and the riggers knocked out the pin.

The bell cracked down the lip. Baoshan stood under it with his eyes crossed and his sandals driven a thumb’s depth into the flagstones, the lanterns on the temple steps all swung one way, and the crowd lost its collective mind. Then Sister Ran of the Copper Steamer, who had money on the other side, stepped up behind him and put a coated toasting fork into his left buttock up to the handle, on the sound legal ground that the wager had specified a bell. He bled through three aprons. The Nine-Ladle Hall lost the recipe, its broth has been worse ever since, and Baoshan ate his dinners standing for a season.

Every fighting school I have visited tells this story on the first day, and they are right to. What he did to the bell is a focused coat. What she did to him is the price of one. A colleague of mine in the Measurewrights (the Guild’s sworn measurers) holds that the bell was already cracked, and he has written thirty pages on the subject, and he has never stood under anything.

What it is

A practitioner’s stats (the measured strengths of body and soul, read off the Soul Crystal, which is the soul itself, the thing that stores and sends out their power) live in the body all the time as a passive base: always on, costing nothing. Reinforcement is the active layer on top: power pulled in as Aether from the ground around them and laid over flesh, bone and whatever they hold, to push the body further for a price in reserve (the one store a soul carries). What the coat is made of follows the road. A Body Path fighter wears raw Aether, the Charge packed in the Shell and never refined; a Spirit Path worker wears Essence (Aether refined in the Crystal and stamped as her own); a Fate Path fighter wears her claim, the grip her Attraction Layer has on what happens near her, paid for in Essence like the Spirit worker’s; and all three pay for it out of the same kind of bar. What the coat guards follows the road as well. Only a Shell coat hardens a body, and it does so at its wearer’s Body Mastery, whatever road she leads on.

How it looks

A coat of their own aura. Reinforcement shows as a skin of the practitioner’s own color: the hue of the Wellspring they have tempered on longest (a Wellspring being the governing idea that transforms how a soul uses Aether) at the clarity of their Stage (their step on the soul’s sixteen-step ladder): clouded and smoky low down, clearing as they climb. A Pyreveil fighter’s coat shimmers ember-orange; a Judicium fighter’s crackles arc-blue along the forearms; a Petralon fighter’s shows as nothing but a weight the dust leans toward. It is thickest where they push. That first Wellspring sets the base color, any others add texture and sound, and a Class (the name the soul offers at thresholds) or a big Trait adds a shape, so that two Pyreveil fighters may share a color and nothing else: one coat crawling like coals, the other standing off the shoulders like a mantle.

The body under it. Veins stand up, skin tightens and takes a sheen, the stance settles lower, and the breath deepens. Heat comes off a hard-pushed Body coat in waves, and a Spirit coat pushed as hard goes cold instead.

Focus leaves gaps. Gathered into one place, the coat blazes there and goes pale everywhere else. A fist burns; the far flank fades to bare skin. Reading where it went is half the fight, and a good fighter moves the focus faster than an enemy can read it.

Everyone has a word for it, and an argument. The coat is the most famous art in the world, and each people names it to suit their opinion of it. A Moto calls it matoi, the wearing, and holds that a person of breeding does not show it in company, which is why Moto courts are so full of calm people who may or may not be about to kill you. The Mahuo call it gabot, armor, drill it like armor in ranks at dawn, and say a coat you cannot see is a coat you have not paid for. The lineage halls call it hùtǐ, guarding the body, and have been fighting for six hundred years over which hall thought of it first. A Marrowkin is on shift when it is up, and enters the minutes. A Kharven soldier calls it the hide and compares prices. In the bracket crowds of the Accord towns it is your shine, as in he’s lost his shine, get your money down. All of them mean the table above, and any of them will explain at length why the others do it wrong.

The coat by road. Anyone woken can wear one, and it is bought with reserve on every road. The table above is the Shell’s table: it is what a coat of raw Aether does for flesh and bone, and anyone may lay one, since every soul has a Shell, though a soul that leads elsewhere lays it at its own Body Mastery and no better. The other two Paths wear coats of their own that guard other things. What it is made of and how it sits follow what the road spends, and a trained eye reads the road off the coat before the first blow.

  • A Body coat is raw Aether. It hugs the skin like heat-haze off a summer road, close enough to move when the muscle under it moves, and it runs warm, because it is the Charge itself standing in the Shell, and the Shell is a furnace that never goes cold: what it burns comes off as heat. This is the coat the copybooks draw. It guards the hide, and the fist and ribs under it take the Body Path’s full force bonus.
  • A Spirit coat is Essence. It hangs a half-step off the body, like a second outline that has not quite caught up, and shadows fall a little wrong around it. It is cool to stand near. It wards the soul and not the hide: a thrown dread or a stroke at the Core has to get through it first, which is a thing no Body coat can offer, and a maul goes through it as through fog. The older copybooks say it lifts the body by the same table, and the halls have stopped teaching that line. A Spirit worker who wants her ribs hardened lays a Shell coat under it like anyone else, in raw Aether, at whatever Body Mastery her Shell has reached, which in a pure thrower is seldom much. Pushed hard, it is paid for in the Core, as a hollowing, and none of that is heat.
  • A Fate fighter’s coat barely shows. A faint edge of color in the right light and no more. What gives it away is the room: dust lies still near them, and a dropped coin lands face up. It is the claim worn as presence, and what it lifts is Pressure and the settling of the room, so that a blow aimed at its wearer has a little more to get past before it starts. It hardens nothing; for that a Fate fighter lays a Shell coat at her Body Mastery like the Spirit worker. It is easy to take for no coat at all, which has been the last mistake of a number of people.

Someone who walks two roads wears the coat of whichever layer they are pushing through at that moment, and a good one changes it in the middle of an exchange.

Held gear, never other people. The coat runs into what the hand is holding: a blade, a shield, a staff, the hilt of a hammer. Let go and it leaves the steel within a breath. It cannot be laid over another person, however close or however loved.

Masters can hide it. Most practitioners must choose: hold their Pressure (the weight their presence puts on a room) in, which is tiring work, or coat up and show their color. A master can reinforce with no color showing at all, and that calm is a warning in itself. Few manage it below Master standing, and the bookies shorten their odds the moment one walks in unlit. The other direction is just as old: a fighter short on reserve can flare a coat bigger than the body behind it, which is why anything but a true read (a stronger Gnosis Perception against a weaker Resilience Ward) is an estimate said aloud.

What it costs

  • Reserve, by the second. A card writes it as time from full: how long the coat holds, started on a full reserve, before the reserve reaches its tenth. A spread coat runs about ten minutes for an Adept, longer as their yield (η, the share of a spend that arrives) climbs; a focused coat runs about half that. The practitioner’s own window (the soul reporting its numbers to its owner, unseen by anyone else) shows the running figure as a percentage and a bar, and nobody else in the fight gets to see it.
  • A layer on the stack. A focused coat’s one-step lift is a power-up like any other and counts toward the cap (no more than two steps on the hidden scale over the sustainable figure on their registered card) and toward the fracture risk that comes with stacking (see Stacking and Spending). A spread coat lifts only within its step and adds no risk.
  • Starvation. Below a tenth of reserve the active coat fails outright and the passive base itself sags: the body reads weaker than its sheet, worse the deeper the reserve goes, and Traits (the soul’s standing laws) begin to flicker. Then the road sends its own bill: a Body fighter’s Shell starts drawing the Charge out of the flesh, and the skin splits in fine lines; a Spirit worker’s Core frays, names first; and a Fate fighter, whose reserve is Essence too, frays the same way while her own name starts to sit wrong in other mouths (Stacking and Spending has the table).
  • Rebuilding. A coat dropped by surprise comes back in one beat once the practitioner knows what is happening. One beat is a long time with a knife already in.

How it is beaten

  • Ambush. Asleep, drugged or surprised, a practitioner keeps only the passive base. Assassins, bandits and jealous rivals live by this. Guards are posted over sleeping masters for exactly this reason.
  • Read the focus. Strike where the coat has gone pale. A fighter who gathers everything into the guarding arm has left the ribs at base.
  • Outlast it. Every second costs reserve. Make them chase, make them hold the coat through a long approach, and fight them when the coat fails and the base sags.
  • Disarm. Knock the weapon loose and the coat leaves it. A coated blade is a different object from the same blade on the floor.
  • Go past it. A Body coat guards the hide and nothing behind it, so a Spirit strike goes through to the soul as if the coat were not there. A Spirit coat guards the soul and not the hide, so a spear does the same from the other side unless there is a Shell coat under it. Read which coat it is before choosing the blow.
  • Go after the ward. Reinforcement protects the reinforced body only. The child, the client or the wounded friend beside them stands in plain skin.
  • Cut the ground. On thin, salted or bled ground (Aether-poor by nature, worked against pooling, or pulled empty), or beside a Core (a Wellspring site, in common speech) whose keepers have closed it, nothing fills the reserve back up, everything costs more and the coat runs down sooner.

Holding a break shut

A practitioner with a broken bone can keep fighting on it by holding the break shut with a working, which the trade calls an Essence splint. It is a Bind: the two edges of the break are held to one shape, the way a Bind holds anything to a form, and the limb carries load as if it were whole for as long as the hold lasts.

  • What it costs. The splint is a focused coat laid on the break, so it runs on the focused coat’s clock: about five minutes from a full reserve at Adept standing for a small or single bone (a finger, a rib, a forearm), roughly two percent of reserve every turn. A load-bearing break (a thigh, a hip, the spine, the skull) draws twice that, about two and a half minutes from full, roughly four percent a turn. Both sit on top of whatever else the practitioner spends, and both outrun passive recovery, which gives back about one percent a turn.
  • How long it can be held. Tempering decides how long the soul can keep the hold at all, whatever is left in the reserve. A low Tempering lets go of the break long before the reserve runs dry.
  • When it ends. It ends at Starvation, when Tempering gives out, or when the Bind is broken, and the wound is worse afterward than if it had been left alone: the bone has been worked on while broken, and it heals on the slower count.
  • How it is beaten. Anything that breaks a Bind breaks the splint, and Silence drops it at once. The simplest answer is to outlast it: make the holder move, keep the limb loaded, and wait for the clock.

Example

Mags Tolley, a Stage V hired hunter tempered on Judicium (the Wellspring of Truth, arc-blue, which reads flaws), sleeps at a waystation with her boots on. A thief with a knife comes through the shutter. She is asleep, so she has only her passive base, but her Durability stands at 240, a long way above any knife, and the blade skids off her collarbone and opens a shallow line instead of a deep one. She wakes, and in one beat the coat comes up a smoky arc-blue over her forearms, hissing like a wet wick.

The thief has a partner at the door, Stage V like her and swinging a coated maul. Mags gathers the coat into her left forearm and takes the maul there: the arm is lifted a full step and the iron rings off it. But the blue has drained from her right side to bare skin, the partner sees it, and his second swing goes for her right ribs. She turns her hip, eats half of it at base, and feels a rib go. Her window reports it the moment after, in the tongue she grew up with:

[Hit: right ribs, one cracked]
[Reserve: 81% ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓░░]

From here she has a little under four minutes of focused coat from what is left in her, and she knows it.
