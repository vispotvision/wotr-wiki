The Three Grounds: Density, Depth and Weight

The Three Grounds: Density, Depth and Weight

Nov 17, 202621 min read

What a piece of ground holds for each of the three Paths, how a Measurewright reads it, where the famous ground lies, and what people have done to each other to stand on it

The parties agree to meet on ground with nothing in it. The parties further agree that this was a mistake.

Articles of the Garrick and Voyle duel, second draft, with the amendment in a different hand

In the spring of 702 the pike-captain Hob Garrick of Hammerford, a Body fighter with a neck like a gatepost, called out Sister Anselma Voyle of the Veil-Saints over a remark about his mother, and the two of them then spent eleven months failing to fight. Garrick named a slate quarry above Ridgewall, on ground so rich that a man could fill his reserve in the time it takes to lace a boot. Voyle named the nave of a drowned church off Alabaster at low tide. Garrick’s second said the church was an ambush with a roof on. Voyle’s second said the quarry was a larder. A bookie from the Kessen road, who had taken eleven months of bets and wanted them settled, proposed a crossroads he knew, and both seconds refused it in the same breath, because nobody on either side could say what a crossroads would do and that is the worst thing ground can be. They settled at last on Cobb’s New Barn outside Barleyford, built that winter on thin plowland, where nobody had died, sworn anything or decided so much as the price of a pig.

Four thousand people paid to watch. Garrick ran dry in the time it takes to say so, Voyle could find nothing in the place to take hold of, and the two finest duelists of the decade circled each other on clean straw until the crowd started throwing turnips. The bookie refunded nobody. Old Cobb charged a penny a head for years to see where it had not happened, and the barn has since acquired, by long use as a place where people go to be disappointed, a small character of its own.

Every fighter in the four quarters knows the lesson, and most can give it in a sentence: the ground is the third fighter, and it takes sides by Path.

The three are separate readings, taken with separate instruments, and a place can run high in one, two or all three. Only the first is a thing a practitioner can draw. Nobody fills a reserve from a graveyard’s memory or a crossroads’ decisions. Depth and Weight are conditions of the place, and what they change is the price of the work done there.

Aetheric Density

This is the measure every pump-master, lamp-warden and bracket fighter already knows: rich ground and thin in a scholar’s mouth, fat and lean in the street. Aether lies in the ground and the air, in sixty kinds, and a body standing on rich ground pulls it in fast.

Why it serves every Path. Nobody works Aether without first pulling it in, and every road starts with that pull. A Body fighter packs it raw in the Shell as the Charge. A Spirit worker refines it in the Crystal into Essence (spiritual energy). A Fate puller refines it into Essence exactly as a Spirit worker does, and that Essence is what every pull is paid from; the soul’s own share is part of that reserve, and the Thread itself is never stored, only pulled for the one working. So rich ground refills all three, and thin ground starves all three. The Body Path feels it soonest, since a Body fighter spends what was in the ground a moment ago and has the least between the pull and the blow. The three routes are set out in The Three Paths.

How it is read. With the assay fork, which rings on rich ground in the note and hue of the Wellspring pooled there, and against the sites ladder every hunting hall uses: Freshet, Millrace, Riptide, Whirlpool, Fountainhead (see On Walking Into the Current). The fork reads Density and nothing else. It is silent on the other two measures, and a surveyor who trusts it past its duty is how crews end up cheerful in a Spirit dungeon.

Folk signs. Moss in a strange color, warm stones, dogs lying down on one patch of a field, a tool humming in the belt, and a tiredness that lifts as you walk onto it.

What it takes from the Fate road. Rich ground fills a Fate puller’s reserve like anyone’s, and it crowds the reach. A Fate fighter’s Range and the radius of a Domain are a field of their own standing above the ground’s, and the denser the ground the higher that floor: both fall as the square root of the local Density, which is the Measurewrights’ rule of thumb, and the reason is that a voice is harder to hear in a crowded room. They go to an eighteenth on concentrated ground and to a thousandth at a Nexus. Thread Weight pushes the other way, as its own section says, so a Fate lord reads the fork and the hundred casts together before agreeing to a field.

The catch. Rich ground breeds. Wells spawn creatures, a Well-spawn goes toward the pull, and the richest ground in the world is the ground with the most teeth on it. Dungeons has the whole bill.

Echo Depth

A place where people have lived, prayed, grieved and died for a long time holds what they left. Souls passed through it, meanings were laid on it and kept, and the Soul Plane lies close under such ground the way water lies close under a marsh. The Measurewrights call the reading Echo Depth. Villagers say a house is deep or shallow, and a deep house is one where you lower your voice without being asked.

Why it serves the Spirit Path. A Spirit working takes hold of a soul or a meaning, and it must first find purchase. On shallow ground the worker reaches a long way for it and pays for the reach in Essence, which is to say in themselves. On deep ground souls and meanings stand thick and near, the hold comes easily, and the same working costs less of the Core. It gives a Spirit worker no fire to throw and moves no stone. Spirit force lands on souls there as it does everywhere, and the Shell remains the only door into matter. See Soul Wounds and the Menders for what that force does on arrival.

How it is read. With the still-glass, a shallow bowl of blackened silver filled to the brim with water, set level on the ground and looked into. On shallow ground the face in the water moves with the face above it. On deep ground it lags, and the Measurewright counts the lag in breaths and enters it in fathoms, a word borrowed from the leadsmen. A new barn reads nothing. A parish church three centuries old reads four or five fathoms. The Guild will not print a figure for the worst naves of the Drowned Parishes, since the two clerks sent to take it disagree about whose face they saw.

Folk signs. Colors a little flatter than they ought to be. Shadows that fall slightly wrong. A stillness animals notice before people do. Bread that tastes of somebody else’s kitchen, a child who names a great-aunt nobody told her about, and the strong sense, on a stair, of being the second person on it.

The bands. The still-glass gives five, and each one above shallow raises yield (η, the share of a spend that arrives as intended, as a distillation has its yield) for Spirit work by a set step, a tenth by the Guild’s rule, in the same manner as each band of Density does for work on matter. It multiplies with what the worker’s Essence Class and Wellspring already give, and it can never carry yield past the whole.

The catch. Deep ground is deep for everyone. A Spirit worker’s enemy pays the same low price, and the unwoken can take soul wounds on haunted ground with no fighter anywhere near. Stay long enough and the place starts to lay its own meanings on you. Menders who keep house on deep ground rotate their novices, and the ones who do not rotate them end up with novices who answer to the wrong name.

Thread Weight

Some ground has had great things decided on it: a battle that settled a border, a crowning, a treaty, a verdict, an oath that held for four hundred years, a crossroads where ten thousand travelers a year choose a road. Decisions leave Fate lying heavier on a place, in the plain sense that more outcomes there already have a way of coming about. The Measurewrights call the reading Thread Weight. Soldiers call such a field heavy, gamblers call it crooked, and both are describing the same thing from different sides of a purse.

Why it serves the Fate Path. A pull moves only what already has a way of happening. On light ground a Fate puller hunts for the one loose strap, the one tired guard, and pays a ruinous share of reserve to lean on it. On heavy ground the ways are many and lie near to hand, so the pull costs less and fails less often. A pull that lies nearer to likely also leaves a smaller knot, by the same law that prices every knot, and no ground has ever let a puller off the knot altogether. The full account of the pull, its tell and its bill is in The Tell and the Knot.

How it is read. By the hundred casts. The Measurewright throws an assayed coin one hundred times onto a slate and counts. Honest ground gives its faces in no order worth the name. Heavy ground gives runs, nine and twelve and twenty alike in a row, and the length of the longest run is entered as the Weight. The method is slow, it looks like idleness, and it is the only instrument in the Guild’s chest that has been banned from three gaming towns. Fate does not show on the fork or in the still-glass. Scholars are frank that the hundred casts measure a shadow of the thing, and that nobody has a tool for the thing itself.

Folk signs. Dust that lies very still. Dice that will not behave. Oaths that bite harder than the swearer meant them to, and the same two strangers met three times in a day. A bookie who will not open a book within sight of the place has taken the reading for you.

The bands. The hundred casts give five, and each one above light raises yield for a pull by the same set step, a tenth a band. For a soul at Thread I or II, where a tenth to a fifth of the spend arrives on honest ground, that step is most of the difference between a pull that lands and one that only bleeds.

Reach. Weight also lengthens what Density shortens. A Fate fighter’s Range and the radius of a Domain run farther on heavy ground, because more of what lies in front of the fighter is already leaning the way of a claim. On a field both rich and heavy the two readings pull against each other, and the fork’s square root usually wins unless the Weight is very great.

The catch. Heavy ground is heavy for both sides, it bends for whoever pulls first and best, and it has a way of making small choices count. People who idle on heavy ground find they have promised things.

A Place Can Run High in More Than One

The measures are independent, and the famous places are mostly the ones where they stack.

The Long Barrow at Sorley Bridge is the textbook case. Nine thousand dead went into one trench there in the autumn of 631, on the field where the battle was settled, and in the next generation the trench turned and a Mortalis Well opened in it. It is rich in Density, fathoms deep in Echo, and heavy with Weight, all on a few acres. The Moot lets a clearing contract there every spring, and recruits of every Path come back saying it was the easiest working of their lives and that they would rather not go again.

The Shambles, the fourth belt of the Scourge of Hell, is the dead battlefields of the Great Spirit War, twenty leagues of them. The Density there reads Riptide to Whirlpool, and no Measurewright has stood still long enough to finish a hundred casts.

The eastern arc is the opposite lesson. Young volcanic ground, chartered from nothing within living memory, with some of the richest Density in the world and almost no Depth or Weight under its towns, because nothing has yet had time to be remembered or decided there. Eastern Body schools thrive. Eastern Spirit halls complain that the whole country feels like a rented room. The old ground beside the arc, and the barrow-country of the Tsohanto Reach, are another matter, and their people find the word nothing in the charter very funny.

The western quarter carries the reverse. Its Density has thinned every year since 645, and every reserve in the west fills slowly now, whatever the Path. Its Depth and Weight have not fallen at all, for the west has the oldest towns, the fullest churchyards and the most decided history on the continent. A western Spirit hall fills as slowly as its neighbors and spends less of what it has, and bracket fighters of the Body Path have noticed who is still smiling.

Where a Spirit Wellspring or a Wellspring of Fate has pooled, the Density is of that kind and the ground often runs deep or heavy as well. Which came first is an old quarrel. One school holds that grief draws Thaloré the way a hollow draws rain, and the other that Thaloré ground makes people grieve there until the Depth follows. The Guild funds both chairs and seats them at opposite ends of the table.

What a Fight Looks Like on Each

When two fighters are otherwise matched, the ground breaks the tie, and it reads for each of them on their own road: Density for the Body fighter, Depth for the Spirit worker, Weight for the Fate puller. Footing, light, cold and wet still count for what they always counted.

Walkers of two Paths choose ground for the road they mean to lean on that day, which tells an observant enemy a good deal. The cheapest scouting in the world is to ask where the other side wanted to meet.

Choosing and refusing. Among professionals the argument over ground is the first exchange of the fight, and it is conducted by seconds, in writing, with a surveyor’s reading attached. A challenge that names a graveyard has announced a Spirit worker. A challenge that names a crossroads at noon has announced something worse. Tournament law in most realms requires a ring on plain ground, meaning read and entered as ordinary on all three measures, and ring-wardens are bribed about the reading more often than about the judging.

Who Fights Over It

Rich ground is rented. A worked Core sells its hours, a modest site out-earns a good county, and companies, crowns and sects sign and break charters over keepers’ contracts the way other countries fight over a river crossing. This is the oldest of the three quarrels and the best documented.

Deep ground is inherited, bought and grown. Depth cannot be dug, but it can be had three ways. A Spirit sect builds its hall on an old temple, or buys the churchyard next door, and the parish discovers that its dead have a new landlord. A house keeps its own: ancestral halls, family crypts, the bedroom every heir was born in, tended across centuries for exactly this purpose. And a patient order makes it from nothing, by living, praying and burying in one place for three hundred years, which is why the oldest Spirit halls are so plain and so unwilling to move. Grave-rents, crypt leases and the sale of a dead family’s house to a mender’s hall are ordinary business in every old town. The crimes follow the trade. Bones are moved by night to carry a little Depth with them, with indifferent results. Rival halls have burned each other’s chapels, and a conqueror who means it does not stop at the garrison. He empties the churchyard and plows the temple under, and the ground reads shallow for a generation.

Heavy ground is occupied and manufactured. The great houses crown their heirs in one hall and refuse to crown them anywhere else. They sign every treaty at home when they can and insist on it when they cannot, because every decision made in a room is a little more Weight for the next one, and a house with a Fate bloodline wants its enemies to come and negotiate on four centuries of the family getting its way. Guest-right exists in part to make that survivable. An envoy with any sense asks to meet in a tent.

Armies march for it. A general with a Fate champion offers battle on an old field, where a line has broken before and has a way of breaking again. A general without one declines, and marches three days round. Captains with Spirit companies anchor a flank on a churchyard, and captains facing them have been known to fire it first. Levies care about none of this and are told none of it, and the ground decides more battles than the levies ever learn. Body champions want the richest ground on the field under their own feet, and the first skirmish of most engagements is two vanguards racing for the same warm hillside.

Everybody lies about the reading. A hundred casts can be thrown badly on purpose, a still-glass can be read by a man who was paid to see nothing, and a fork is easily rung against the wrong stone. The Accord hangs for a falsified coil. It has not yet decided what to do about a falsified fathom, and a good many duels have been lost in that gap.
