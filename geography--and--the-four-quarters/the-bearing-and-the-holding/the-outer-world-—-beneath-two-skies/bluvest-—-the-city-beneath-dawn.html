Bluvest — The City Beneath Dawn

Bluvest — The City Beneath Dawn

Nov 17, 202622 min read

Say itBLOO-vest

The Blood Throne · The City Beneath Dawn · Capital of the Dreadvalis Empire

In the autumn of Year 711 the lamp-wardens of Bluvest went on strike, and the greatest night-city in the world discovered that it could not see in the dark. The Vampires could, of course. Their eyes drink the moon through a mile of stone. But the upper districts belong to the living, to the channel-keepers who open the sluices, the bakers and coopers, the Tithe physicians and the porters on the Lantern Stair, and every one of them works by moon-lamp, and every moon-lamp in the city is filled, trimmed and charmed to its soft blue burn by a guild of four hundred mortals who had asked for a copper more a lamp and been told by a Night Governor’s clerk that the dark was free. The guild-mistress, a tall cheerful widow named Hessa Brannock with frost-white braids and a laugh like a dropped tray, put out the lamp on her own doorstep first, in front of the clerk, and then went home and had her supper by touch.

For three nights the deeps did not notice. On the fourth the ration came late, because the keepers on the blood channels could not read their gauges, and the Common Vampire families of the middle city stood in line at the ration-wells in their best red coats for eleven hours, hungry, insulted and perfectly able to see one another’s faces. On the sixth a lord of one of the Seven Blood Estates came up the Stair in a fury, in sable and garnets, to explain the situation to the upper districts personally, and walked straight off the end of the Third Landing into the water channel, which had been moved in Year 704 and which nobody had thought to tell him about, since he had not come up in nine years. On the ninth the Ledger did something it had never done for anything that was not blood. It entered the lamp-wardens’ wage as a protected contribution, payable in grain and tithe-exemption for every warden’s household, and made a crime of stinting it. The lamps went back on at dusk, all of them at once, from the Western Gate to the Third Landing, and the upper districts came out into the streets and cheered as if it were the Long Night.

The Throne, they say, sent Hessa a single red-metal ring for her braid. She wears it to market. Dreadvalis rules by being the one who never runs out, and for nine nights it ran out of the one thing it had never thought to count.

In one breath: a capital sunk a mile into black stone under the oldest veil in the world, where the rent goes up the deeper you live, noon never comes, and a sea of blood is kept in the cellar against the day the north runs short.

Two hands on this page. It stands in Year 715, the twenty-fifth of the Dark Ages. Lines marked Later hand come from the annotator of the Concordance of Ages, writing in the last winter before the turn. Later hand: the lamp-right of 711 is still in the Ledger at the turn of the century, and the wardens still strike over it about once a reign.

Bluvest is the answer to a single problem, written out in stone. A Vampire burns in true sunlight in seconds, and a race that must go underground before every dawn will either build a world underground or be hunted to ash on the surface by anyone with a clear sky and a grudge. The first High Vampires built it. They sank the city down through black stone, reflective mineral, enchanted metal and blood-wards, under a veil that turns noon into deep blue twilight, and they arranged it the only way the curse allows: the deeper you live, the further you are from the sun, and the more you are worth.

The city is a ladder, and everyone in it can tell you which rung they stand on. Turned Vampires and the living at the top, where a breach in the veil would reach first. Common Vampire families in the middle. The High Vampires, the royal halls and the Red Sea at the bottom, where no natural light has ever fallen.

The City and What It Looks Like

You come to Bluvest through the Moonlight Forest, and for the last day of the road the forest is the brightest thing you have ever seen in the dark. The silver-barked conifers and pale hardwoods hold light like new snow, so that under the veil every trunk glows faintly blue-white, and the canopy overhead (the Silver Ocean, the foresters call it) rolls like surf under a moon that shows through the veil at noon. Then the trees open on a broad hollow of black stone, and in it stands the Western Gate: two leaves of black iron forty feet high, worked with red-metal vines, under a lintel cut with the inscription every child of the empire can recite. The Sun was made our enemy. Hunger was placed inside us. Neither fact determines what we choose when the doors close and night begins. Beyond the gate the ground simply stops, and the city goes down.

The Lantern Stair is the great spiral of the upper city, a shaft a quarter mile across with the districts built into its walls in rings, terrace under terrace, every balcony hung with moon-lamps in brass cages, so that from the gate it looks like a well full of blue fireflies. Bridges cross it. Lifts run on chains. Mortal porters run the stairs with baskets on their heads, and every hundred feet of descent the Stair passes another Landing, a broad market terrace with its own bells, shrines and quarrels.

Under the streets run the Three Channels, which Arkhaven Bluvest laid out before anyone had built a house to put over them. Water runs in pale limestone. The alchemical nutrients that keep the living districts healthy run in channels of green glass. Blood runs in red-lacquered pipe with brass gauges every forty yards, and keepers in grey aprons walk them day and night reading the dials. Nobody mixes the three. A Bluvest proverb holds that a city is three rivers and a promise to keep them apart.

Light comes down the Mirror Shafts: tall bright tubes lined with polished mineral that carry the glow of the Moonlight Groves from the forest floor into the middle city, so that the Common Vampire districts live under a soft silver daylight of their own, borrowed from trees. The light is Luminalis glow and never sunlight, which is the whole of the trick, and every shaft has a Tenebra shutter at the top that can drop in a heartbeat. Children in the middle city grow up believing the sky is silver and the forest is the sun.

The major places

  • The Western Gate. The only surface gate open to foreigners, with the inscription over it and a Tithe Hall beside it, where the mortals of the forest villages give their portion to a physician and go home with a stamped tin and a bag of twilight barley.
  • The Lantern Stair and its Landings. The upper city: Turned barracks, mortal workshops, the lamp-wardens’ guildhall, the bakers’ row on the Second Landing and the breach-crews’ bell towers. Cheapest rent in the city and nearest to the sky.
  • The Middle City. The Common Vampire districts under the Mirror Shafts: broad galleried streets, warm-houses, fighting yards, the ration-wells where the Red Sea’s allowance is drawn, and the loudest night markets in the north.
  • The Deeps. The High Vampire estates, the ancestral vaults, the High Courts and the royal halls, cut into the black stone below the last Mirror Shaft and lit by nothing but red-metal lamps and the wearer’s own aura.
  • The Red Sea. The great alchemical reservoir at the city’s heart: donated, taxed, purchased, inherited and enslaved blood, kept cold in a vaulted basin so wide a lamp on the far shore looks like a star. Lord Sevran Molovar, Keeper of Seventeen Veins, drew its sluices, and every Tithe Hall in the north runs on his designs.
  • The Arkhaven Door. A small black door on the Fourth Landing with a brass sunburst on it, behind which is the experimental solar chamber where Arkhaven Bluvest, the city’s architect, died after misclassifying one wavelength as harmless. The door has not been opened since. Every apprentice ward-glazier in the city touches it on the first day of their training, and the sunburst is worn bright.
  • The Veilspire of Bluvest. The anchor tower of the oldest Great Veil, rising from the forest above the city like a black needle, worked through Vantabriel and Tenebra, with Therosan masons living in its foot and charging the empire for every flaw they find.

Roads. The Veil Roads run out from the Western Gate under the veils, strung with the Hollow Lanterns, little undercities of waystations, granaries and Tithe Halls a day’s walk apart. East through the forest to the Seven Blood Estates and the Elyran Green Crowns. North across the Moonlight Snow Plains to the black walls of Morvayne. South, by Veyran’s grain roads and the long way down to the Red Marches, toward the sun.

Who Rules

The Blood Throne. Bluvest calls whoever sits it simply the Throne, in the way a farmer says the weather. It belongs to the Royal Vein, the line descended from the blood authority of Vaelora Dreadvalis, First Blood Empress, and the Vein does more than govern. Its blood is woven into the city’s oldest wards, reservoirs and oaths, so that the gates answer it, the Red Sea recognizes it and the Ledger’s law takes its standing from it. Succession runs in the blood, in the soul and in the stone at once: a lawful heir inherits a crown and a place inside the systems that keep the city alive. A pretender without the blood can sit the chair, and the doors will not open for them.

The Seven Blood Estates ring the city in the deep Moonlight Forest, each a Great Clan’s blood-system with its own wards, reservoirs and founder’s blood. A mortal of the upper districts is taught to say their names only in Old Bluven, and only at a bow. The Estates sit in the deeps when the Throne calls them, keep town houses on the lowest galleries, and each holds a key to one sluice of the Red Sea, which is the city’s oldest constitutional arrangement and its most frequently threatened.

The offices. Night Governors run the districts and the provinces beyond the gate. The Warden of the Sluices keeps the Red Sea’s ledger of what goes in and what comes out, an office the Molovar line has held since the first basin was cut. The High Courts of Bluvest try what the Crimson Ledger Courts of Morvayne send up to them: clan feuds, blood crimes of the nobility, treason against the Vein. Morvayne holds the machinery of the empire. Bluvest holds the throne, the blood and the last word, and each city thinks the other is a branch office.

The silence. Malakar walks the Material World again, and every Vampire in Bluvest can feel their maker in their blood like a second pulse. The Throne has said nothing in public. In the deeps the oldest High Vampires, the ones who remember the Taken Winter, are asking whether the thing that made them has come back for them, and the Malakarian loyalists have stopped asking and started leaving, south, in ones and twos, with the city gates logging their going in a book nobody is allowed to read.

Wealth and What It Trades

Bluvest makes very little and holds a great deal. It is a capital, which is to say a place where the empire’s surplus comes to be counted, and the surplus of Dreadvalis is everything its rulers cannot eat: grain, metal, medicine, timber and time.

  • Blood. The Red Sea is the city’s treasury and its granary at once. In famine every recognized citizen draws a ration, the size set by rank. In plenty the Warden of the Sluices sells the surplus forward to the Estates as vein-notes, promises of blood in a lean year, and vein-notes trade in the deeps the way gold does in the south.
  • Ward-glass and moon-lamps. The light-proof glazing that seals every window in the empire is cut on the Fourth Landing, and so are the moon-lamps of every Hollow Lantern from the Archipelago to the Red Marches. Both carry the city’s mark.
  • The red alchemists. In the catacombs below the High Courts the red alchemists keep forges that run on blood instead of coal, and make the empire’s finest worked gear in their own long, cold way. Their masterworks have names and reputations.
  • What comes in. Twilight barley from Veyran and the Cold Granaries for the living districts; moonlight timber, spirit amber and preservation resins from the Elyran Green Crowns, by treaty; frozen fish and whale oil from the Archipelago; and Rhaegyr steel from the far side of the Twilight March, by way of Calverne’s road, because Dreadvalis armies wear dragon-forged plate and nobody in Bluvest finds that funny.

Rent, Food and the Street

Rent runs upside down. On the Lantern Stair a room costs almost nothing and a household owes breach duty besides, nights on the shutter crews that would seal a tear in the veil above. In the middle city rent is reckoned in ration-share, so many cups a month to the landlord’s clan. In the deeps nobody rents; a gallery there is inherited, married into, or taken by a court. A mortal family that saves for three generations can buy one floor further down, and does, and holds a party on the new landing to which the old neighbors come in order to complain about the stairs.

The living eat twilight barley bread, pale and faintly sweet; moon elk and smoked pale stag; frozen Archipelago fish fried in elk fat; frostroot mash; and moonpetal tea, which every Tithe physician prescribes after a draw and every child hates. The bakers’ row on the Second Landing sells hot barley rolls stamped with the Western Gate inscription, and a widow there has a hedge-worker’s trick that keeps them warm exactly as long as you are polite to her.

The dead drink by law. The ration comes from the ration-wells, drawn by rank. Above the ration a Common Vampire with money goes to a warm-house, where donor houses sell blood across the counter by its legal kind: sevrathi, freely given, is the luxury, poured warm in small glass cups and paid for in silver, and the donor’s name is chalked on a board behind the bar so the drinker knows whom to thank. Draal, blood with no recognized owner, is cheap, comes cold and comes without questions. The difference between the two is the first thing a reformist wants to talk about and the last thing a traditionalist will.

The street runs on bells and pulses. The bells are the Veiling of Dawn, rung before every sunrise though no sun will reach the city: gates close, the Mirror Shaft shutters drop to half, and the whole of Bluvest pauses for the length of one breath it does not need to take. The pulses are Bloodcant, the Vampires’ language of heartbeat and touch, so that a crowd in the middle city is always quietly talking under its talking, and a Turned porter feels a passing High Vampire’s displeasure as pressure in his bite scar before he hears a word. Children play Noon, in which one child shouts the word and the rest dive under anything they can find, and the last one standing is ash.

Shared arts. The aura coat is the shroud in Bluvest, worn close and dark, and a Common Vampire who lets theirs flare red in the street is showing off for someone. Pressure is the cold, for what a stronger soul does to the air of a gallery. Sensing is hearing the vein: Vampires read heartbeats, wounds and fear the way other people read faces, and a Bluvest card-player who can hear the vein will not sit at a table with one who can.

What they joke about. The south, where people live in the sky with no lid on it and cry at the brightness. Morvayne’s clerks, who are said to have filed the Taken Winter in triplicate. Dragons, who eat the ground. The Turned, mostly by the Turned. Lords who have not come up the Stair in nine years.

Culture

Old Bluven is the city’s ceremonial tongue: long, quiet and full of pauses, many of its sentences meant to be spoken with the heart stilled. Blood marriages, trials, royal declarations and investitures are held in it. Dreadvalic is the street, and its grammar shifts with whom you are addressing, prey, protected mortal, kin, maker, fledgling, clan sovereign, elder or royal blood, so that a sentence in Bluvest carries a social map inside its verbs.

The calendar has three holy nights. Kethra, the First Thirst, when a fledgling’s first sanctioned feeding is witnessed by its clan, and the proof of civilization is the moment the fledgling lets go. The Moon Hunt, at full moon, when the clans that keep the custom hunt the forest together. And the Long Night, every few winters, when the far north goes weeks without real daylight even outside the veils, and the entire city goes up through the Western Gate at once and lives on the forest floor: banners on the trees, red lanterns strung through the Moonlight Groves, fighting schools in the hollows, the Throne’s household walking among the crowds, Vampire children seeing snow under an open sky for the first time and the mortal ones laughing at them.

The dead. The sun leaves little but ash. A Vampire who burns is mixed with black resin and sealed inside a named memorial mask in the Ash Wake, and the masks hang in the ancestral vaults of the deeps in rows that run back to the founding. Their weight is why the deeps run high in Echo Depth, and why a Spirit-led duelist will pay a great deal to be challenged down there.

Executions at the High Courts are dressed for like weddings, in red and white, with music, and seats near the block sell at a premium, because the city believes a death should be witnessed as fully as a marriage, and both are entries in the Ledger.

Doctrine. The Imperial Doctrine of Blood is cut into the lintel of the High Courts: blood is sustenance, memory, inheritance, evidence, consent, debt and power, the record of a life carried in liquid form. The radical reading, that whoever drinks a life takes responsibility for what it held, is preached openly on the Third Landing and quietly denied in the deeps.

Law and How It Fights

Law. The Crimson Ledger governs every clan in the city: blood consumed, mortals protected, debts, unlawful killings, births, turnings and inherited obligations. Its courts sit at Morvayne; the High Courts of Bluvest hear what Morvayne sends up and what the nobility does to itself. The crimes that matter most in Bluvest are blood crimes: feeding on a protected mortal, a child or a diplomat; stealing another clan’s donor; illegal turning; wasting valuable blood; and drinking another Vampire. Red Guest-Law protects any mortal under declared hospitality, and a household that breaks it loses standing in Fate as well as in society, which starts blood feuds that run for decades. Since 711 the Ledger also protects the lamp-wardens’ wage, and is a little embarrassed about it.

The two faces. The tributary kingdoms are shown the Tithe Hall by the Western Gate, with its clean tins, physicians and exemptions. They are not shown the donor houses of the deeps, some of them hereditary prisons under polite names, or the livestock pens the traditionalist Estates keep on their own land, which the Ledger tolerates and the Throne does not discuss. Bluvest lives with both faces at once, and every reformist in the city has a theory about which one is the real one.

How it fights. Bluvest is a vertical fortress, and the only attack that really frightens it comes from above. An army at the Western Gate is a nuisance; a tear in the Great Veil over the Stair at noon is a massacre. So the city drills for breaches the way a port drills for fire. Every upper household serves on the breach crews, every Landing has a bell tower and a shutter-gang, and at the first gleam of real sunlight in a shaft the crews drop Tenebra shutters, roll ward-glass screens across the galleries and drive the Turned and the living down past the Mirror Shafts in an order every child has practiced since they could walk. The fair answer to Bluvest is the sky: tear the veil, bring daylight down a shaft, and the city’s strength becomes a trap. The fair answer to that is the shutter, and the Veilspire, and the Therosan masons who are paid very well to keep it standing.

The garrisons. Turned soldiers hold the upper districts and the gate, Common Vampire companies the middle city, and the deeps defend themselves, since a High Vampire in their own gallery, on their own ground, with their clan’s founder-blood in the walls, is about as dangerous a thing as the north contains. Moonlight and shadow strengthen every Vampire, and a fight in Bluvest is always a fight under both.

The Wild Around the Gate

The Dreadvalis do not hunt for food, so the forest around Bluvest is crowded with game that only mortals and predators pursue. Moon elk cross the Veil Roads in herds whose antlers glow under the veil, and the gate guard will stop a caravan for an hour rather than argue with one. Veil owls, great pale hunters of the permanent twilight, nest in the Veilspire and are a city emblem. Pale stags shed the Groves’ glow from their coats and leave the forest floor sparkling where they bed down.

The danger is the Night Gaunts, light-swallowing hunters of the darkest veil-country, and the veil over Bluvest is the darkest in the empire. A gaunt can drink a moon-lamp out from across a clearing and take whoever was holding it, and they are dangerous even to Vampires. The Night Governor of the forest posts a standing bounty, paid at the Western Gate in silver or ration-share at the hunter’s choice, and the gaunt-hunters of the Hollow Lanterns are a famous, loud and short-lived profession.

The ferals. Every Vampire can fall into the Feral State, and a starving one goes down through the five stages, Drying, Crimson Fever, Blood Frenzy, Soul Consumption and Feral Hollowing, until hunger is all that governs. The old construction shafts under the Stair, sealed when the Mirror Shafts were cut, are where Bluvest’s ferals go to ground, and the Hollow Watch, a company of Turned volunteers with silver and stakes, goes down after them. Early ferals can be brought back with blood and time. A Blood Hollow is put down, and its mask is made like any other.

Neighbors and Grudges

  • Morvayne. The city of law, under the Moonlight Snow Plains behind its black walls. Morvayne thinks Bluvest is a beautiful cellar with a chair in it. Bluvest thinks Morvayne is a filing cabinet with a wall around it. Both are right, and the empire runs on the arrangement.
  • The Seven Blood Estates. Close enough to be family, which is the problem. Each holds a sluice key, and each has threatened at least once to stop turning it.
  • The Ashen Courts. The oldest High Vampire nobility, on the gray stone of the Northern Tablelands, who keep their distance from the Throne and their opinions to themselves. Bluvest invites them to every Long Night, and they send masks.
  • The Elyran Green Crowns. The Elven canopy cities of the Moonlight Forest, who pay no blood and tend the Groves whose glow lights the Mirror Shafts. The city depends on them for its daylight, and the Elves are courteous enough to mention it only once a year.
  • The Kingdom of Veyran. Grain, horses and the tithe, paid on a schedule of harvests. Veyran’s ambassador in Bluvest lives on the Second Landing by choice, says it is for the bread, and is a very good spy.
  • The Red Marches and the south. The frontier is a long way down the Veil Roads, and Bluvest thinks about it the way a sleeper thinks about a window left open. See The Twilight March.
