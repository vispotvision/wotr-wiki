The Twilight March — Where the Veils End

The Twilight March — Where the Veils End

Nov 28, 202618 min read

Say itTWY-lite MARCH

The Frontier of Two Skies · The Grass Sea, the Five Rivers and the Golden Escarpment · Where Blood-Law Meets Tribute

In the spring of Year 709 the reeve of Corran’s Hollow, a hamlet of thirty-one households on the Horse Rivers, walked into the Twin Assize at Sallow Ford carrying two pieces of paper and a wheelbarrow of turnips. The first paper was the northern Edge Roll, sealed in red wax by Morvayne’s Tithe Ministry, and it said that Corran’s Hollow had lain under the veil for the whole of the year past and owed a full blood tithe. The second was the southern Edge Roll, sealed in gold by the clerks of Akhazar Gate, and it said that Corran’s Hollow had lain under open sun for the whole of the year past and owed a full tribute in grain and cattle. The turnips were what Corran’s Hollow had left after paying both.

The reeve was a short, freckled, extremely polite woman named Ottilie Marsh, and she set the two rolls side by side on the Assize table, in the arc-blue light of the Judicium reader who sits at every Assize, and asked the court which of them was lying. The reader looked for a long time. Then she said, in front of both delegations, that neither roll was lying: both clerks had copied the same survey, taken in the Long Night winter of 706 by a Therosan mason who had been drunk, and who had marked Corran’s Hollow on both sides of the Dusk Line at once out of a sense of fairness. The northern delegation laughed. The southern delegation did not, being dragons, but one of them was seen to look at the ceiling for a while.

The Assize ruled that week that the weeks dark and the weeks of sun on the two rolls must add up to one year and no more, and that where they do not, the town pays the lesser of the two bills until the clerks agree. It has been called the Marsh Rule ever since, and every reeve on the March can recite it. Ottilie went home with her turnips, a refund in grain from the south, a refund in blood-days from the north, and the first copy of a ruling that both empires have been trying to read narrowly ever since. On the March the law is the one thing both skies have to share, so a hamlet with a wheelbarrow can make both of them blink.

In one breath: the frontier band across the middle of the Outer World, where the veils run out and the sun comes back, so that whether a town owes blood north or tribute south depends on how many weeks of the year the dark lay over it, and both empires keep their own count.

Two hands on this page. It stands in Year 715, the twenty-fifth of the Dark Ages. Lines marked Later hand come from the annotator of the Concordance of Ages, writing in the last winter before the turn. Later hand: the Marsh Rule outlives the March it was written for, and both successor courts after the split cite it as their own.

The March exists because neither empire can live in the other’s sky. A Vampire burns in the southern sun, and no veil can be raised over the volcanic belt. A fire-dragon slows in the northern cold, and under the veils even House Dovaryn’s Wyverns fly half-blind. So between them lies a broad band of grassland, river country and escarpment where neither empire is comfortable, and there the mortal kingdoms that survived the Taken Winter make their living by being worth more alive than conquered.

The March is the only place in the Outer World where both laws apply at once. In the north a border follows the veil. In the south a border is layered, mortal king below, House above, Sovereign over all. On the March both systems run on the same farm, and the farmer keeps two sets of books.

The Dusk Line

The veils fail at the March. They thin along the empire’s southern edge, and somewhere in the Grass Sea the last of them gives out altogether, and that edge is never still. In winter, when the sun is low and the nights are long, the Therosan veil-keepers can push it south. In summer the sun burns it back north. A farm on the Horse Rivers can spend the frost months under deep blue twilight at noon and the harvest months under open sky, and the line where the dark stops is called the Dusk Line.

Along it stand the dusk stones: waist-high posts of black Therosan basalt, each with a red band painted on its north face and a gold band on its south, set a mile apart from the Red Marches to the Golden Escarpment. Every week of the year a Ministry surveyor and an Akhazar clerk walk the stones together and note on which side of each the dark lay at noon. Neither trusts the other to walk alone, and both are usually right.

Weeks dark. Every town on the March is assessed by how many weeks of the year the veil lay over it. The northern tithe is owed in proportion to the weeks dark, the southern tribute in proportion to the weeks of sun, and a town wholly under the veil owes only blood, a town wholly in the sun owes only tribute, and a town in between owes both in their shares. The two counts are kept on the Edge Rolls, one in red wax at Morvayne’s Tithe Ministry and one in gold at Akhazar Gate, and the two rolls have never once agreed. Since 709 the Marsh Rule holds that the weeks on both rolls must add up to one year, and where they do not, the town pays the lesser bill until the clerks settle.

The arrangement makes the line itself valuable. Land a mile north of the winter mark owes almost nothing but blood; land a mile south of the summer mark owes almost nothing but grain. Land between the two marks owes a little of both and is raided by both, so it sells cheapest on the continent, and the March is full of people who bought it anyway.

The Land and What It Looks Like

From the Red Marches the land opens south into the Great Grass Sea, the Endless Green, a temperate grassland on deep black soil running further than a Wyvern can fly in a day. In winter its northern reaches lie under the last thin veil, a dim silver dusk over pale stubble and frost-white grass, and you can watch the twilight end on the horizon like the edge of a lake. In summer the whole sea stands green and gold under open sky, horse herds moving through it like weather.

East of the Grass Sea lies the old Five River country, the most crowded land in the Outer World: river towns, bridges, mills, walled market-cities and toll-houses on every crossing, five broad rivers running south out of the cold and every one of them crossed by the Dusk Line twice a year. Further east the land climbs onto the Golden Escarpment, the southern face of the Serrathan Highlands, a weathered wall of yellow stone and waterfalls where House Zhaeryn mines gold within sight of Vel Sarith’s northern silver.

The major places

  • The Red Marches. The empire’s southern frontier province, a belt of Hollow Lanterns, Turned garrisons and Vein-Marshals’ forts along the winter mark, built low and half underground, with ward-glass shutters that close the moment the summer sun comes over the line.
  • Sallow Ford. The neutral crossing on the Horse Rivers where the Twin Assize sits each autumn. It stands on a Judicium site, dry and windy in the season the Assize meets, and its Thread Weight is the heaviest on the March: a treaty sworn there in the arc-blue light holds, and a lie told there comes away from its maker in front of everyone.
  • Ered-Vaelor, the Granary Citadel. House Eredan’s seat in the southern Grass Sea, a dragon-fortress built around barns the size of cathedrals, where a drought in the south is answered in grain bought north.
  • Akhazar Gate. The Continental Embassy-Fortress on the southern edge of the March, between Drakyssia and the Five River country, where every road permit, tribute schedule and southern Edge Roll is sealed in gold.
  • The Saren towns. The fortified towns of the Free March of Saren, founded by refugees from dragon conquest who chose the tithe, with walls built thick against fire and Tithe Halls in every square.
  • Zhaer-Khazad and the Golden Escarpment. House Zhaeryn’s Golden Mountain at the March’s eastern end, where the Escarpment’s gold is weighed, certified and sent south, and where Ordas’s silver gets its stamp.

Roads. The March Roads are the busiest on the continent, grain and medicine going south, gold and steel going north, with a toll-house on every bridge and an Akhazar permit required on the southern side. Calverne holds the road from the northern mines to the coast and charges everyone for it. Dovaryn’s Wyverns fly the line overhead, and a caravan that sees a wing turn toward it stops and shows its papers to the sky.

Who Rules

Nobody, and everybody. The March has no throne of its own. Each kingdom on it rules its own ground, each empire rules the kingdoms sworn to it, and between them the treaties drawn after the first raids, between Western Dragon diplomats, the envoys of House Akhazar and Morvayne’s Ledger Courts, are recognized by Fate and taken seriously by both.

North of the line. Veyran holds the northern Grass Sea, a horse-and-grain monarchy that feeds the Cold Granaries. Therosan’s engineers and masons built most of the Veilspires and still keep the frontier veils, which makes them the people who decide where the Dusk Line sits in winter, and they know it. Calverne holds the mine road. Saren holds its walls. Over all of them the Vein-Marshals of the Red Marches keep the frontier for the Blood Throne, with Turned garrisons who can stand a gray winter noon and not much else.

South of the line. Valecar sells cavalry, grain and mercenaries; its herds feed Eredan’s ranches, and its companies fight in Drakyssian wars for pay. Ordas is small and rich, and its silver pays for a professional army that Zhaeryn certifies by the coin. Over them stands House Akhazar, Keepers of Roads and Foreign Crowns, who govern everyone in the south who is not Drakyssian, and who since the Taken Winter have also handled the empire’s most delicate relationship, with Morvayne’s Ledger Courts across the March.

The Twin Assize. Once a year, in the dry windy autumn, both empires send a delegation to Sallow Ford and sit at one table under a Judicium reader. They hear the year’s border disputes, raid claims, Edge Roll quarrels and treaty breaches, and their rulings bind both sides. It is the only court on the continent where a dragon and a Vampire lord sit on the same bench, and the only one where a reeve with a wheelbarrow can win.

Wealth and What It Trades

The March is rich because everything that crosses the continent crosses it.

  • What crosses. Grain, preserved food, medicine, physicians, surgical steel, the common metals, Frozen-Well crystals, moonlight timber, spirit amber and resins go south. Gold, dragon minerals, House Rhaegyr’s weapons and scale plate, volcanic glass, war beasts, hides and livestock come north. Almost all of it moves under House Avarax contracts and Akhazar permits on the south side and through Morvayne’s Tithe Ministries on the north. A Drakyssian war raises the price of grain in Bluvest; a hard northern winter raises the price of steel in Kra’Vorrak; and the March takes a toll both ways.
  • What it makes. Grain, livestock, leather, wool, salt and iron from the Grass Sea; cavalry horses, engineering, ward-work and mercenary contracts from the Five Rivers; gold and silver from the Escarpment.
  • The crossings. On the March the land matters less than the bridges, fords and passes. A good ford is worth more than a county, and every one of them has been fought over at least once.

Rent, Food and the Street

Rent comes in two coins. A room in a Five River town is quoted in silver by the week and in blood-days by the season, and a landlord who wants a tenant from the other side of the line will take either. Farmland is priced by weeks dark: the more weeks of the year a field lies under the veil, the more of its rent is owed in blood, and the less a southern buyer will pay for it. The cheapest land on the continent lies between the winter and summer marks, owing both bills and raided from both directions, and it is farmed by people who say they like the view.

The table. Grass Sea beef and black barley bread; river eel smoked over willow; Valecar horse-milk cheese, sharp enough to start an argument; frostroot and onion stew in the cold rains; hot spiced cider through the frost months. A March tavern keeps two price boards, red and gold, and a meal eaten exactly on the Dusk Line is said to be free, which is why so many taverns claim to stand on it.

The street. Every market town on the March has a tithe cart and a tribute wagon, often parked at opposite ends of the same square, and children are raised knowing which one their family’s name is written on this season. A Turned soldier and a Dragonkin drover can drink at the same bar, sit at opposite ends of it and pretend not to notice each other, and the barkeep charges both a little extra for the pretending. Children play Line, in which one child is the Dusk Line and walks slowly across the yard, and everyone on the dark side must speak only in a whisper, and everyone on the bright side must shout.

Shared arts. Bluvest calls the aura coat the shroud and wears it close. On the March it is the half-cloak, worn loose and plain so that nobody at a crossing can tell which sky taught it. Pressure is the lean, for the weight a stronger soul puts on a conversation at a crossing. Sensing is reading the weather, because on the March you learn early to feel which way the next raid is coming.

What they joke about. Morvayne’s clerks and Akhazar’s clerks, who are said to have married each other’s sisters in order to argue at home. Therosan masons and their surveys. Wyverns who land in the wrong field. Bluvest, where people pay rent to live in a hole.

Culture

The March speaks Dreadvalic in the north, Drakyssian in the south and the common mortal tongues of the Five Rivers everywhere, and most traders speak all three badly. Its peoples keep their old kingdoms’ customs, and over them the March has grown customs of its own.

The Turning of the Stones. Twice a year, at the first frost and the first green, every town on the March walks out to its nearest dusk stone and watches the line go past. In autumn the northern families sing and the southern families bank their fires; in spring the other way about. The day the line crosses a town is a holiday in both calendars.

Hybrids. The rare Vampire-Dragon hybrids have only ever been born in the borderlands where the two empires meet, and the March is where people tell stories about them, mostly untrue. Saren towns treat the subject as unlucky. Valecar companies treat it as a recruiting opportunity.

The Long Night. When the far north goes weeks without real daylight, the Dusk Line runs further south than in any other winter, and whole Grass Sea counties spend a month under the dark. Dovaryn doubles its patrols for as long as it lasts, and the March holds its breath until the sun comes back.

Law and How It Fights

Law. Three laws run on the March at once: the Crimson Ledger north of the line, Akhazar’s frontier law south of it, and the Fate-recognized treaties over both. A crime committed on the Dusk Line is tried at the Twin Assize, and it is a well-known March trick to commit one there on purpose and wait for autumn. Red Guest-Law holds in the north; Drakyssian hospitality holds in the south; a traveler on the line is safest with both.

How it fights. Raids are common, and the raiding calendar follows the line. In winter, when the veil comes south, the Vein-Marshals’ Turned can ride further into the Grass Sea, and the north raids. In summer, when the sun burns the line back, Dovaryn’s wings and Valecar’s cavalry can reach further north, and the south raids. A March town learns to read the season like a farmer reads the sky. Neither empire wants a real war on the March: conquest there is expensive, the kingdoms on it are more useful whole, and no single Drakyssian House can march north alone. So the raids stay raids, and every autumn the Twin Assize sits and the damages are counted.

The fair answer. To the north’s winter raid, the sun: every Red Marches fort has to be back under the veil before the line moves, and a garrison caught on the wrong side in the first warm week burns. To the south’s summer raid, the cold and the dark: a Wyvern wing that follows a raid too far north flies half-blind under the veils, and the Turned are waiting.

Monsters on the Roads

The Grass Sea’s herds draw hunters of every kind. Wild horse-herds run the Endless Green, and the drovers who follow them for Eredan or Veyran fight off the big grassland predators every season. Along the line itself the danger is hunger: ferals drifting south out of the Red Marches in the gray weeks, starved past the Crimson Fever and looking for a farm the Ledger does not protect, and young drakes flown off from Dovaryn’s roosts who have learned that cattle near the line are seldom guarded by anything they fear. Both empires post bounties, and a March bounty-hunter is paid in whichever coin the corpse came from.

Neighbors and Grudges

  • The Dreadvalis Empire. The north sees the March as its pantry, its toll-road and its front door, and keeps the door locked from the inside. See Bluvest.
  • Drakyssia. The south sees the March as a granary it rents and a border it watches. The Council of Ten Wings has never agreed that the north is worth the cost of taking.
  • Each other. Veyran and Valecar share the Grass Sea, the Horse Rivers and a centuries-old argument about where one ends and the other begins, settled every autumn at Sallow Ford and reopened every spring.
  • Malakar. Since the Vampires’ maker returned, the Council of Ten Wings has ordered Dovaryn to double its patrols along the Red Marches and Akhazar to learn what the Ledger Courts are not saying. Malakarian loyalists leaving the undercities have to cross the March to go anywhere at all, and the Saren towns have begun asking travelers questions at the gate.
