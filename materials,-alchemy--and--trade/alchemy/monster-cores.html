Monster Cores

Monster Cores

Nov 28, 202631 min read

Where the stone sits, what it fetches by the beast’s own Grade and Level, how fast it dies, what the bench makes of it, what happens if you eat it, the crime that looks exactly like it, and the people who live off all of the above

One stone, of the wyrm called Grandfather Rent. Received of: the rope.

Receipt in the Verath hall’s day book for the harvest of 677, in the hand of a clerk who resigned the same afternoon

For sixty years an SS-Grade earth wyrm called Grandfather Rent took one wagon in ten off the salt road below Verath, regular as a landlord, and the bounty on him had reached twelve hundred gold and been painted on every rim-town door when a crew called the Copper Lanterns finally killed him in the summer of 677, under a sky the color of brass, at a cost of five of their eleven. It took three days to cut him open. The stone came out of him at dusk, a fist of amber glass with a slow light turning in it, worth more than the town: twenty thousand gold by private treaty before anyone had said his name, and the name would have doubled it. The six survivors carried it up to the hunting hall behind every drum in Verath, and they were on their third keg and their second knife fight over who had got the kill, when somebody noticed they had put it down.

It was on the hall roof. It was in the right fist of the crew’s porter, a Bearkin woman the size of a cider press, who had hauled their tent poles and their wounded for six years. Under the Accord’s porter clause she was entered on the crew’s instrument along with the rope, as nemo, nobody, and so she had no share in the wyrm she had bled for and no name on any roll, and people called her Nem. She stood up there against the sunset with the amber light coming through her fingers and her mouth open, holding the stone an inch from her teeth, and she told all of Verath in a voice you could hear on the salt road that she would very much like to discuss terms.

Everybody knows what comes out of a person who goes raw on a stone that size, which is to say nobody does. Most die. The rest might be anything, standing on your roof, with sixty years of a wyrm’s tempering going off inside them at once. So the town stopped. For three days the Charter’s reeves, the hall’s master, the crew, an envoy from a Midmarch king and four factors stood in the square negotiating with a porter on a roof, while the sausage-sellers set up and the bookies gave nine to one she would swallow by Sunday. It was the hall’s advocate who found the real difficulty, around noon of the second day, and he sat down on the steps and put his head in his hands. By law she could not be a party. She could not sign, could not be bound, could not receive consideration and could not be bribed, because she was equipment, and the whole Sealed Charter of the Concord was being held hostage by a length of rope.

They settled the only way the formulary allows. The safe-conduct out of Verath, which is still framed in the Waxgate law library, is made out to “the rope, and whatever it may be carrying.” Nem came down off the roof with the stone in her shirt and walked north to Brokencollar with forty porters behind her, and she opened an inn there, and it has never once been robbed. The stone is still in a strongbox over the bar. Her granddaughter keeps the bar now, and bookies still quote odds on the box. A commoner holding a big monster core is the most frightening sight a lord will ever see, and this page is about why, and about the much larger number of people for whom it is merely Tuesday’s wages.

At a Glance

What it is. A monster’s core, which crews and renderers call the stone, is in a way its Soul Crystal: the seat of the beast’s nature, hardened over its life into glassy stuff in its own color. It is the lawful Crystal Draw (Grade IV stock, rendered from something that carried a soul) of The Standing Index, and worth more the stronger the monster.

How it looks. A lump of colored glass with a slow movement of light inside it, warm to the hand, with fine bands in it like the rings of a tree. The color is the hue of the Wellspring the beast ran on: ember orange off Pyreveil, the deepest teal off Thaloré, ochre-gold off Basilithe, chlorophyll green off Verdantia. A dead stone is gray.

What it costs. A crew, usually. See below for what it pays them.

What it becomes. An alchemist renders it into Essence Drafts, about a dozen doses at the grade it serves, drunk as a course to feed tempering and lift the drinker’s Level (the melting-down and rebuilding of the soul, layer by layer, that refines Essence and leaves the body stronger and carrying Traits). Swallowed raw it gives everything at once.

Example. Tib Hask, porter, goes back into a Green Sigil gallery for the C-Grade stone the crew’s captain left in a Greater Wolf. It goes in his shirt, walnut-sized and faintly warm. The hall’s clerk writes him a chit with the date and the beast’s name, and a rendering house by the hall pays him four and a half gold, which is more than he has held in his life. He spends one night with the stone against his skin before he sells it, and for a week afterward he does not like dogs.

Where It Sits

An animal has a soul with all three layers, as the register’s table sets out, and it lives on the first of them. Its Essence Core (the seat of its own nature) is awake, and its Aether Shell, the door through which Aether comes in and a working goes back out into the world of matter, is sealed, the same as an unwoken farmer’s. What a beast takes in from the ground and from its kills has nowhere to go. So the Essence Core lays it down, kill by kill and year by year, as hard bands around the old center, and that is the stone. Evolution or a Level threshold may crack the seal, which is when a beast starts to show an aura and put something behind its howl, and even then the crack is a small vent on a great store. Monsters gain Levels by killing, each other included, and an old one has done a great deal of tempering of its own, all of it still in there.

Monsters on the Three Roads

A monster that has cracked its seal leans on one layer of its soul, as a person does, and the register’s Leans on line says which. How the thing lives sets the lean. The kind of Wellspring it fed on only tips it, so a stone’s color tells you its Wellspring and never, by itself, its road. The lean decides how the thing fights, and it decides what its stone is made of, because the Essence Core bands up whatever the beast lived on.

What the hunters say. A Body monster kills the crew in front of it. A Spirit monster kills the one at the back who was not warded. A Fate monster kills nobody, and the crew is dead by Thursday anyway, of a wheel, a well-rope and a kitchen fire. The Saffron Hoods took a jinx-hare the size of a calf on the Hask Moor in 695, clean, with no losses, and sold the skin off its stone in a cellar in Verath for eleven hundred gold. Within the month four of the seven were dead of separate accidents and the buyer’s house had burned. The hall posts no bounty on a Fate monster. It posts a warning, and the bookies will not quote it.

The breakthrough bottle follows the road. A breakthrough Draft has to supply the Catalyst the buyer’s own road demands, so the house asks which layer the client leans on before it quotes. A Body buyer’s bottle is poured on a Salt base, out of Body stones. A Spirit buyer’s is poured on Core stock, the finer the better. A Fate buyer’s needs the Layer reagent, which makes it the dearest of the three and the only one that is a hanging matter before the cork is drawn. A soul that walks two roads buys for the one it leads on.

Reading the road off a stone. Weigh it, and feel it. Heavy and warm is a Body stone, light and cool is a Spirit stone, and a stone that makes the dice on the next table behave oddly should be put down and reported, which it never is. The price table below is the same for all three by Grade, and a Fate skin sells apart from its stone, by private treaty, for more than the stone.

Cutting. A stone comes out hot from the bigger beasts. A crew’s cutter, generally whoever has the strongest stomach and worst sense of smell, uses a long hook and a leather sling for anything at A or above, because skin contact with a fresh one starts the leak (see Keeping it, below). Most stones go home in somebody’s shirt regardless, since a shirt is what people have.

What It Is Worth

The stone is read off the beast. The coil (the brass assay instrument) reads a stone and a carcass at the beast’s own Grade and Level, the way it reads any lot on a counter, and that read sets the Stage the stone serves and what it fetches. The Commission posts every beast at a Grade of its own as well, E to EX, the same letters lords post bounties by, and the whole trade uses the posted Grade as shorthand for danger and price, because for most beasts the two line up. Every table on this page is written in that shorthand. Older hall rolls give the same rows as Threat Tiers, T1 to T9, and a hunter of the old school still calls an S-Grade beast a T6.

Where they part, the coil wins, because the posted letter also counts what a beast does to a crew, a road or a coast, and none of that is in the stone. A Lamp-drinker is posted at S for what it does in the dark, and it comes off a floor of D-Grade stock with a hardest blow at Hamlet-bane, so it reads B at the very best and pays out a hen’s egg, whatever the hunters expected for a Gold Sigil. The Leviathan is posted at EX for its reach, its plate and its tie to the deep, and its hardest surge is a March-bane’s, so its stone reads S to SS and serves Stages VIII to X. A very large stone it is, and nobody will sell it to you.

The price table. Prices are raw, on the coil, at a renderer’s door, for a clean stone with a hall’s chit. Monsters level and evolve, so the coil prices the beast on the day it died rather than the row its kind usually sits in. An old wolf with a name can fetch the row above. The figures here are those of What Things Cost, and the rest of the page explains them.

A house pays between a third and a half of what the course will fetch poured, and a stone rendered in a hunting town sells a third under the city price, having been nearer the beast. A famous monster’s core carries a premium for the name, on top of any bounty, and the bounty is usually the smaller part. Hide, bone and scale sell separately (Hide, Horn and Bone), for about as much again while fresh, falling every day from the kill.

What the shares look like. A crew argues about all of it. Level moves by danger overcome, so killing something stronger moves it more than a hundred weak kills, and everybody wants to have landed the blow. The money follows the instrument, which by custom gives the captain two shares, the caller and the fork one and a half, each fighter one, and the porter a wage and nothing else. The hall takes its tithe at the door. The hall’s own tally of crew arguments that have ended in a killing, by Grade, is a very popular document in Verath.

How Fast It Dies

A stone begins to die when the beast does. With no heart behind it and no ground feeding it, Essence seeps out through its skin, the outer bands first, and the color drains inward. Small stones go fast and great stones hardly seem to go at all. Rendering houses read the bands with a glass and a coil, and they pay by what is left.

Keeping it. Salt and cold about double the times above, which is why stones from the Cold Larder go to the hall in a sack of snow. Heat halves them. On ground of its own Wellspring (rich with the same kind of Aether it ran on) a stone stops fading altogether and may even take a little back, so the richest vaults in the world are built on top of worked Cores (a Core, in the street’s sense, being a site where one Wellspring’s Aether pools, and worth more than the entire vault). On ground its Wellspring hates, or the ground of one of its Contraries (each entry in the Sixty names both), it dies twice as fast. In a shirt, against a living person, it leaks into you.

A stale stone is the dangerous one. What seeps away is Essence. The beast’s nature stays behind in the bands, so a half-dead stone renders fewer doses and every dose has twice the wolf in it. Stale stock dressed with a fresh color is to the core trade what spoiled Cutwater is to the apothecary, and people who buy cheap courses from a stranger’s cart are the ones who end up living in the barn.

Gray stones have no Essence worth pouring. They still go to the ward trade by weight, where carry is all anyone is buying (carry being what a creature’s life leaves in the material rendered out of it), and a child in a rim town can get a few copper for a sackful.

Why the sizes matter, for those who ask. A Measurewright will tell you the fade runs like a pot cooling, since what a stone holds goes as its bulk and what it loses goes as its skin. Double the width and you have eight times the stock behind four times the skin. Stronger stones are also denser, and that does the rest. This account is correct as far as it goes, and it does not explain the X-Grade stone at Sealhall that has been putting on weight.

The Bench: Rendering

The full chain is in Alchemetrica, under The Core at the Bench. Here is what a client should understand before paying for it.

  1. The read. Beast, grade, carry and age, off the bands. The Appraisal skill and the coil, and a good renderer’s nose. A careful house enters the beast’s name and the crew’s chit against the lot.
  2. The burn and the Parting. The stone goes through the first Turnings of the bench, burned and taken and parted, and what comes off the Parting is Essence with the beast’s nature still in it.
  3. The washings. Here is the entire art. Each washing lowers the carry and loses Essence with it, and the house decides with the client how much wolf to leave in.
  4. The pour. By the stone’s road: a Body stone pours mostly as Salts for the Shell, a Spirit stone as Core stock, and a Fate stone as Core stock once its skin is off. Core stock refills a Spirit reserve and a Fate one alike, both being Essence, and no house pours a fight Draft for the Fate road. Bottled to the Stage it serves, with the beast’s Grade on the seal, and usually the beast’s name, because people like to know.

The course

A course is twelve doses, one every few days up to the melt. Essence refines in the Soul Crystal, the body gains, Level climbs, and a neighbor who did it the slow way on a good hill gets the same lines on the window in four times the years. What the course gives is Level; the Catalyst that opens the next Stage is a dearer bottle, or a life.

Identity Contamination

The window does not report it. The soul making the report is the soul it happened to, and it feels fine.

Identity Contamination is the beast’s way of being coming across with its Essence, a band at a time. A Trait is a law a soul survived into existence, and carry is a law something else survived. A melt cools around whatever seeds it finds, so tempering on core stock grows parts of a person around an animal’s convictions. Serious houses keep a tally of doses against each client’s name, and every other house keeps a story about someone who growls.

Going Raw

A stone can be swallowed or broken raw, with no alchemist and no fee. The trade’s phrase is going raw, and it is used mostly of the dead.

Everything arrives at once: every band the beast laid down in its life, all the Essence and all of the nature, with no Parting and no washing, into a Soul Crystal that was built to hold a good deal less. The Crystal goes to the melt on the spot. If the stone is near the eater’s own Stage the odds are merely bad. If it is several Stages above, the Shell scalds, the Crystal cracks along whatever it was feeling at the time, and what comes out is built on that.

The wrong ones are the ones crews tell stories about. Hob Wendle of Rimward ate an S-Grade stone off a tunneling thing at Nine Wells in 640, and came up three Stages with the strength of a siege ram and the need to dig. He dug out through the floor of his own house that night and through two neighbors’ cellars by morning. He was still recognizably Hob, he kept apologizing, and his voice could be heard coming up through the well for most of a year.

The whole ones are why it keeps happening. Sister Wenlyn Oake, a kitchen servant at a sect in the Violet Peaks, swallowed an A-Grade stone in 655 with the elders’ dogs at the door, and walked out through them at Stage VI with a Level the swallow itself had paid for, since nothing she had ever faced was as dangerous as that stone, a Skill column with nothing in it but Soup, and a coat in a blazing kingfisher blue. It takes a bold person to hunt somebody like that, and the sect did, for eleven years. A raw climb has all the gaps of a bought one, with no Release until it is found and mastered by fighting, and the hunters come quickly.

Why lords care. A raw stone in a commoner’s fist is the one thing a lord’s whole apparatus of grain, land, debt and Draft supply has no hold on, and it costs nothing to threaten. Most realms therefore hang people for holding an unrendered stone above B without a hall’s chit, and lords have burned villages on the rumor of one. Nem’s terms have been studied by more peasants than the catechism.

The Crime

A Crystal cut from a dead practitioner makes the strongest Draft of all. It is the worst crime in every realm, and the most profitable, and it is why Grade IV has never balanced on the Index. No monster ever tempered as deliberately as a person who spent a lifetime at it. A beast lays down what happens to it, while a practitioner chooses, for decades, what to become, and that is what the buyer gets.

The Dead Man’s Cordial, as the trade says when it is sure of the room, runs several times the price of a core lot of the same grade. It tempers faster than anything alive. It also carries a person, identity-shaped and complete, and a drinker may wake with a stranger’s way of seeing, a grudge against someone they have never met, or a very good left-hand parry and a language nobody at home can place. Buyers report that it is good company.

How it passes. A practitioner’s Crystal comes out with all three of its layers grown, the Essence Core, the Aether Shell and the Attraction Layer. A beast had all three as well, but its Shell never opened wide and its Layer never thickened, so an ordinary stone is banded Core and nothing else. A Body monster’s stone is no exception to that: the Charge it packed has set into the Core’s bands as Salt, and there is no Shell on it to shave. The thin skin on a Fate monster’s stone is the one exception. So the first job is done by a dresser, who shaves away what remains of the Shell and Layer, buffs the surface, and stains in some bands. The shavings have a market of their own, and The Common Drafts says what it buys. After that it reads the same on the coil. A rendering house can still spot one on the bench if it cares to look, and the two-instrument comparison described in The Standing Index would catch it on the shelf: the coil’s Holding and the fork’s density will not reconcile. Most benches own one instrument. Then there is a chit that says wyvern, from a factor three towns away, and a house that has decided not to ask.

Where they come from. Undeclared wars, first of all, since a declared one has a quartermaster counting. Battlefields after dark, behind the licensed rendering houses, which may take the flesh, the bone and the residue of the war dead and never the Crystal (see The Crossing). Hanged men whose families cannot pay a guard. Duelists’ graves. Sect elders, sometimes, from their own students. Famous fighters above all, because a Crystal with a name on it sells for what a kingdom sells for, and every generation has its scandal in which a great family’s fortunes improved abruptly after a war.

The wake. This crime is why a practitioner’s body is never left alone. Across most of the Inner World the kin sit an armed wake, weapons across their knees and lamps lit, in the dead one’s school colors, until the Crystal has been broken by a relative’s hand or the pyre has burned down, and the wake is often the best party the family has thrown in years. A Moto house speaks to its dead and keeps them under guard while it does. Marrowkin enter the Crystal in the Tally. Kharven families have been known to sell theirs quietly and hold the wake anyway. A person who dies with no one to sit up with them is what the night-cutters look for.

The law. Every realm hangs for it, and most hang the bench and the buyer too on paper. In practice the cutter hangs. The three old warrants for rendering the human dead (see The Standing Index) reach the body and stop at the Crystal, and a surprising number of great houses have had that explained to them at length, under seal, without consequence.

Penning, its cousin, is lawful in some places and ought to worry you more. Monsters gain Levels by killing. So you can raise a line in a pit, feed it things to kill, stronger each season, and cut it at the top of its growth for a far better stone than its row would ever fetch. The Hundred Crowns have kings who do this. Most of what gets fed to the pit is goats. The Accord has struck three charters over the rest of it, and the fourth is pending. Every pen in the record has ended the same way, and the only variable is how far the town was from the pit.
