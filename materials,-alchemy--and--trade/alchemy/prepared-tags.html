Prepared Tags

Prepared Tags

Nov 17, 202612 min read

The common tags of the field kit, as cut by licensed hands, copied by unlicensed ones and guarded by the schools · prices in Accord marks

A wall cut by an Engraver holds for three hundred years. A tag holds for three weeks and fires once, and in a fight that is the better bargain. Twelve tags make up most of what is carried into battle.

Tamsin Cordwain warranted tags at Quenby for forty-one years under a mark the whole county could sketch from memory, and her will asked for one thing: that she go into the ground the way she had lived, loudly and at her own expense. So on the morning they buried her, forty apprentices in amber sashes lined the Cutters’ Stair with the four hundred and twelve tags laid up in her strongbox, every one of them fired on the air or on a stored charge, and as the bier came down they touched them off one by one. Flash tags went up the Stair like a string of small white suns. Smoke rolled down the steps a room’s worth at a time. A Hush tag dropped the whole hill into silence for a few held breaths, and two Blast tags took the weathercock off the chandlery, by prior arrangement with the chandler. Under all of it ran the thing every cutter knows and few ever see in bulk: an ambient or stored tag lights in its maker’s color, so that for a quarter of an hour, three days dead, Tamsin Cordwain went on signing her name up the length of the Stair in honey amber, and grown men wept into their hats.

Then the ninety-first tag lit spark blue, and so did the ninety-second.

In the second rank of mourners, in his good black, stood Fulk Pyke, Warden of the Company of the Slate, whose own color every cutter in Quenby had been looking at across a feud for thirty years. Twenty-seven tags in that box carried Tamsin Cordwain’s mark and burned Fulk Pyke’s blue. She had spent nine years tracing them through the ports, each one a forgery of her warranty that had already killed somebody or would, and in her last month she had bought twenty-seven fresh off the boats, through a nephew, and left instructions about the order in which they should be laid out. Forty apprentices turned around. The fight went down the Stair and through the churchyard with a hundred people in it and every sleeve in the trade emptying at once. Three mourners died on those steps and a fourth went home without his jaw. Pyke ran with his coat alight and the Accord hanged him at the autumn assize, and the Slate and the Cordwain apprentices still break each other’s windows on her day. The children of Quenby count out their games by it: Amber holds and amber stays, blue will hang you all your days.

That funeral is a working knowledge of tags, delivered at volume. What a tag is, who can fire it, what color it lights and why the mark on it matters are set out below, with fewer casualties.

What it is

A tag (a slap or a strip in any tavern with fighters in it) is a rune (a working written into a material so that it holds without its maker) cut fast onto paper, leather or slate, carried in a sleeve, a belt or a boot, and fired in a single beat. A wall needs a season because it has to stand for centuries. A tag needs an hour because it only has to stand until it is used, and its last line is its own burning: when the tag is spent the material is ash, and the working ends with it. Cutting is a skill and a trade like any other. Anyone with a steady hand and a teacher can level it, it shows on the cutter’s window (the soul reporting its numbers to its owner, unseen by anyone else) as a Grade, and a cutter’s guild title caps what they may lawfully sell under their mark.

Only practitioners can fire a tag. The trigger is a touch of the user’s own Essence (the stuff a working is made of) through a woken Soul Crystal (the soul itself, opened for use), with a word or a hand sign. An unwoken hand holding a tag is holding paper.

A tag is also why low-Stage fighters love them. Below Refraction (Stage VII, the seventh rung of the sixteen-step ladder of power) every working needs something outside the body to hold its shape: a voice, a hand, ink or blood. A tag is that ink, already written. The user supplies the trigger and the tag supplies the rest.

Every tag runs on one of three fuels, and the tag decides which:

FuelWhere the power comes fromWhat it means in a fight
Ambient AetherThe magic in the air around it, at the room’s Density (how much Aether, the raw stuff of magic, a place holds)Cheap and never stale. Fails in dead ground (salted ground, worked by hedge-workers so Aether will not pool on it; bled ground, pulled empty; a place beside a Core, a worked Wellspring site, whose keepers have closed it; or a place no Wellspring’s Aether reaches) and weakens anywhere thin
The user’s reserveThe Essence the person firing it can spend before running dryAs strong as the user, up to the maker’s Stage. It costs the user, and the more they feed it the longer it holds
The maker’s stored chargeReserve the cutter poured into the tag when it was madeWorks anywhere and for anyone who can fire it. Goes stale: strongest the day it is cut, weaker each week, dead in a season

A tag can never work above its maker’s Stage. A reserve-fed tag works at whichever is lower, the maker’s Stage or the user’s.

How it looks

Fine lines cut or burned into the material, sometimes inked, always in a fixed order the eye can follow if it knows the school. Paper is cheap, quick and dies in the rain. Leather lasts months and survives a soaking. Slate is heavy, lasts a year, and shatters when it fires.

As it fires, the lines light up in an aura color: the maker’s own color for an ambient or stored tag, the user’s color for a reserve tag. A trained eye reads who is paying for the working from the color alone. Then the material chars from the edges inward, with the smell of hot paper or scorched hide, and each kind of tag has its own sound: the flash tag’s crack, the ward tag’s glassy ring, the blast tag’s rising whine in the last second before it goes.

A tag is slapped onto a surface or a body, laid in advance, or wrapped round a knife and thrown.

What it costs

Coin. Tags compete with enhanced bolts (crossbow bolts carrying a working) and are priced against them, by the maker’s Stage:

Maker’s StagePrice, one tagNote
I · Murmuring4 to 8 copperPenny tags. One in three fails
II · Welling2 to 4 silverThe field standard; about the price of one enhanced bolt
III · Ascension1 to 2 goldBought singly, by people who expect to live because of it
IV · Flourishing3 to 6 goldOfficers’, bounty hunters’ and sect disciples’ kit
V · Splintering9 to 18 goldRarely on a counter
VI and up25 gold and upNot sold. Issued, inherited, or stolen

Failure. Cheap tags fail, and they fail three ways. Most often the tag fizzles: the lines glow and nothing comes. Sometimes it fires wrong: the ward comes up a hand’s breadth to the left, the smoke comes out as sparks. Rarely, and worst, it does not stop: the closing line was cut badly, so the working runs on after the tag should be ash, a flash tag that keeps flashing, a kindling tag that burns until the building does.

The license. Licensed tags carry the cutter’s mark (a cutter’s mark is a signature and a warranty at once) and an excise stamp. Cutting without a license is a crime the Accord enforces with real violence, and the black market runs regardless.

How it is beaten

  • Wet it. A soaked paper tag swells and its lines blur. Leather and slate shrug it off.
  • Cut the line. A tag torn through its lines will not fire, or fires wrong. A blade across a stuck tag is the fastest answer to a Binding or a Seal.
  • Take the fight off rich ground, onto salted, bled or thin ground. Ambient tags need Aether in the air.
  • Read the color. A reserve tag tells you who is feeding it. Hit them, and the tag starves.
  • Wait it out. A stored-charge tag weakens by the week. An old tag from a dead cutter is a gamble.
  • Watch the hand. Slapping, laying or throwing a tag takes a beat, and the hand that goes to the sleeve is a tell.

Example

Orla Venn, a Stage III bounty hunter, walks into a warehouse after a forger. She lays a Tripwire Tag across the back door, keeps a Flash Tag in her left sleeve and a Binding Tag in her right. The forger runs, the Tripwire shrieks, and she throws the Flash Tag wrapped round a knife; it cracks white in his face. She closes and slaps the Binding Tag on his thigh, and the lines light up in her own color as she feeds it. The forger’s partner sees the color, knows who is paying, and goes for Orla instead of the tag. She has to choose between holding the leg and defending her throat, and the tag goes dark the moment she lets go.

Who cuts them

  • Licensed cutters. Accord-licensed hands working under a master’s mark, selling over a counter with an excise stamp. Most tags on the kept streets (the city quarters whose lamps and wards are kept by hired crafts) come from them, and the Accord guards the monopoly hard. Stage brackets bar purchased tags outright, which has done nothing for the trade in tags sewn into a fighter’s hat.
  • The black market. Unlicensed cutters, failed apprentices and forgers of other men’s marks. Cheaper, unwarranted, and the source of most tags that do not stop.
  • The sects. The schools and great houses of the East keep their best tag forms as secrets, taught by rank, and a school’s tag-hand can be spotted at fifty paces by the way the sleeve is cut. A disciple who leaves with a Binding or an Anchor form in their hands is hunted for what they know.

In the north almost nobody cuts. What tags the north carries come from Accord quartermasters, and a northern soldier pays for them in kind.
