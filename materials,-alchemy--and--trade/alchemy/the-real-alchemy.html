What the laboratory did, from the fourteenth century to the end of the nineteenth, with the figures the sources give. An out-of-world reference page.

In 1810 the British warship HMS Triumph, at Cadiz with a cargo of mercury aboard, sprang the worst kind of leak there is. The metal got loose below decks. Within three weeks the men’s gums had swelled and their mouths were running with spit, and about two hundred of the 587 aboard fell ill. The animals aboard died. Coins and watches came up silvered, the ship’s small change plated by the air everyone was breathing, and you could have drawn the disease on a plan of the berths: the men under the forecastle got off with sore gums, and of those who berthed below, the midshipmen came off lightest, because their duties kept them on deck. Nobody smelled a thing, because mercury vapor has no smell. Most of the salivated men were back aboard after sixteen to twenty-seven days ashore, to a ship poisoned with a metal the physicians of the age were prescribing by the ounce.

That is the real trade, and nothing anyone has invented for it is louder. Isaac Newton and his assistant sat up on alternate nights for six weeks every spring and six more every autumn, feeding a fire that scarcely went out, and when his dated experiments stopped he stood, by his own plan, at least ten steps short of the stone. Herman Boerhaave distilled the same mercury five hundred times, broke retorts along the way, and got no gold for it. In 1658, with antimony ninety-two years under the ban of the Paris Faculty of Medicine, the physician Vallot gave Louis XIV emetic wine at Calais and the king got up cured, after which the Parlement of Paris needed eight more years to lift the ban. An Edinburgh chemist named Stewart broke a vessel of fuming nitric acid in 1863, within hours he and his assistant could hardly breathe, and both of them died. And in a modern laboratory, working from a recipe in George Starkey’s notebook, Lawrence Principe came in one morning and found a metallic tree standing in his sealed flask.

The Operations

Calcination

The practice. A solid was held at strong heat in open air, in a pan, a crucible or a reverberatory, until it fell to a calx. Lemery calcined tin in a wide unglazed earthen pan over a great fire for thirty-six hours, stirring now and then. The same tin in a crucible took four days. The pan spread the metal thin (Lemery 1686). Gold cemented between layers of a salt paste took ten to twelve hours of violent heat, and a red powder fixed on gold leaf took three days in the reverberatory (Newman 2019). Lavoisier held four ounces of mercury just under its boil for twelve days in 1777. Red specks showed on the second day. At the end he had about forty-five grains of red calx, and the air in the vessel had lost about a sixth of its volume (Lavoisier 1777).

The fault. Calcination is slow and its yield is small. The result follows how long the fire was kept, far more than how fierce it was. A calx also changes as it burns: Lemery found that longer calcination of red precipitate lightened it and made it milder.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Burning runs four hours to three days (The Twelve Turnings; Time, and What It Costs). Real calcinations ran from thirty-six hours (Lemery’s tin in a pan) and four days (the same tin in a crucible) to Lavoisier’s twelve days, past the Burning’s upper figure. The law under the Burning is Cinerion’s. The Burning’s smell of hot stone and scorched bone, and the fume not to be breathed, match Lemery’s bench.

Solution

The practice. A solid was taken into a liquid, the menstruum. Newton’s standing routine dissolved his matter in aqua fortis with stibnite and sal ammoniac over heat, then decanted and filtered the liquor and boiled it dry. A heavy calx usually stayed undissolved at the bottom (Newman 2019). Lemery soaked corrosive sublimate in spirit of wine, cold, for six or seven hours. He noted that iron would not go into plain water if it were boiled a month, and went readily once the water carried tartar (Lemery 1686).

The fault. The menstruum decides everything. The wrong one leaves the matter untouched, and an impure stock dissolves into something the worker did not expect.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Taking is the Turning where a workshop learns its stock was not what the seller said. The historical bench met impure stock at the same step. The Taking is the bench’s Dissolution, and Dissolution is at once a Wellspring, a named operation and a stage of the Work.

Separation

The practice. The bench parted what the solution had mixed. It decanted clear liquor off a settled sediment, filtered through a cone of brown paper, precipitated, and washed the precipitate until the washings ran clean. That washing was called edulcoration (Lemery 1686; Newman 2019). Lixiviation drew the fixed salt of a plant. The residue left after distilling was calcined in open fire until the black soot burned off it, and the salt was leached out of the ash with water (Lemery 1686). Precipitation could be slow: Lemery’s silver Tree of Diana, a cold metallic precipitation, stood still in its matrass “in some convenient place” for forty days (Lemery 1686).

The fault. Every step loses matter. Paper holds liquor, washings carry salts away, and a careless decant takes the sediment with it.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Parting is filtration and decanting, slow and unglamorous, where most of a journeyman’s early years are spent. The historical parting was as slow, and it lost matter at every step. The law it draws on is Judicium, discernment, which is why a good parting hand and a good liar-catcher are sometimes the same person.

Fermentation

The practice. Lemery defined fermentation as a boiling-up raised by spirits forcing their way out of a body, which changes its nature. Fruit, honey, berries, seeds, leaves and flowers could be set to ferment like wine. Dry matter was beaten, wetted, and given a little yeast to hasten it. Distilling the ferment gave a burning spirit (Lemery 1686).

The fault. A ferment runs on its own clock and its own heat. It can foam over, sour, or stall, and a sealed ferment builds gas.

In WOTR. The Alftian Codex draws Furveus’s Mercury as the spirit from the ferment, which is Lemery’s route. The Codex’s seven stages of the Work (Volume the Second) place Fermentation fifth, and the stage draws on Rebirthine.

Distillation

The practice. Heat raised vapor from the charge. The cold of the head or the neck brought it back down as liquid, drop by drop, into a receiver. Lemery distilled balm in a copper still with a tinned head, its joint luted with wet bladder. The fire was of the second degree. He kept it between the second and third degree, because too low a fire would not raise the volatile part. Too high, and the water tasted burned. The trade called that empyreuma. The drops were to come slowly, one after another (Lemery 1686).

The fault. Distillation separates by heat alone, and heat is a blunt blade. Everything volatile at that heat comes over together, and a hot fire scorches the charge.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Distillation separates a signal from noise, and without a Direction glyph it separates every signature present. Lemery’s still had the same failing with no glyph to help it. The law Distillation draws on is Sublimare’s, the spirit risen and returned, and the trade reads the spirit that finds its own road up the neck as Cymorath.

The strong waters

The practice. The bench made its own acids by distillation, and the recipes are exact.

  • Aqua fortis (nitric acid), literally “strong water.” Lemery charged a luted retort with saltpetre, white-calcined vitriol and dried clay, thirty-two ounces of each, and left a third of the retort empty. He warmed it by degrees. When red clouds passed into the receiver he held that degree fifteen or sixteen hours, then pushed the fire to its extremity until the clouds turned white (Lemery 1686). Assayers used it to part silver from gold (Wikipedia, “Nitric acid”).
  • Aqua regia (“royal water”). It was first made by dissolving sal ammoniac in aqua fortis, a method in pseudo-Geber’s De inventione veritatis of about 1300. It dissolves gold (Wikipedia, “Aqua regia”) (single source).
  • Sal ammoniac (ammonium chloride). It is a white crystal raised by sublimation, used as a flux and as the salt that turns aqua fortis into aqua regia (Wikipedia, “Aqua regia”; Newman 2019).
  • Oil of vitriol (sulfuric acid). Eighteen or twenty hours was not enough. The oil came over only after three days of continuous distilling. Lemery ran one retort of German vitriol three days and three nights. In his remarks on aqua fortis he adds that a fire kept five days and nights would still leave the receiver full of fume (Lemery 1686).
  • Spirit of salt (hydrochloric acid). Glauber made it from common salt and oil of vitriol. Its by-product, Glauber’s salt, is dated to his discovery about 1625 in one account and to his published process in De natura salium of 1658 in another, and the date is disputed (Wikipedia, “Johann Rudolf Glauber”; Ahonen, DSB). The fault. Every one of these is made hot and delivered as fume. The luting has to hold for a day or three, and the receiver fills with the thing that kills.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Menstrua carry all five. Strongwater is aqua fortis, down to the saltpetre and vitriol, the day of graduated heat, the red-orange fume, and gold left untouched. Kingswater is aqua regia, Ghostsalt is sal ammoniac, Blackstone Oil is oil of vitriol with its three days, and Salt-spirit is spirit of salt. Copper “eaten alive by Strongwater in under an hour” has no period figure behind it. Real rates turned on the acid’s strength and the metal’s thickness.

Cohobation

The practice. The distillate was poured back on the residue in the vessel and distilled again, over and over, “to open Bodies, or to Volatilize the Spirits” (Lemery 1686; Wikipedia, “Cohobation”). It differs from circulation because the liquor is drawn off each time. Newton digested spirit of wine on a sublimed salt twenty-four hours, then distilled it seven times or more (Newman 2019). Boerhaave carried repetition to its limit. He distilled the same mercury five hundred times, 448 of them in one series, lost weight to broken retorts and cleaning, and got no transmutation (Boerhaave 1730s).

The fault. Modern chemistry finds cohobation does nothing that holding the charge at heat for the same time would not do (Wikipedia, “Cohobation”). Each pass also costs matter and risks the glass.

In WOTR. No counterpart. Alchemetrica names no repeated-return distillation, and the Standing Index’s nine recrystallizations under witness are a different operation.

Circulation

The practice. In a pelican or a sealed double vessel, heat drove vapor up and the cold of the arms brought it back into the charge, without the liquor ever being drawn off. The aim was to refine a spirit or to open a hard body (Lemery 1686; Brunschwig 1500). Rupescissa’s quintessence was wine distilled repeatedly and then circulated in a closed glass for “a long period,” and period recipes rarely give a time (Stewart 2013). In the adept’s menstruum Fatio described, five pounds of matter gave four and a half in the receiver, which stood several days in a cold place before its vapors settled (Newman 2019).

The fault. A closed circulation cannot be tasted, measured or corrected. A crack in the arm ends it, and so does a fire that falters.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s circulation in the Returner runs turns or months, the vessel never opened. The Alftian Codex runs Furveus’s spirit in the Returner for most of a month. Both sit inside the recorded order of time.

Sublimation

The practice. A volatile solid was driven up by heat and caught as a solid on the cold upper walls or in the head. For corrosive sublimate, Lemery’s matrass poured red nitrous vapor from its mouth for at least three hours, and the earthy residue at the bottom weighed twenty-eight ounces (Lemery 1686). The Treatise Newton copied sublimed on a mild fire for three days. It left a caput mortuum at the bottom, a cinnabar in the middle and a white sublimate at the top. The product was mixed and sublimed again two or three more times. Newton graded his own sublimations from a candle, to a sand bath, to the naked fire on a fire shovel, to be sure all that could rise had risen (Newman 2019).

The fault. Sublimation leaves a residue and layers its product. A single pass is rarely clean, so the real trade sublimed again and again.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Rising collects on the cold walls of the Riser, and what failed to rise is lost. There is no partial Sublimation. What did not rise stays as a fused mass broken out with a chisel. The historical bench sublimed partially and repeatedly, and its unrisen residue is the period counterpart of that fused mass. Canon names no sublimation outside the Sublimatio operation, whose law elevates a thing without passing through the states between, with no stage at which the working can be inspected or corrected.

Coagulation, congelation and fixation

The practice. Coagulation gave a liquid a body by evaporating it or by mixing two liquors. Congelation was letting a melt set (Lemery 1686). Fixation made a volatile thing stand the fire. Boerhaave took the fire acting through the glass for the cause that fixed his mercury powder. When he then held that powder red-hot for four hours in a retort coated with sand and clay, most of it came over as running mercury, and of seventeen ounces of mercury worked in all, fifteen grains stayed fixed (Boerhaave 1730s).

The fault. A thing “fixed” by one fire may rise again under a hotter one. The word named a result at a stated heat, and the heat was rarely stated.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Coagulation mirrors Coagulatio, and its Setting is the Turning where process becomes object. Lemery’s and Boerhaave’s operations are the physical register those entries name. Coagulatio and Fixatio are both among the six Wellsprings that are also named operations: every Sealing chain on the bench runs under Fixatio’s law, and the Bench files a Fixatio Draft as a Coagulation.

Conjunction

The practice. At the bench, conjunction was the joining of prepared matters in one vessel. Gold was amalgamated with a prepared mercury, or the “Secret’s” ferment went into its menstruum, four ounces to one, between two matrasses sealed mouth to mouth with lute in a sand bath (Newman 2019). The vessel was then sealed and digested for weeks. Principe, working from a recipe in Starkey’s notebook, put the prepared mercury and gold, worked to a buttery lump, into a sealed flask in a heated sand bath, and one morning found a metallic tree grown in it. The report gives no elapsed time (Conniff 2014).

The fault. The danger was pressure in sealed glass. Newton’s notes record his egg-glass breaking when volatile vitriolic salts rose during a digestion (Spargo 2005).

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Wedding is fast and decisive, and the Alftian Codex’s Furveus pours the principles in one movement with an Aperture glyph at the neck to let pressure out. Recombination at the historical bench, of metals or of plant principles, was a slow sealed digestion (Lemery: days to a fortnight in dung). The fast Wedding is WOTR’s own. The period danger at the joining was pressure in sealed glass, which is what the Aperture answers. Canon’s own danger at the Wedding is Rupture. Conjunction is the one stage with no Wellspring of its own: it runs on Attraction, and a Conjunction corrupted injures Attraction itself, which the Heresiology treats as the Corruption of Instrumental Union.

Cementation, cupellation and parting

The practice. These were the assayer’s operations. Cementation packed gold plate in layers with a salt paste and held it at violent heat ten to twelve hours to purge its base metals (Lemery 1686). Cupellation needs a porous cup of bone ash. Lead oxide melts at 888 °C and soaks into the cup, leaving the silver or gold bead on top, in a furnace at about 960 to 1,000 °C under an oxidizing draft (Wikipedia, “Cupellation”). The stibnite parting, first set out in the German assaying booklets of the early sixteenth century, drew silver and base metals into sulfides and left the gold (Craddock 2000). The excavated laboratory at Oberstockstall, in Lower Austria, held about three hundred triangular crucibles and about forty scorifiers. It held between sixty and a hundred bone-ash cupels, by differing counts (Martinón-Torres and Rehren).

The fault. Each parting carried a little gold away with it. Cupellation also breathed lead fume into the room.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Reading is the assay before any working, and Weight, Measure and the Standard sets the assay pound so a bead’s mass reads directly as fineness. The Working Vocabulary names the cupel, scorification, litharge, the button and the blick as the assay’s trade words, and Alchemetrica keeps Kingswater at every parting bench. The trade reads the blick as Mirithane. Cementation has no counterpart.

The order of the operations

The practice. No two authors agreed on the list. George Ripley’s Compound of Alchymy (1471) gave twelve gates: calcination, solution, separation, conjunction, putrefaction, congelation, cibation, sublimation, fermentation, exaltation, multiplication and projection (Wikipedia, “Magnum opus”; Newman 1996). Samuel Norton in the sixteenth century gave fourteen (Wikipedia, “Magnum opus”). The seven-stage list that runs from calcination to coagulation circulates in modern writing on spiritual alchemy. No early modern source for that order has been traced.

The fault. A list of operations in a book was a teaching order, and often a cover. It omitted steps and repeated others under new names.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Twelve Turnings echo Ripley’s twelve in number, with cibation as the Feeding. The Alftian Codex’s seven stages of the Work are WOTR’s own list and need no real warrant, and they carry a weight the real lists never could: six of the seven draw on a Wellspring’s law.

The stone

The practice. Every long list of operations pointed at a finished thing. Ripley’s twelve gates close on multiplication and projection (Wikipedia, “Magnum opus”; Newman 1996), and the end of the road was a stone or medicine that a small measure of would carry its virtue into a great deal else. The real record of pursuit is the rest of this page: Newton’s fires, stopped by his own plan at least ten steps short (Newman 2019), and Boerhaave’s five hundred distillations of one mercury with no gold at the end of them (Boerhaave 1730s).

The fault. Nobody on these sources made it. The claims that survive are claims.

In WOTR. The stone has a counterpart, and it works. A Master Work is a piece that does what its Grade and material should not be able to, made once at the end of a lifetime, always Unique or Mythic, and named, and the bench’s own are Drafts. The great one is the Fate Essence Draft, which bottles a share of the Plane of Fate, the control of Attraction Force and Obsession Force and of what the odds are allowed to do. Its account is in Alchemetrica, under Master Works of the Bench. Its real kin is the stone’s reputation: extremely hard, very coveted, the prize of a lifetime, and the reason a great house will pay for decades of a fire nobody may let go out. The real trade’s goal was gold, and WOTR’s money runs the other way. Most of what a WOTR house actually pours is Essence Drafts from monster cores, for which the real bench has no counterpart at all.

The Apparatus and the Fire

The degrees of fire

The practice. Lemery counted four degrees of fire by fuel, not by number (Lemery 1686):

  • the first, two or three lit coals, the gentlest;
  • the second, three or four coals, the vessel sensibly warm but bearable under a hand for a while;
  • the third, enough fire to boil a pot of five or six quarts of water;
  • the fourth, coals and wood together, the fiercest. Sand, iron filings and ash made graded beds, each with degrees of its own, and ash was the mildest because it holds little heat. The reverberatory ran hotter than any of them. A “wheel fire” ringed a crucible with live coals for fusion. The Treatise Newton copied set a water-bath digestion at a heat “which you can hardly bear to touch” (Newman 2019).

The fault. A degree measured in coals depends on the coal, the draft and the furnace. Two workshops following one recipe ran two different fires.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Heat says every alchemist judges temperature by flame color, the sound of the charge, and a hand held where a hand should not go. That is Lemery’s method. The conclusion that a written formula never reproduces the product follows from the period practice too. The trade reads the first degree as Ignivale and the climb by degrees as Ascensio, and a practitioner harmonized to either will tell you that he can feel the difference between your third degree and your fourth, and may be right.

Instruments

The practice. Fahrenheit made the first reliable mercury thermometers in 1713 and 1714 and published his scale in 1724 (Wikipedia, “Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit”). Mercury boils at 356.7 °C, which caps the instrument. By the 1730s Boerhaave measured digestion heats on Fahrenheit’s scale and held a fire at 180 °F for months (Boerhaave 1730s). Above red heat there was nothing until Wedgwood’s clay-shrinkage pyrometer of 1782, whose scale was later shown to be badly wrong (Wikipedia, “Wedgwood scale”; Royal Society 2023). Daniell’s expansion pyrometer of 1822 moved a pointer with a platinum rod. It gave the first continuous reading, though a poor one by later standards. Prinsep’s gas pyrometer followed in 1828, Pouillet’s color table in 1836, and Siemens’s resistance pyrometer, built for furnaces, in 1871, though it took years to make reliable. Seger’s pyrometric cones came in 1886. Le Chatelier’s platinum and rhodioplatinum thermocouple of 1886 and 1887 made reliable electrical reading practicable, and his optical pyrometer of 1892 read heats past the melting point of platinum (Darling 1911; Johnson Matthey).

The fault. Every early instrument covered the gentle end or read the fierce end wrongly. The furnace heats that mattered most were measured crudely from the 1820s and reliably only from 1886.

In WOTR. None of these instruments exists in any era: what is built and carried stays medieval. A Dark Ages bench owns brass and glass, assay forks that ring truer as the ground thickens, water clocks for the hours, and the bead-glasses and clay trial-cones Alchemetrica describes, which are the crude cousins of the thermometer and Seger’s cone. They read the bed or the fire, and none of them reads the charge inside a sealed vessel, so heat at the charge stays a craft skill. A high-Stage Measurewright may know through the Aether what a thermocouple would have said, to the degree, and will certainly tell you, and you will still be judging your fire by its color.

The water bath

The practice. The balneum Mariae stood the vessel in water over the fire, so the charge could never pass the boil (Lemery 1686; Sella 2009). The bath is traditionally credited to Maria the Jewess. Zosimos, about 300 CE, credits her with the tribikos, the three-spouted still, and describes the kerotakis. Whether he gave her the bath is disputed, and the name balneum Mariae is medieval, introduced by Arnold of Villanova in the fourteenth century (Wikipedia, “Mary the Jewess”; Sella 2009). Her writings survive only as quotations in later authors (Sella 2009). The name was argued over by the seventeenth century. The Treatise Newton copied offers both Miriam, sister of Moses, and maris, because it boils in waves like the sea (Newman 2019). The vaporous bath set the vessel in the steam of boiling water rather than in the water (Lemery 1686).

The fault. The bath limits itself at the boil, which protects the charge and also caps it. A bath allowed to boil dry stops protecting anything.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Still Bath is the balneum Mariae, self-limiting at the boil. It is named for Ithra, credited in three traditions and attested in none, which has its period parallel in Maria’s bath, a credit carried by a name coined about a thousand years after her. The trade reads the Still Bath as Nyxial, self-limiting whatever the fire below it does. The Cinderhand’s bath that must not go dry is an apprentice’s task on any period bench. The vaporous bath has no counterpart.

Sand, ash and filings

The practice. The vessel was packed round with sand, ash or iron filings in an iron or earthen pan set over the bars (Lemery 1686). Sand ran hotter than water and more evenly than the naked fire. Ash was gentler and slower. Newton ran sublimations in a sand bath with a retort, and Principe buried his sealed flask in a heated sand bath (Newman 2019; Conniff 2014).

The fault. A deep bed lags. It takes long to heat and long to let go, so a correction to the fire reaches the vessel late.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Sand is the workhorse bed and its Ash is slower and gentler, as Lemery ranks them. The Ash holding through a night without tending has no period figure in these sources. The iron-filing bed has no counterpart.

The dung bath

The practice. The flask was buried in fermenting horse dung, the venter equinus, for a long, low, steady heat (Lemery 1686; Wikipedia, “Homunculus”). Lemery prescribed dung digestions of four days, four or five days, and a fortnight, and named lamp-fire, the sun and dung together as the steady heats fit for digesting gold. Brunschwig’s distilling book of 1500 describes distilling in horse dung (Taape 2014). No period measurement of a dung bath survives. Modern measurements of manure and compost give the curve:

  • a hotbed of manure and straw passes 50 °C within days, and a well-built pile holds 61 to 68 °C for a week or more, giving useful heat for about one to two months (single source, gardening literature);
  • the hot stage of composting runs 40 to 60 °C for weeks to months by mass, peaks at 60 to 70 °C in three to five days, sags as the stage ends, and peaks again when turned, until curing sets in (Trautmann 1996);
  • above about 70 °C the risk of self-ignition rises, and piles are broken up at 82 to 93 °C (Rynk 1992). The fault. Dung heat is neither constant nor blood-warm. It starts hot, falls over weeks, has to be turned or renewed, and a large pile can fire itself.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Bed holds blood-warmth for three to four and a half turns (six to nine weeks) without fuel, tending, or any possibility of overheating. The span matches the working life of a dung heat. The even blood-warmth and the safety from overheating are WOTR’s own; a small bed packed round one vessel is the nearest real case. The Bed’s smell, which nobody in the workshop notices after a month, is the dung bath’s own. The trade reads the Bed’s warmth as Anima Spirare, the quickening in which a charge stays alive.

Lamp, sun and the little furnace

The practice. Lemery listed lamp-fire beside sun and dung as the steady digesting heats (Lemery 1686), and Newton asked a correspondent how a collaborator “contrives his Lamp” (Newman 2019). Boerhaave’s little furnace was a small wooden box on the pattern of the Dutch foot-stove. It burned coal or peat that had stopped smoking. It held a moderate heat for up to twenty-four hours and served his “summer-heat” plant distillations, about 85 °F in ARTECHNE’s modern conversion (ARTECHNE).

The fault. A lamp’s heat rises and falls with its wick and its oil, and no period figure for it is recorded in these sources.

In WOTR. No counterpart. Alchemetrica’s Heat table has no lamp furnace and no sun-digestion.

The reverberatory

The practice. Lemery’s standard furnace was fixed brick laid in a lute of one part potter’s earth, one part horse dung and two parts sand, kneaded in water. The walls were two bricks thick to hold the heat. The ash-hole stood a foot high. Two iron bars about a thumb thick carried the retort, a finger’s breadth clear of the walls. Over it sat a dome. Its stoppered hole and foot-high chimney threw the flame back down on the vessel. Portable furnaces were built of three parts powdered potsherd to two of clay (Lemery 1686). Glauber’s Furni novi philosophici, printed between 1646 and 1649, was the first book to give full furnace designs with their dimensions, and it sorted furnaces into five kinds (Mineralogical Record) (single source).

The fault. The reverberatory’s heat is fierce and uneven. It melts glass, which is why Lemery coated glass retorts in lute before they went in.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s open flame with the bellows is the top of the range. The reverberatory dome itself has no named counterpart.

The athanor

The practice. The athanor, also called piger Henricus or “Slow Henry,” was built for uniform, constant digesting heat. The sixteenth-century form had a separate fuel tower so that charcoal slid down into the fire of its own weight, like a magazine stove, and ran unattended (Wikipedia, “Athanor”). The name comes from Arabic al-tannūr, “oven.” Libavius’s ideal chemical house of 1606 gave the laboratory ten furnaces, noted that Tycho Brahe’s held sixteen, and set the athanor apart in a private sanctuary for incubating the philosophical mercury in an egg-shaped vessel (Newman 1999).

The fault. No measured burn time for a charge of the tower is recorded in these sources. The real long runs on record were fires kept by people. Humphrey Newton wrote that the fire in Newton’s laboratory scarcely went out night or day for about six weeks in spring and again in autumn, the two men sitting up on alternate nights (Newman 2019).

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Long Furnace is the athanor, fuel tower and gravity feed and all. Its turns or months without a hand on it outruns the evidence, which shows tended runs of six weeks (Newman 2019) to several months (Boerhaave 1730s), with no unattended burn time measured. The small noise the furnace makes settling as the charcoal drops is the gravity feed’s own, and the trade reads its slow fire as Pyraeon.

The still

The practice. The cucurbit, or body, held the charge. The alembic, or head, sat on it, caught the vapor, and ran the condensate down a beak into the receiver (Veronesi 2021; Newman 2019). Lemery used a copper body tinned inside, with a tinned copper head and a water-cooled worm, for plant waters and essences, and earthen or glass bodies for other work (Lemery 1686). A glass alembic from Oberstockstall survives at 41.8 cm tall (Veronesi 2021). Humphrey Newton listed Newton’s apparatus as bodies, receivers, heads and crucibles, and said only the crucibles saw much use (Newman 2019).

The fault. The joint between body and head is the weak point of the whole train. Copper stills served only waters and essences; acids needed glass or earthenware.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Belly and Hood is the cucurbit and alembic, with the belly in earthenware or copper and the hood in glass because the hood is where the working is watched.

The retort and receiver

The practice. The retort is body and neck in one piece, the vapor condensing in the neck and running into a receiver (Veronesi 2021). Lemery preferred earthen retorts for acid spirits because they stood the utmost fire and would not melt as glass did, and he held that good earthenware had pores as close as glass. Receivers for acid spirits had to be very large, so the spirits had room to circulate. Retorts were filled to about two thirds (Lemery 1686).

The fault. The retort has no joint within itself, and its joint to the receiver still has to be luted. Lemery’s lute set so hard that the joints had to be softened with wet cloths before the retort could be parted.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Retort, one piece of glass with no joint to fail, is Lemery’s retort. The retort-to-receiver joint is the exception, and both Lemery’s hard-set lute and Yworth’s fuming joint failed there.

Matrass and double vessel

The practice. The matrass was a long-necked flask for digestions and sublimations. The neck of one matrass pushed into the neck of another, with the joint well luted, made a double vessel for circulating spirits (Lemery 1686).

The fault. A double vessel doubles its joints, and every luted joint can breathe fume.

In WOTR. No counterpart by name. The Returner does the same work.

The pelican

The practice. The pelican was a circulatory vessel whose one or two curved arms carried condensate back into the body, so the charge was refluxed and never drawn off (Brunschwig 1512; Princeton University Library). It is drawn in Brunschwig’s distilling book of 1512. The twin “gemini” flasks of the pseudo-Lullian texts are its relatives and may never have been built.

The fault. A glass arm is a thin tube under repeated heating and cooling, and it cracks where it joins the body.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Returner is the pelican, two side-arms curving back into the body. The Alftian Codex’s Returner has the same two glass arms.

The aludel

The practice. The aludel was a stack of bottomless earthen pots fitted one over another, narrowing upward, over a pot of the charge, with a head at the top. The sublimate settled on the walls (Lemery 1686; Wikipedia, “Aludel”). The word comes from Arabic al-uthāl.

The fault. Each joint in the stack leaks, and the earthen walls hide the work until the stack is broken down.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Riser is the aludel: tall conical earthenware, stacked in series, the sublimate scraped out cold.

The philosophical egg

The practice. An ovoid flask with a long neck, sealed shut for long digestions (Newman 1999; Princeton University Library). The Treatise Newton copied filled it a quarter full (Newman 2019). Newton’s own notes record breaking his egg-glass when volatile salts rose in it (Spargo 2005). Its wall thickness goes unrecorded.

The fault. The egg’s only signal is its color and its only failure is breaking. Sealed glass heated for weeks holds whatever pressure the charge makes.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Standing Egg is the philosophical egg, with no failure signal short of the color going wrong or the glass going. The historical egg gave the same two signals. Eggs under heat for years or centuries are WOTR’s own; the real planned regimens ran seven to seventeen months. The trade reads the egg before it turns as Somnalis, every color it might go held at once and unread, and the long wait as Hypnather.

Crucibles

The practice. At Oberstockstall, a sixteenth-century laboratory dug in 1980 and again in 1993 and 1994, all the triangular crucibles were one refractory clay tempered with sand, grog or graphite. The graphite-tempered ones conducted heat better and resisted attack (Martinón-Torres, Rehren and von Osten). Hessian crucibles, made in Hesse from the late Middle Ages and traded across Europe, were kaolinitic clay with little flux and added quartz sand, fired above 1,300 °C. That firing formed mullite, which gave strength at high heat and resistance to sudden change of temperature, the earliest industrial use of mullite in Europe (Martinón-Torres, Rehren and Freestone 2006). Newton’s records show metals melted in crucibles and breakages noted (Spargo 2005).

The fault. A crucible is opaque. The melt inside is judged by the color of the fire, the sound, and what comes out.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Crucible is refractory ceramic for fusion and calcination at the top of the heat range, opaque, a fusion judged by ear and by flame color. It calls refractory ceramic porous. Real good crucibles and stoneware were dense; the cupel was the porous vessel, and porous by design.

Glass

The practice. Soda-lime glass softens at about 730 °C and survives a sudden change of only about 40 °C, against about 170 °C for borosilicate (Wikipedia, “Soda-lime glass”; “Borosilicate glass”). Glass was chosen wherever the reaction had to be watched (Veronesi 2021). It broke. Boerhaave’s five hundred mercury distillations cost him retorts along the way (Boerhaave 1730s), and Lemery coated his glass retorts in lute because a reverberatory could melt them (Lemery 1686). Otto Schott developed borosilicate glass at Jena in the late nineteenth century. It softens near 820 °C.

The fault. Glass cracks under thermal shock and cannot take the top of the range.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s account of glass, which cracks under thermal shock and will not take the top of the range, is the period account of soda and potash glass, and no era of WOTR makes borosilicate to argue with it. Onceglass has no real counterpart.

Luting and Sealing

What lute was for

The practice. Lute sealed the joints of a distilling train, coated vessels against flame and cracking, especially the bottom and sides of glass, and patched broken vessels (Veronesi and Hanß 2023; Lemery 1686). Gessner in 1583 sorted three: a common lute for furnace interiors, the lutum sapientiae for the outside of alembics, and a lute for mending (Veronesi and Hanß 2023).

The fault. Lute is the cheapest thing on the bench and holds the lives in the room. It is also made by eye and dried by guess.

What an excavated lute contains

The practice. A thick clay coat from the outside of a distilling glass at Oberstockstall was examined under the microscope and by electron-beam analysis (Veronesi and Hanß 2023; Veronesi 2021). Oxides of silicon, antimony and calcium made up over eighty per cent of it. The antimony was powdered stibnite. No known recipe calls for it. There was abundant angular sand and a calcium-and-sulfur binder, probably gypsum. Long voids showed where hair, cloth or straw had burned out, and round voids may have been seeds or grain. Two long fissures parallel to the glass show it was put on in two coats. Egg white leaves nothing that can be analyzed. Courts paid for the materials: the Württemberg laboratory spent 4 gulden 8 kreuzer on hair in 1618 and 1619.

The fault. What a workshop wrote down and what it put on the glass were two different things.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Marrowkin fourth lute, which they do not sell and will not discuss, has its nearest real case in the Oberstockstall stibnite lute that departs from every printed recipe.

How lutes failed

The practice. The record names five failures:

  • Hard-setting. Lemery’s vessel lute dried so hard the joints had to be softened with wet cloths before the retort would part from the receiver (Lemery 1686).
  • Vapor through the lute. In the 1690s William Yworth wrapped his joints in bladder under the lute. The fume still came through, and on opening the receiver he nearly fainted. He took that stinking breakthrough, after three to five days of distilling, as the sign to stop (Newman 2019).
  • Bladder eaten through. Corrosive spirits ate bladder, which is why Lemery changed to egg white and lime for acids (Lemery 1686).
  • Breakage under repeated fire. A modern reconstructor’s luted cucurbit survived three or four hot distillations and broke while solid acid residue was being cleared out of it (Stewart 2013) (single source).
  • Shrinkage and cracking on drying (Veronesi and Hanß 2023; Wikipedia, “Lute (material)”). The fault. A failed lute announces itself by smell, after the fume is already in the room.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s failed lute vents Strongwater fume into a closed workshop. Yworth’s receiver is the period case. The order of death in that room is treated under acid fume, below.

The hermetic seal

The practice. Lemery heated the neck of the glass little by little with burning coals until it was ready to melt, then closed it with pincers heated red-hot. The seal was used when the matter had to circulate without escaping (Lemery 1686). It was a skilled glass-worker’s job (Princeton University Library).

The fault. A seal made hot leaves strain in the glass, and the strained neck is where a sealed vessel gives.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Standing Egg is shut once and not opened until the working ends. Lemery’s closing was this seal.

Wax, cement and stoppers

The practice. Boerhaave closed a mercury bottle with a cork dipped in a melted cement of pitch, rosin, tallow and brimstone, then bound it with linen and packthread (Boerhaave 1730s). Ground stoppers existed by the 1690s, in Yworth’s separatory glass (Newman 2019). Still joints were made with lute, bladder, cork, rubber bungs or custom-ground glass until makers standardized their own ranges in the 1920s, and interchangeable ground joints came about 1930 (Wikipedia, “Ground glass joint”).

The fault. Wax and resin soften with heat, so a waxed stopper suits a finished product in storage and fails on a working vessel.

In WOTR. The Farrant Papers’ vessel carries a lute of gray wax over the stopper, cut and pressed back, with the consignor’s mark in it. That matches period wax cements over finished stoppers. Alchemetrica’s statement that there are no ground joints holds for the still train before standardized, interchangeable ground joints of about 1930; custom-ground joints and stoppers were older.

Who luted

The practice. No source names who mixed the lute in a real workshop. Courts bought its hair by the account, and the recipes were meant to be followed by hands that did not write them.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Luteboy and its fourteen-year-old with a bowl of clay are WOTR’s own. The children raking white arsenic out of Cornish calciners, under the body section, are the nearest real case of children on a poison bench.

Spagyria

The word and the theory

The practice. Paracelsus coined the word, which appears in his Paragranum of about 1530. It is traditionally taken from Greek spao, to draw apart, and ageiro, to gather (Principe 2013, as cited in Wikipedia, “Paracelsianism”). The spagyrist broke a medicine into its three principles, purified each, and put them back together. Done right, the method kept the useful part and shed the harmful, and could even turn some poisons into medicines, or so its makers held. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries “spagyric” often meant simply chymistry.

The fault. The preparation, as well as the quantity, was supposed to decide whether a thing was a poison. For minerals that was dangerous ground. Calomel, the mildest mercurial, turns partly into corrosive sublimate in the wrong company (see Calomel, below).

In WOTR. The Alftian Codex gives Furveus’s method as Spagyria, parting a plant into three principles, purifying each and putting them back. That is Paracelsus’s definition as the historians give it.

The tria prima

The practice. Sulfur, Mercury and Salt appear in full form in Paracelsus’s Opus Paramirum of about 1530 and 1531 (Wikipedia, “Paracelsus”). They are principles, not the common substances. Sulfur is what makes a body burn. Mercury is what is fluid and volatile and rises as vapor or smoke. Salt stays fixed in the fire. Earlier alchemy had only Sulfur and Mercury; Paracelsus added Salt (Read 1957). He is said to have shown the three with a burning log: flame from the Sulfur, smoke from the Mercury, ash from the Salt (single source for the attribution to Paracelsus himself). Paracelsian physicians sorted diseases the same way: sulfurous for fevers and inflammations, mercurial for excess moisture, saline for eruptions of the skin (Reading Early Medicine). The mapping onto body, soul and spirit was never fixed. One Paracelsian passage makes Mercury the spirit, Sulfur the soul and Salt the body (Read 1957), and later tables reverse the first two (Brown 1913). Boyle complained that chymists used the three words loosely and shifted their senses within a single book (Boyle 1675).

The fault. Boyle’s objection in The Sceptical Chymist (1661): burning does not reveal what a body is made of. It makes new compounds. The ash, oil and spirit are products of the fire, and gold cannot be resolved into the three at all (Boyle 1661, as summarized in Thorpe 1909). Van Helmont added that the three cannot be drawn from a living body without destroying what made it alive.

In WOTR. The Tria Prima of Furveus reads Sulfur onto the Core, Salt onto the Shell and Mercury onto the Attraction Layer (Alftian Codex, Volumes the First and Second). Because every real author mapped the triad his own way, the real record leaves room for it. Salt as the fixed body fits the Shell on real doctrine. Sulfur to the Core and Mercury to the Attraction Layer are WOTR’s own choices. The trade adds its own reading on top, with three Wellsprings standing for the three principles at the bench: Pyreveil for Sulfur at full heat, Cymorath for volatile Mercury, and Petralon for Salt in its fixed office.

Five principles

The practice. By Lemery’s Cours de chymie (1675; English 1677 and 1686) the chymists counted five principles. Three were active: Spirit, the old Mercury; Oil, the old Sulfur; and Salt, while two were passive: Phlegm, which is water, and Earth, the caput mortuum. Lemery gave the recovery for each. Spirit rises first in the still. Oil rises after it and floats on the water, as rosemary and lavender do, or sinks, as box, guaiacum and cloves do. Salt stays in the residue and is drawn out with water, filtered and evaporated (Lemery 1686).

The fault. The five are still products of the fire. Lemery himself took a mechanical view of them and kept them only as a working analysis.

In WOTR. No counterpart. WOTR’s spagyria runs on the three.

The procedure at the bench

The practice. Lemery’s balm is a full real spagyric preparation (Lemery 1686):

  1. The water and the oil. Beat fresh balm in a mortar, wet it with a strong decoction of more balm, and leave it covered two days in digestion. Distil in a copper still with a tinned head, the joint luted with wet bladder, at a fire of the second degree, until about half the added water has come over. Stand the water open in the sun five or six days before stopping it.
  2. The extract. Press the residue through linen, let it settle, filter, and evaporate gently to the thickness of honey.
  3. The spirit. A fermentable herb, fruit or flower is set to ferment with a little yeast and distilled, and the burning spirit rises first.
  4. The salt. Dry the pressed residue, burn it, and calcine the ash in a crucible. Steep it in hot water ten or twelve hours, boil an hour, filter the lye, and evaporate to dryness in an earthen or glass pan set in sand. For guaiacum, calcine the ash some hours in a potter’s furnace, lixiviate and evaporate, then calcine the salt again in a strong fire to whiten it. What is left, the caput mortuum, is good for nothing.
  5. Digestion. Seal a double vessel, lute the joint with wet bladder, and set it in horse dung four or five days, or a fortnight for a stronger charge. The fault. Every step runs a separate fire, and the salt step runs the fiercest one, which destroys what the others kept.

In WOTR. The Alftian Codex’s Salt, the ash of what is left burned white and crystallized, is Lemery’s calcine, leach, evaporate and whiten, step for step. Its oil and its spirit follow Lemery’s routes. The Codex’s Whitening read as pale and pearly in a spirit has no counterpart in these sources; the whitened thing at Lemery’s bench was the salt.

The fixed salt

The practice. The fixed salt of a burned plant is mostly potassium carbonate. Lemery himself called it an alkali, like all the fixed salts drawn from plants (Lemery 1686).

The fault. The plant’s particular virtue is lost in the fire. What the salt puts back into the recombined medicine is alkali, much the same from one plant as from another.

In WOTR. No counterpart states the salt’s chemistry. The Tria Prima of Furveus leaves the Salt’s virtue to the Shell.

Doses of the spagyric products

The practice. Lemery dosed balm water at two to six ounces, the extract at a scruple to a drachm (1.3 to 3.9 g), and the fixed salt at ten grains to a scruple (0.65 to 1.3 g) (Lemery 1686).

The fault. Plant doses were broad because plant products were mild. The narrow doses belong to the minerals.

In WOTR. No counterpart. The Codex doses no plant medicine by weight.

Iatrochemistry and the Dose

The dose makes the poison

The practice. The line comes from the Third Defense of Paracelsus’s Septem Defensiones. He wrote it in Carinthia in 1538, and it was first printed in 1564. Its targets were physicians who accused him of prescribing poisons, meaning his mineral remedies (Deichmann et al. 1986; Grandjean 2016). The German says, in this page’s plain rendering, that all things are poison and none is without it, and that the dose alone makes a thing no poison. The standard English opens with his question, “What is there that is not poison?” (Deichmann et al. 1986). The Latin slogan sola dosis facit venenum is later. It compresses his sentence and turns it about, since Paracelsus said the dose makes a thing not a poison (Bödeker and Moebus 2023).

The fault. The maxim assumes a threshold below which there is no harm. That holds for a single poisoning and fails for the cumulative metals. Mercury and arsenic taken at a “safe” daily dose build up in the body, and the chronic diseases they cause are what ended the remedies (Grandjean 2016). Age, timing and the patient’s own make-up also decide the poison.

In WOTR. The Alftian Codex states it in its own words: each heals at a measure and poisons past it, and the man who measures pays in his hands. The toxicology adds that the man who measures pays cumulatively, which the Codex’s “every day he measures” already carries. The trade reads the survived measure as Contrition: the body keeps the receipt.

Paracelsus and mercury

The practice. In the syphilis quarrel of 1529 and 1530 Paracelsus sided with mercury against guaiac wood. The Fugger house, which imported guaiac, helped get his syphilis work blocked at Nuremberg (The Renaissance Mathematicus 2021) (single source). He also attacked physicians who poisoned their patients with mercury (Grandjean 2016). His quarrel was between measured mercury and heroic mercury. His Von der Bergsucht, written about 1533 and printed in 1567, was the first book on the lung diseases of miners and smelters (Garrison-Morton 2630).

The fault. The measured course was his argument. The heroic course was the practice that won, and ran for three centuries.

In WOTR. No counterpart for the quarrel. The Codex’s Furveus is a measurer.

The pharmacopoeias

The practice. Jean Beguin’s Tyrocinium Chymicum (Paris, 1610), often called the first chemistry textbook, came from public lectures on preparing spagyric medicines (Wikipedia, “Jean Beguin”). It already describes calomel (Davidson) (single source). The Pharmacopoeia Londinensis of 1618, the first English official formulary, compiled by the College of Physicians, carried chemical medicines in two short sections at its end, among them mercurius dulcis, which is calomel (Wood 2018). It is said to list eighty-five chemical preparations (single source). Glauber’s Pharmacopoea spagyrica (1654 to 1668) gathered his mineral remedies, and he gave out free medicines daily, among them an antimony panacea (Ahonen, DSB). Paracelsian theory declined in the later seventeenth century. Its mineral remedies stayed in the pharmacopoeias for two hundred years more.

The fault. A heretic’s recipes became a college’s official list within eighty years, and the list outlived the theory that justified it.

In WOTR. No pharmacopoeia by name. The nearest counterpart is the Standing Index, the Bench’s register of entered formulas, physic among them, entered in plain language against a reference lot.

Calomel

The practice. Calomel is mercurous chloride, mercurius dulcis. Beguin made it by grinding corrosive sublimate with as much quicksilver as it would “kill” and subliming. The 1870 method sublimed quicksilver sulfate with common salt and more quicksilver, powdered the sublimate, and washed it with boiling water until the washings showed no corrosive sublimate (Wood and Bache 1870). The fault. Calomel is nearly insoluble, so little is absorbed, and enormous quantities were given apparently without harm. The danger is conversion. With common salt, sal ammoniac, bitter almonds or strong acids it turns partly into corrosive sublimate. The 1870 authors report, at second hand, a death after calomel given with nitromuriatic acid, and relay Bauwens’s observation that physicians near brackish water seldom gave calomel and that naval surgeons stopped giving it to sailors living on salt meat (Wood and Bache 1870). The same grains are mild or corrosive by what they meet. No reliable human lethal dose is recorded; the rat oral LD50 is 210 mg per kg (Wikipedia, “Mercury(I) chloride”).

In WOTR. The Alftian Codex’s calomel for a gut that would not move is the period purgative use; the 1870 adult purgative dose ran five to fifteen grains.

Corrosive sublimate

The practice. Mercuric chloride was given at one twelfth to one quarter of a grain (5.4 to 16 mg) three or four times a day, for secondary syphilis and diseases of the skin (Wood and Bache 1870).

The fault. It is soluble, so the gap between dose and death is narrow. The human lethal dose is about one to four grams. One gram corrodes the gut and two raise mortality steeply (Forensic Toxicology 2022; Wikipedia, “Mercury(II) chloride”). The top single dose was a quarter grain. Death lay sixty to two hundred and fifty times above it. Thénard swallowed some by mistake during a lecture in 1825 and was saved with egg white (single source).

In WOTR. No counterpart by name.

Quicksilver itself

The practice. Liquid mercury was swallowed a pound or two at a time for obstruction of the bowel, to act by its weight (Wood and Bache 1870) (single source). The 1870 authors doubted it. Blue mass, the mercury pill, was about one third metallic mercury. That is some 65 mg to a pill, taken two or three times a day (Wikipedia, “Blue mass”) (single source). For syphilis, mercury went in three ways: ointment rubbed in before a fire, fumigation over heated crucibles, or red or white precipitate by mouth. The course ran until the patient salivated. One eighteenth-century course used five or six ounces of ointment a rubbing, with precipitate at twelve to fifteen grains, for six to twelve days (Edward Worth Library). The 1870 dispensatory still gave doses “with a view to salivation.”

The fault. Swallowed liquid mercury is barely absorbed. Inhaled mercury vapor is absorbed at about seventy to eighty-five per cent (Bernhoft 2012; ATSDR 2024). The metal in the gut is nearly inert. The metal hot at the bench is the poison. The popular figure of three pints of saliva a day as the sign of cure, and its credit to Paracelsus, rest on popular sources and are disputed.

In WOTR. The Alftian Codex’s quicksilver among Furveus’s remedies is attested practice. The body section below carries what it did to him.

White arsenic and Fowler’s solution

The practice. White arsenic is arsenious acid, arsenic trioxide. In 1870 the average dose was one tenth of a grain (6.5 mg) three times a day in pill, usually with opium. Henry Hunt gave one twentieth of a grain (3.2 mg) three times a day for ulcerated cancer of the womb and for menorrhagia. It was given for cancer of the lip, skin disease, chorea, periodic headache and agues. Asthmatics smoked it in cigarettes at a quarter grain (Wood and Bache 1870). Thomas Fowler of Stafford published his solution in 1786, and it entered the London Pharmacopoeia in 1809 (Wood and Bache 1870; Wikipedia, “Fowler’s solution”). The US formula boiled 64 grains of arsenious acid with 64 of potassium bicarbonate in twelve fluid ounces of water, added half an ounce of compound spirit of lavender “to give it taste, and prevent its being mistaken for water,” and made it up to a wine pint. That gives half a grain per fluidrachm, about 8.8 mg per ml. The usual adult dose was ten drops two or three times a day, about 5.4 mg a dose if a drop is a minim. Mitchell pushed it to fifteen or twenty drops a dose for agues (Wood and Bache 1870). By the 1880s it was given for ague, sleeping sickness, psoriasis, chorea, asthma, anemia and rheumatism, and from about 1865 for leukemia.

The fault. The margin is thin. A fatal dose of arsenic trioxide is given as 70 to 180 mg (ATSDR), 70 to 200 mg (Wikipedia, “Arsenic poisoning”), and 50 to 300 mg of inorganic arsenic (IPCS). The 1858 Bradford analyst put a fatal dose at 4½ grains, about 290 mg. That is about ten to forty-five of the tenth-grain pills, or roughly 8 to 34 ml of Fowler’s, no more than about an ounce of the bottle. Fowler’s also decays: in flint glass it slowly decomposes, smells of garlic, and silvers the inside of the bottle with lead drawn from the glass.

In WOTR. The Alftian Codex’s white arsenic in small measure for a girl with no color in her is the period anemia indication at a period small dose.

What the Bench Does to the Body

Mercury vapor

The practice. Mercury enters the body through the lungs. The vapor has no smell (Emsley 2005). At 20 °C, air over open mercury saturates at about 14 mg per cubic meter, a hundred and forty times the 1964 permissible level (Earles 1964). Heat multiplies it. Computed from the vapor-pressure table, the saturated air holds about 77 mg per cubic meter at 42 °C, about 690 at 77 °C and about 6,100 at 120 °C (Wikipedia, “Mercury (element)”) (single source for the table; the concentrations are arithmetic). Mercury’s half-life in the blood is three to five days and in the brain longer than several years (Bernhoft 2012) (single source).

The fault. A warm sand bath, a gilder’s pan or a mercury “fixed” over a fire puts grams of vapor into each cubic meter of a closed room, and nobody smells it.

In WOTR. The Alftian Codex holds that the man who measures takes his own share every day he measures. The main route is the vapor at the heated bench more than the doses handed out.

The mercurial triad

The practice. Classical mercurialism has three signs (Bernhoft 2012; Wikipedia, “Erethism”):

  • Tremor. A fine tremor starts in the hands, spreads to the eyelids, lips and tongue, and is broken every few minutes by coarse jerks. At its worst a man can hardly cross the floor. It shows first in the handwriting. The trade called it hatter’s shakes and gilder’s palsy. Ramazzini in 1700 wrote of gilders with vertigo, asthma, paralysis and a palsy of the neck (single source, via a summary of the translation).
  • Erethism. Irritability, timidity and pathological shyness, low confidence, depression, memory loss and insomnia. The shyness came late to the literature: New Jersey hatters’ tremor was described in 1860 and their shyness only in 1912 (Wedeen 1989).
  • The mouth. Swollen gums and copious saliva first, then loose teeth and ulcers, and at high dose a swollen tongue and face and a rotting jaw (Bernhoft 2012; Earles 1964). The fault. The tremor is not specific to mercury and has to be read against a man’s exposure. Newton’s case shows how hard that is.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s crucible palsy is mercurialism in its recorded order: a tremor in the writing hand, climbing to the head and jaw, with irritability, then shyness, then a reluctance to be looked at, which the family sees first. Alchemetrica’s master whose later formulas are dictated, and Furveus dictating in the Alftian Codex, carry the handwriting sign. The recorded tremor climbs through the eyelids, lips and tongue; Ramazzini’s palsy of the neck supports the head.

Onset and recovery

The practice. Onset tracks the concentration in the air; years of service matter little. The US Public Health Service felt-hat study of 1941 found no case of mercurialism below 0.1 mg per cubic meter. Below 0.08, three of sixty-six men were borderline. Between 0.08 and 0.15, fifteen of a hundred and twenty-seven were borderline or first-stage (Neal et al. 1941). At low levels symptoms come over months to years. At high levels they come in weeks. On HMS Triumph in 1810, after a spill of cargo mercury below decks, cases appeared within three weeks and about two hundred of 587 men fell ill. Poisoning followed where a man slept. The men under the forecastle got off with sore gums, and among those who berthed below, the midshipmen, whose duties kept them on deck, came off lightest. The animals aboard died, and coins and watches came up silvered (Earles 1964). Most of the salivated men were back aboard after sixteen to twenty-seven days ashore. Recovery is partial. Former chloralkali workers examined an average of 12.3 years after exposure ended had more postural tremor than unexposed men, 18.2 against 7.5 per cent (Andersen et al. 1993). Men exposed more than thirty years before still showed nerve damage in proportion to their dose (Letz et al. 2000).

The fault. The disease depends on ventilation and distance from the heat, which no career count captures. The mouth heals in weeks off the bench; the tremor may fade over years or stay.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s onset at roughly nine years of bench service is WOTR’s own figure; real onset ran from weeks at saturation to years below the threshold. Its “permanent, progressive” is harsher than the real disease, which partly fades once exposure stops and leaves lasting signs.

Named cases

The practice. Four figures of the trade are argued over:

  • Isaac Newton. Furnaces from 1669 and heavy work with mercury and antimony. In 1693 he broke down with insomnia, poor digestion, memory loss and paranoid letters. Hair said to be his, analyzed in 1979, showed raised mercury: about fifteen times normal is one reported figure (Lesney 2003), and other reports run to about forty times. Spargo and Pounds and Johnson and Wolbarsht argued for poisoning in 1979. The case against reads the breakdown as a depressive illness with no mouth signs and no tremor in the handwriting (Ditchburn 1980; Keynes 1981); the hair’s provenance is also open, and some put the collapse down to his break with Fatio de Duillier (Wikipedia, “Later life of Isaac Newton”).
  • Johann Rudolf Glauber. His health failed from about 1660, an illness put down, as a modern inference, to the mercury, arsenic, antimony and lead of his trade. A fall from a wagon in 1666 crippled him and left him bedridden. He sold his books and apparatus to keep his family and died in Amsterdam in 1670 (Wikipedia, “Johann Rudolf Glauber”).
  • Carl Wilhelm Scheele. He smelled and tasted his new substances. He had kidney disease from 1785 and died at forty-three. His doctors called it mercury poisoning; modern accounts add arsenic and lead (Wikipedia, “Carl Wilhelm Scheele”).
  • Charles II. He kept a laboratory and worked at fixing mercury. His death in 1685 in convulsions has been put down to mercury vapor, and also to stroke and kidney failure (Emsley 2005). The fault. No case in the old record carries a measured dose. Every attribution is argued from signs and occupation after the fact.

In WOTR. The Alftian Codex’s Furveus, dictating when his hand will no longer write a formula small enough to read, follows the recorded pattern of a working chymist’s decline.

Arsenic, acute

The practice. Within one to two hours: a burning mouth and throat, drooling, trouble swallowing, vomiting, pain in the belly, diarrhea (UKPID). Garlic breath is among the early signs (ATSDR). The 1870 dispensatory, after Christison, gave three courses: inflammation of the gut killing in one to three days; collapse with little inflammation, killing in five or six hours; and gut symptoms followed by palsy, fits and coma. Some cases resembled cholera. In the 1858 Bradford sweets poisoning, a lozenge maker’s mistake put about nine grains of arsenic into each sweet. About twenty people died and more than two hundred fell seriously ill (Wikipedia, “1858 Bradford sweets poisoning”).

The fault. Huge doses are often vomited, and so men survived a drachm while others died of a few grains.

In WOTR. No counterpart for acute arsenic poisoning.

Arsenic, delayed and chronic

The practice. A symmetrical numbness and burning of the hands and feet begins weeks after the first signs ease; the agency’s own pages give two to four weeks and seven to fourteen days (ATSDR). Pale bands across the nails, Mees’ lines, appear weeks to months after the dose. They were described by Reynolds in 1901, Aldrich in 1904 and Mees in 1919, and they mark a pause in the nail’s growth (Wikipedia, “Mees’ lines”; LITFL). Fingernails grow about 3.5 mm a month and replace themselves in five to six months (Yaemsiri et al. 2010). A band’s distance from the cuticle dates the dose. Chronic intake darkens the skin with scattered pale spots, “raindrops on a dusty road,” and thickens the palms and soles (ATSDR). Hutchinson in 1887 and 1888 tied skin cancers to long courses of arsenic, five of his six cases being psoriasis patients (National Research Council 1977) (single source). Prolonged medicinal use at three to six milligrams a day often gave mild poisoning (single source). Styrian peasants were reported in 1851 to eat arsenic as a tonic, starting on a piece the size of a lentil. Alfred Swain Taylor called the reports exaggerated and Roscoe defended them (Hektoen International 2017).

The fault. The signs come late and are not unique to arsenic. Mees’ lines follow other poisons and illnesses too.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Draft-mark sits in the sclera and under the nailbeds, keyed to what a man handled most. The Alftian Codex puts it on Furveus as gray nail-beds and pale bands across the nails that no washing lifted, keyed to quicksilver, calomel and white arsenic. Those bands are Mees’ lines. A Mees’ line grows out in five or six months, so a band still visible dates a dose within the last half-year. The discoloration of the sclera has no real counterpart for mercury or arsenic; silver’s slate-gray argyria of skin, nails and eye, and lead’s blue gum line, are the nearest real marks (DermNet). A renderer reading a career off the hands has real ground. Yellow nitric-acid stains on skin and nails, Mees’ lines, the lead line and argyria each belong to one substance.

Lead

The practice. Lead reached the chymist through cupellation and litharge, lead-chamber vitriol and lead vessels. In 1656 Samuel Stockhausen of Goslar blamed the smelters’ disease on litharge fume (Wikipedia, “Samuel Stockhausen”), and in 1767 George Baker traced the Devonshire colic to lead in cider presses. The signs are colic with obstinate constipation, wrist-drop from weakness of the forearm muscles, anemia, and a narrow blue-black line along the gum, which Henry Burton described in 1840 (Wikipedia, “Burton’s line”; ATSDR).

The fault. Lead gives colic long before it gives the gum line, and the colic was blamed on diet for a century.

In WOTR. No counterpart. No WOTR condition is keyed to lead.

Antimony and calcination fume

The practice. Workers in antimony dust and fume develop “antimony spots,” itchy pimples around the sweat glands, commonest in summer, and a dusting of the lungs with chronic cough (UKPID; ATSDR). Smelter workers breathing sulfur dioxide and dust develop chronic bronchitis, now a recognized compensable disease (WSIB). Paracelsus’s Von der Bergsucht was the first account of it.

The fault. The lung disease builds with dose and never reverses.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s vein cough, from calcination fume and especially sulfide roast, is smelter’s bronchitis. Its twenty-year onset is WOTR’s own figure.

Acid fume

The practice. Nitrous fume, the red-brown gas off aqua fortis, dissolves poorly in water. It spares the throat and reaches deep into the lung. After a free interval of three to thirty hours the lungs fill with fluid, and a scarring of the small airways can follow weeks later (ATSDR, “Nitrogen Oxides”; Case Reports in Emergency Medicine 2019). In 1863 an Edinburgh chemist named Stewart broke a vessel of fuming nitric acid. Within hours he and his assistant could hardly breathe, and both died (Babcock 1882) (single source). Oil of vitriol meets water with violent heat: a hundred millilitres of each at 19 °C pass 131 °C in under a minute, and water poured onto the acid boils at the surface and throws it (Wikipedia, “Sulfuric acid”).

The fault. Nitrous fume kills late. Men walk out of the room and drown in their beds hours later, sometimes the next day.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Strongwater fume is the commonest cause of death in the trade, which fits nitrous fume. In the closed workshop of a failed lute, nearest-first holds for how large a dose each man took, and the deaths themselves come hours later. Blackstone Oil meeting water violently enough to break its vessel is the heat of dilution, and a bench scar from learning it is an acid burn of the ordinary kind.

Tests and antidotes

The practice. Scheele turned arsenic trioxide into garlic-smelling arsine with zinc and acid in 1775. Early tests were too weak for a court: in the Bodle trial of 1832 the yellow precipitate spoiled before it reached the jury. James Marsh’s test of 1836 digested the sample in acid with zinc and led the arsine through a heated tube or onto cold porcelain, where it left a silvery-black mirror, sensitive to about 0.02 mg. Antimony gives a like mirror, told apart by solubility. Orfila used the test to convict Marie Lafarge in 1840 (National Library of Medicine; Wikipedia, “Marsh test”). Hugo Reinsch’s test of 1841 heated bright copper foil in the acidified sample and caught arsenic, antimony, bismuth and mercury as a gray film (Wikipedia, “Reinsch test”). Bunsen and Berthold found hydrated ferric oxide as an antidote to arsenic in 1834 (DSB, “Bunsen”; Wikipedia, “Robert Bunsen”).

The fault. The Marsh test makes arsine, the gas that killed Gehlen. The test itself was a hazard to the analyst.

In WOTR. No counterpart. No WOTR canon names a test for a poison, and the Marsh and Reinsch tests are nineteenth-century glassware that no era of WOTR will build. The nearest thing a WOTR court can call is a Judicium reader, whose art is to identify composition from signature alone, at a reader’s fee.

The whole career

The practice. Scheele died at forty-three after about twenty-five working years, and Glauber fell ill at about fifty-six, was crippled by a fall at sixty-two and was dead at sixty-six. Mercury miners, gilders and hatters were typically disabled rather than killed. Reform came by ventilation and removal. In 1818 d’Arcet won the Ravrio prize of three thousand francs with a gilders’ forge whose draft carried the fume away (d’Arcet 1818). Electroplating after 1840 began to replace fire-gilding (Schwarcz 2023) (single source). Children and bal maidens raked white arsenic out of the Cornish calciner flues with a cloth over the face (National Academies; Cornish Mining World Heritage Site) (single source).

The fault. No career-length statistic for alchemists exists. Nobody gathered one.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s twenty good years for a pure alchemist, an estimate the Archives never gathered the figures for, is consistent with the historical record, which has no figure either. The quiet and law-fragment burn have no real counterpart; the quiet resembles erethism’s apathy only loosely.

Cover-Names and Colors

Decknamen

The practice. A Deckname is a cover-name: a word that hides a substance, an operation or a vessel (Newman 1996; Newton Project). The German historians Ruska and Wiedemann published on alchemical cover-names in 1924. Newman’s paper of 1996 decoded Starkey’s most obscure allegory using only texts inside the Philalethes corpus and showed that the imagery hid laboratory practice on purpose. That is the standard answer to the Jungian reading of alchemy as projection. Principe and Newman hold the same position: the images are chemistry in code (Principe 2013; Conniff 2014).

The fault. The cover protected the secret from the thief and from the heir alike. Whole recipes were lost when the key was.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Cover-Names substitutes a figure for a substance, decipherable inside the house that wrote it. That is the practice Newman decoded, and the trade reads it as Tenebra.

The fault. A dispersed or syncopated recipe fails its own house when a book is lost or a master dies before he explains.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s syncope and dispersion are Newman’s and Kraus’s devices. Its parathesis, a false step a knowledgeable reader recognizes as absurd and skips, is WOTR’s own sense of Newman’s word.

Why they concealed

The practice. Secrets were lucrative (Newman 1996). Craft secrecy passed recipes only to those judged worthy (Meyer 2014). Gold-making could debase coin, and rulers are thought to have used alchemists to debase their own coinage while forbidding it to anyone else (Newman 2005). Commentators decoded older authors to claim their authority. Starkey, as Eirenaeus Philalethes, published his process in allegory, and in a letter to Boyle of 1651 gave the same process in plain English (Newman 1987).

The fault. One man wrote both versions. The plain one survived by the accident of a letter.

In WOTR. The Standing Index, as Alchemetrica describes it, is a record of what the trade was willing to surrender, and a petition for Entry demands the plain version. Starkey’s letter to Boyle is the period case of a plain version given to a single reader.

Meanings that drift

The practice. A cover-name meant different things to different authors. Newton’s Index chemicus lists several meanings for the Green Lion, each tied to a named author (Newman 2018). Starkey used Saturn for stibnite, not lead. It took Newton years to see it (Newman 2005; Newton Project). “Stinking spirit” is usually ammonium carbonate in Starkey’s notebooks and sulfur in another author (Chemistry World).

The fault. A reader decoding one house’s book with another house’s key gets a confident wrong recipe.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Green Lion for the vitriol or something else, depending on which house’s book, is the drift Newton’s index records. Our Lead for the antimony is exact: Philalethes’ Saturn is stibnite.

Cover-names decoded

The practice. Historians have read these back into chemistry:

  • The gray wolf. Basil Valentine’s First Key (German 1599, woodcuts 1602) feeds the king to a ravenous gray wolf and burns the wolf, and the king returns. The wolf is stibnite; the king, gold: the assayer’s purification of gold with antimony sulfide (Principe 2013, via Wikipedia, “The Twelve Keys of Basil Valentine”). Principe tested readings of several Keys in the laboratory (single source).
  • The green lion of Ripley’s Bosom Book (printed 1683). Thirty pounds of “sericon,” dissolved a pound to a gallon of twice-distilled vinegar, set in a water bath to a green gum called our Green Lion. Distilled from a luted retort, it gave a faint water, a white fume, a reddish fume, then drops like blood, and left a black residue called our Dragon (Ripley 1683). Sericon was an impure red lead, and the green gum was crude lead acetate (Rampling 2014). Later readers took sericon for antimony. In Philalethes the green lion is crude antimony (Newman 1996).
  • Saturnia, the daughter of Saturn, is crude antimony. Mars is iron. Cadmus and his blood are iron and its sulfur. The Net that caught Mars and Venus is an alloy of copper with the star regulus (Newman 1996; Newman 2005).
  • The eagle. In Philalethes each preparation of the mercury with its arsenic is one eagle, taken to the seventh flight and sometimes the tenth. One eagle took about half a day by Starkey’s improved method (Philalethes, “Preparations of the Sophic Mercury”).
  • The king and the queen. In Philalethes the King is gold and weds the philosophical mercury. Both whiten, die, putrefy through livid, black, bluish and yellowish, and lie in a black tomb that promises resurrection if the fire is kept constant (Newman 1996).
  • The philosophers’ mercury. Reduce stibnite with iron to the star regulus and fuse it with silver. Amalgamate with quicksilver, then purge and distil in eagles. De Maets’ decipherment gives one ounce of regulus to two of cupelled silver to three of the purest mercury (Newman 1996). The fault. The published recipes left the silver out, a clean syncope. Hertodt in 1678 was stuck until he found in another author that the star regulus will not take up mercury without silver (Newman 1996).

In WOTR. WOTR has no gray wolf, eagle or star regulus, and no philosophers’ tree as a cover-name; the metal tree in the still flask appears only as the trade’s reading of Terranova. The Gray Bride, the Winter Salt and the Third Cask are WOTR’s own names with no real referent. The Alchemical Index carries no cover-names.

The impurity lesson

The practice. Principe found that a Basil Valentine recipe for glass of antimony failed with pure laboratory reagents and worked with natural Hungarian stibnite, which carries silica, the ore Valentine specified (Principe 1987). Rampling ran Ripley’s lead process under a fume hood and saw its colors (Rampling 2020, reviewed by Stadolnik 2021).

The fault. A recipe that names a source names an impurity. Change the source and the recipe fails, and the failure reads as a lie in the book.

In WOTR. The Standing Index, as Alchemetrica describes it, requires a reference lot because the written formula will not reproduce the product. That is Principe’s impurity lesson in institutional form.

The colors of the Work

The practice. The four-color scheme is Greek: blackening, whitening, yellowing, and reddening or purpling, in Latin nigredo, albedo, citrinitas, rubedo (Wikipedia, “Magnum opus”) (single source for the Greek attribution to Maria and pseudo-Democritus). After the fifteenth century many writers folded the yellowing into the reddening and counted three (Rampling 2024). The peacock’s tail, a show of many colors, was sometimes added. Philalethes summed the Work as black, the peacock’s tail, white, orange and red. He called the black “an image of eternal death,” a sight that gladdens the sage (Philalethes, An Open Entrance).

The fault. The color told the worker where he stood only if his book and his matter agreed, and the schemes did not agree with each other.

In WOTR. Alchemetrica’s Reading the Color uses the Greek four: the Blacking, the Whitening, the Yellowing, the Reddening. The blacking expected and welcome, and colors read through glass in a sealed vessel, are Philalethes’ practice. The trade reads the Blacking as Mortalis, the point where a matter stops holding itself together. WOTR has no peacock’s tail and no green stage.

The regimen in the sealed egg

The practice. Philalethes’ Open Entrance (1667) times the colors for one part purified gold to three of the prepared mercury:

  • Regimen of Mercury, about forty to fifty days. Colors begin about the twentieth day. A green appears near the end of the fourth week, lasts about ten days, and gives way to black.
  • Regimen of Saturn, black as pitch, forty days by Newman’s reading.
  • Regimen of Jupiter, three weeks, all colors, ending in a snowy white deposit on the glass.
  • Regimen of the Moon, three weeks, white, with tiny silver trees.
  • Regimen of Venus, forty days, green. The philosophical tree grows in it.
  • Regimen of Mars, yellow or dirty brown, then the rainbow and the peacock’s tail.
  • Regimen of the Sun, red. The text warns to keep the heat gentle, never shake the vessel, and never burn the flowers. Too much heat turns the green black. Ripley’s Bosom Book gives the same warning at the gum stage (Ripley 1683).

The fault. These are Starkey’s claimed stages and times. The one modern replication reported grew its tree in a sealed heated flask, and the report gives no elapsed time (Conniff 2014).

In WOTR. Alchemetrica says colors arriving too fast mean the working should be abandoned, which matches Philalethes’ warning about heat. WOTR has no color-by-color calendar like Philalethes’ regimens. The nearest are the Rot’s six to nine weeks to black (Alchemetrica) and the Alftian Codex’s three turns to the Blacking and most of a month in the Returner to the Whitening.

The Emerald Tablet in English

The Arabic versions

The practice. The oldest surviving text is an appendix to the Kitāb Sirr al-Khalīqa, the Book of the Secret of Creation, attributed to Balīnās, the pseudo-Apollonius of Tyana. Ruska found and published this version in 1925 and 1926. Kraus dated it to about 813 to 833, Weisser to about 750 to 800, and Ruska earlier still (Wikipedia, “Emerald Tablet”; Marshall, Alchemy Web Site). A second Arabic version is in the Jabirian Kitāb Usṭuqus al-Uss al-Thānī, the Second Book of the Element of the Foundation, which Holmyard found and translated in 1923. A third sits in the tenth-century Sirr al-Asrār, the Secret of Secrets.

The fault. The dating rests on the surrounding works, and scholars have disagreed by a century.

In WOTR. The Alftian Codex’s Venur recites an Arabic-derived Tablet. See Holmyard, below.

The Latin versions

The practice. Hugo of Santalla translated the Book of the Secret of Creation about 1145 to 1151. The Secretum secretorum was partly translated by Johannes Hispalensis about 1140 and fully by Philip of Tripoli in the thirteenth century. The most widely copied Latin, the vulgate, comes from a twelfth-century compilation. Its translator has been tentatively identified as Plato of Tivoli, an identification later scholarship treats as a guess (Wikipedia, “Emerald Tablet,” after Mandosio 2004; Selwood 2023). Hortulanus’ fourteenth-century commentary read the text as a recipe for the stone. It was first printed at Nuremberg by Petreius in 1541. Newton made his own English translation, now King’s College Cambridge Keynes MS 28, about 1680 (Marshall, after Dobbs 1988).

The fault. The Latin translator did not understand the Arabic word for talisman and transliterated it as telesmus, which became one of alchemy’s most argued-over words (Wikipedia, “Emerald Tablet”) (single source).

In WOTR. The Alftian Codex’s Heralds teach the Latin line of descent.

From and like

The practice. The Arabic, in Holmyard’s English, says the above is from the below and the below from the above: derivation. The Latin vulgate, in Steele and Singer’s English, says the above is like the below: likeness. The Jabirian version lacks two things. It has no line on the three parts of the wisdom and no speaker named Trismegistus. It ends instead with the speaker’s claim to have explained the whole in two of his books. The Sirr al-Khalīqa version, in Ruska’s German, does name Hermes as threefold in wisdom, and the Latin gives Hermes Trismegistus with three parts of the wisdom (Marshall, Alchemy Web Site; Wikipedia, “Emerald Tablet”).

The fault. A reader who knows only one version takes its verb for the Tablet’s.

In WOTR. The Alftian Codex distributes its recitations on exactly this line. Venur’s Arabic-derived text uses “from” and names no Trismegistus. Mu-jin’s Heralds’ version uses “like” and ends in the line that earns him the name Thrice-Great, and he reads the principle as a mirror.
