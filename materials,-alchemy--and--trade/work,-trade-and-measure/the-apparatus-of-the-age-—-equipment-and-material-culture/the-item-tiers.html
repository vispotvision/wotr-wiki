Grade and Rarity

Grade and Rarity

Nov 28, 202624 min read

Ask what it is made of. Then ask who made it. Then believe the smaller answer.

Painted over the door of the assay room at Ambleford

Dame Ragnild Thurstan wanted her granddaughter married with the family sword in the room, and the family had no sword, so she had one made out of the family hearth. The hearthstone was Greystone, quarried from the hill the Thurstans had sat on for nine generations, and she paid a smith of Master standing forty gold to cut it into a blade and to set a working in it worthy of the name, in the courtyard, on the wedding day, in front of two hundred guests in every color the valley’s dyers could sell. Master Ordway Hesketh arrived in a scarlet apron with his guild mark embroidered across the chest as large as a platter. His own Journeyman told him, in front of the cook, that the stone would hold a D-Grade working and nothing above it. Hesketh told the courtyard that stone could be persuaded, named the sword Grandmother’s Patience in a voice they heard at the mill, set both hands on the flat, and drove in an A-Grade working with his aura standing off him like a sheet of hot brass. The blade held for about as long as it takes to say so. Then it went white along the edge and came apart in every direction, through the bridal canopy, through the goose, through the left ear of the bridegroom and the right eye of the steward, and one long splinter went forty feet up into the chapel door, where the Thurstans have left it. The wedding went on, because the bride insisted. Hesketh paid back the forty gold and the steward’s pension, and the shards still sell at Ambleford fair for a copper apiece as a charm against boasting. The hilt hangs in the Thurstan hall with a card under it, in the old lady’s hand, giving the sword’s item Tier as none.

Hesketh knew every word of the law below, and so does every apprentice who has been told that story, which is why they tell it. What keeps them awake is the other half of the trade’s folklore, that stone can be persuaded, once, by somebody at the end of a lifetime, and you will find that under Master Works.

Two Ratings

Every worked piece in a lawful shop is sold on two questions, and a buyer who confuses them is about to be robbed.

Grade says how much power it has, on the same letters the Commission posts beasts and missiles by, Hollow up through EX+. The Measurewrights keep the same scale hidden under every person’s figures and never say it of a person aloud. It is a measurement. A coil reads it, and two coils in two kingdoms give the same answer.

Rarity says how scarce it is: Common, Uncommon, Rare, Very Rare, Legendary, Mythic, Unique. It is an argument, conducted by people with money on it, and the assay house that writes it down is mostly recording who won.

The two run independently. A hard-working sect forge can turn out Grade S blades by the rack and call them Rare, and the last scarf ever cut from a burned loom can be Grade E and Mythic. A piece’s Grade stays where its making left it, unless it is bonded. Its Rarity moves whenever the world does, and when a kiln falls in or a war melts a family’s plate, every surviving piece goes up a step overnight and the clerks are very busy.

The Old Words Are the Stamps

Before anybody spoke of Rarity the Accord’s mark carried one word, and it still does. That word is the stamp, the older trade calls what it measures the item Tier, and every assayer alive will read you the old rule by heart:

An item carries an item Tier, Plain through Proscribed: the lower of what its material can hold and the title its maker held in the trade when it was made. A Journeyman’s blade is at most Tempered, holding a C-Grade working and no more. A master cannot put an A-Grade working into Greystone: the stone fails first.

The maker’s side of it is a guild title, read in the maker’s own trade (the old assay books call it the Tier of Standing), and the stamp is simply the Grade said in the Accord’s words. A composite item is graded by the material that carries the working, and the handle and the fittings do not enter into it. Beside its tier a material has its virtue, and the virtue decides which workings it holds well and how it fails: stone remembers, salt fixes, gold will not corrupt, quicksilver will not keep a shape, so a working that must remember sits well in stone and nothing that has to keep its shape will settle in quicksilver. A plain item with no working in it has no Tier at all; it performs as plain matter does, by the world’s old habits, multiplies nothing in the person holding it, and it can still be Rare, since there are only so many ways to come by a two-hundred-year-old yew stave.

Material tier n holds a working up to item Tier n, level with the nth title. Each Tier is the word stamped on the Accord’s mark, in Accord Latin beside the old Probatum est, and said in plain English at the table and the counter.

What a Title Lets You Make and Sell

The same nine titles run through every guild in the Accord, and they grade trust in that trade. A smith’s title caps the Grade she may make. A merchant’s title caps the Rarity he may sell. Neither title says a word about whether its holder can throw a punch, and a Grandmaster merchant may well go down to a thrown cabbage.

Three ceilings stand over any smith at once: what the material can hold, what her title lets her stamp, and the strength of the Essence she can put in, since an enhanced weapon performs no higher than whoever put the Essence into it (The Bolt and the Enhanced Weapon has the law). Nobody makes Expert in a smiths’ guild on a hand that cannot put more than a C-Grade working into steel, because the test piece goes on the coil in front of the examiners, and the guild’s whole value is that the coil agreed.

A merchant above his title is a merchant selling on somebody else’s. Every port has a Grandmaster who lends a token to a sale for a fifth of the price and sits in the corner eating the seller’s pears, and since a Paragon merchant is a thing most realms have never seen, a Unique piece is almost never lawfully sold. It is given, inherited, lost at cards or taken off a body, and the Accord enters whichever of those it can prove.

Armor’s Tier is its proof. A strike against worked armor is read on the striker’s Ardency Penetration against the proof, whatever force sits behind it: high Flux with low Penetration hits like a padded wall, and the harness holds while the man inside takes the bruise. Proofed harness stops any strike whose Penetration reads no higher than a D-Grade working’s. The mark states the Tier, the house that tested it and the test. A strike one Tier above the proof breaks it; a strike two Tiers above passes as if it were not there. Plain armor has no proof and fights by its kind.

What a Piece Does for Its Wearer

Everything above is the piece fighting at its own Grade, and that law stands: a blade hits as its working, a harness stops what its proof says, and the Rarity is the argument it always was. A worked piece does a second thing besides. Worked gear multiplies its wearer. Its line names a Primary and a figure (a helm at Gnosis ×1.15, heavy plate at Vitality ×1.3 and Dexterity ×0.85), the wearer’s own numbers under that Primary are multiplied while the piece is lit, and the Primary is read again from there. Plain gear multiplies nothing, bonded gear’s multipliers grow with the bearer, and a relic’s read as question marks.

It is paid for out of the wearer’s reserve as a draw, light, steady or heavy, and the draw is taken through the layer the piece works on: armor and weapons draw on the Shell, soul-wards and charms on the Core, oath-rings and luck-tokens on the Layer, and all of it comes out of the one reserve. Everything worn together multiplies any one Primary by ×1.5 at most. A person’s sustainable figure is read with the gear off. Gear multiplies on top of that and can move the figure the sheet shows, and the two steps a fighter may boost are counted from the bare figure, never from the dressed one. A piece above its wearer’s Stage gives half or fights them. Worn Power has the whole law, with the sums, the trouble, and the charms and rings that do the same work for the soul.

Gear and the Three Roads

A proof-mark is a statement about matter, and matter is one of the three things that can happen to you. The Three Paths gives the long account. A buyer who means to live should know what the mark on his harness does and does not say.

Against a Body fighter everything above holds as written. At the top of that road the harness itself can be read: armor made too heavy to lift and a blade rusting in the hand. That is the Material Force working on existing substance, which is why a Plain coat of no great substance is sometimes the safer thing to be wearing on that day.

Against a Spirit hand, armor does nothing, because it does not cover the soul. No Tier of plate stops a Read, a Write or a hand on the Core, and the man inside defends with Integrity and Anchoring of his own, and his Ward against a read. A ward can be cut into a piece, or a Hallowed coat consecrated, and then it reads as a working at its own Tier and against the maker’s Spirit Mastery. A soul-ward, a charm or a reliquary worn against the skin goes further and multiplies the wearer’s own Ward and Harmonics. That is real protection at a very real price, and a smith who sells you a plain Hallowed mark as proof against the dead is selling you a Hallowed mark.

Against a Fate hand, a good mark is the best defense money can buy, for a reason the assay never tested. A pull moves only what already has a way of happening. A strap inspected this morning, a buckle from an honest house and a blade with no hidden flaw offer it nothing to lean on, and a forged mark is a way of happening with a stamp on it. The rich man’s untested Legendary and the poor fighter’s mended Common are both more dangerous to their owners on this road than on any other. At high Stage a pull can assign the result outright, and then no strap saves anyone, but it costs the hand a ruinous share of reserve to do it, and most would sooner have an easier target. Oath-rings and luck-tokens multiply the bearer’s Dominion and Resilience against such a hand, and a knot-cutter will take one knot’s debt onto itself and break.

A bonded piece is seated on the Attraction Layer, because a bond is what the Layer holds, and takes the owner’s own power through the Shell, Charge from a Body fighter and Essence from a Spirit or Fate one. So it can be pulled at through the bond, and a hand who breaks it hurts the owner on both layers. A Master Work is the nearest a craftsman comes to Fate in an object: a term on the world, that this door will hold and this cut will finish, which is why it ignores its material and why nobody has made one by trying.

What an Item Costs to Make

Costs are paid out of the maker’s reserve (the Measurewrights’ conversion is 1 EU = 1 MJ):

ItemCost to makeLasts
Enhanced bolt or arrow1% of the maker’s reserve each, whatever speed or hit is worked into it, with a floor of 0.001 EUthe maker’s Duration; consumed when loosed
Enhanced weapon (standing working)10% of the maker’s reserve, onceuntil the working is broken, read or unmade

Relicthe maker’s whole reserve, once, and a spell of Starvation after (Charge Starvation for a Body maker, Essence Starvation for a Spirit or Fate one)permanent while its material holds

A practitioner can equip themselves but cannot equip an army. A Stage II maker (reserve a few thousand EU) can prepare about ninety enhanced bolts from a full reserve before Starvation sets in, or nine enchanted blades, or three suits of proofed armor, or one relic.

Hide, Bone and Scale

Monster parts make gear, and the best gear going is monster gear, because something spent a long life and a great many other animals growing that scale. A part goes to the bench as a material like any other. On the day it comes off the carcass it assays at the beast’s own Grade and Level on the coil, so an A-Grade beast’s plates hold what Aethersteel holds and a drake that reads SS is where Drakehide Steel gets its name. The trade says all this in the Grade the Commission posts a beast at, and for most beasts that is close enough, an A-Grade beast’s plates and an SS drake’s hide being the common way to put it (older hall rolls give the same rows as Threat Tiers, T1 to T9). Where the posted Grade comes from what a beast does and not what it is made of, the coil wins, and a Lamp-drinker’s hide does not hold what its Gold Sigil promised. Then it starts to fall, because a part begins losing grade from the moment the animal stops, and the whole beast trade is an argument with decomposition. Crews have run a wyvern’s wing-leather forty miles in a sack of snow on the understanding that the runner keeps a share and his feet are his own affair.

The old law sits on top of it without any exception. The maker’s title still caps the stamp, so an SS-Grade scale in a Journeyman’s hands makes a Tempered coat with an extremely handsome surface. What the material gives for nothing is its plain toughness, since a hide that turned spears on the living animal goes on turning them whether a working is in it or not.

Two things come with monster gear that a buyer does not get with steel.

It carries the beast. Scale and bone are Creature-Sourced, the same as the Drafts rendered from a core, and a little of what the animal was stays in what it became. A Tinder-fox-hide jerkin keeps its wearer up at night. A coat cut from a pack-hunter has been known to make a quiet man uneasy when he is alone. Good tanners cure most of it out, cheap ones sell it as character, and the Index lists the trouble as Identity Contamination wherever anybody has troubled to measure.

It carries the name. A famous monster has an epithet and a bounty on it before it has a buyer, and the gear made from it inherits both. Rarity runs on this, because there was only ever one Anner Moss Wyvern, and every hunter in the hundred swears he owns its left wing.

Bonded Gear

Gear stays what it is. A Hallowed blade in a Stage XII hand is a Hallowed blade, and it will fail him on the same day it would have failed its first owner, with its stamp still telling the truth about both of them. What it multiplies in him is the same share it multiplied in that first owner, and a share of a Stage XII number is a great deal more.

A bonded piece is the one kind that grows. A fighter who has carried a weapon or a coat for years and worked their art through it can seat it in the soul, and from then on it is part of them: it carries part of their art and part of their body, there is no seam between it and the hand, and it takes its part in a release. Its multipliers on the bearer grow as it does. It has no voice of its own, whatever its owner tells people in taverns. As the owner climbs, so does the piece, and the soul reports the change on its owner’s window, in the plain text block that nobody else can read:

[Bonded: Long Harriet]
Grade: C ▸ B
Holds: 62%

The maker’s title stops mattering at that point, because the Essence in it is now the owner’s. The material still matters. A bonded piece climbs until it reaches what its stuff can hold and there it stops, so a farm boy who bonds his grandfather’s Stillband hatchet at fifteen will find, at about Stage IV, that the hatchet has gone as far as hatchets go, and he has a decision to make that no minstrel has ever got right. A wise master tells a student to bond nothing cheap. The student has generally already done it.

The stamp on a bonded piece is a portrait of it as a child. Assay houses will strike a new one for a fee, and most bonded fighters do not bother, on the sound ground that a stranger who reads Proofed on the hilt is a stranger who has made a mistake.

Master Works

The whole register, piece by piece, is in Master Works.

Here the law on this page stops holding, once per lifetime, and the trade would very much like you not to count on it.

A Master Work is a piece that does what its Grade and material should not be able to, made once at the end of a lifetime, always Unique or Mythic, and named. The coil reads it at its proper Grade, the material is the material, and the thing goes on doing what it does regardless. Nobody has made one by trying harder, nobody has made a second, and most of the people who have tried to learn how from the maker found them unhelpful, or dead. Each area of the world has its own, and each trade.

The doorstep of the assay room at Ambleford is one. Aldous Wray, a mason of eighty-eight with four teeth and a bad opinion of everyone, cut it out of plain Greystone in his last winter and called it Enough, and at the spring fair of the following year a drunk Expert of the Blue Herons, invited to try, broke his own wrist on it. By the table above a strike two Tiers over the proof goes through as if the stone were not there. The Accord has tested that step with everything it could get to Ambleford. It is stamped Proofed, because Proofed is what the coil says, and Unique beside that, because the clerk could think of nothing else, and people wipe their feet on it. Measurewrights have written eleven treatises on why it cannot be doing that. One of them has been shown to be right about why, and the step has not been informed.

Every trade keeps its own list and its own bragging. The Cardmere dyers have a red that does not fade in Vantabriel light, the salt road has a knot, and the coast keeps a net that has never once come up empty and that no fisher will agree to own. Each is the last thing its maker did. In the alchemists’ halls the great one is a Fate Essence Draft: the working of probability itself bottled in a vial, extremely hard to make, a handful in the world at any time, and very coveted. For nearly everyone alive it is the only way Fate Arts will ever come within reach, and great families have gone to war over a stoppered inch of it without being entirely sure what it would do.

Relics, and the Ones That Are Not on This Page

Made relics and the lesser relics that come up off old battlefields are written the same on the roll: a Grade, a stamp and Mythic Rarity, because very few makers have ever been willing to spend themselves empty on one piece.

True relics are off the ladder. The works of the Great Spirit War and of the days of the Physical Epochs, when Bara, Ashura and Aegor walked in human form, read as question marks to any appraiser of any strength, on any instrument. They cannot be repaired or copied, and they may have opinions about who holds them, which the holder generally learns by having the matter settled for them. People kill for them anyway. The Accord stamps them with nothing and enters them under a dash, and a merchant who offers you one has found a very old sword, or has found a way to die.

Appraisal and Forgery

Three things read an item. An assay instrument does it best: the holding coil gives Grade on a good day and the assay fork gives density, and between the two a careful house can tell what a piece is and sometimes what it has been. The Appraisal skill is the one you carry, a trade skill that rises with use and shows on your own sheet as one of the nine ranks, Initiate to Paragon, like Baking or Swordsmanship. Strong Gnosis (the stat of perceiving, reading and noticing) helps both, and it is why a Harmonist can tell a working’s alignment and Duration without touching it. An appraiser’s read arrives as a window, and the window reports what the skill found:

[Appraisal: Journeyman]
Short sword. Grade C. Rarity: Uncommon.
Stamp: Tempered. Mark: Hesketh, Ambleford. Reads true.
Past owners: ???

A relic off the old fields gives the same window with nothing but question marks in it, and experienced buyers treat that as a reading.

A good forger beats a weak appraiser. Forgery is a skill too, on the same nine ranks, and the rank of the skill in use decides the contest. A Journeyman forger has cut a Tempered mark that an Apprentice appraiser’s own soul will report as true, and that appraiser will swear to it before a magistrate with a clear conscience. The usual frauds are a real blade with a false stamp, a low working dressed to run bright for a month, a past owner added to the list, and a Common piece aged into a Rare one in a bucket of cow urine. A cheap proof-mark on repaired steel is hardly even a crime. Provenance is the dearest thing to fake and the hardest to catch, because no instrument has ever measured who died holding something.

So a sane buyer above Rare hires two appraisers who dislike each other and puts the piece on a coil besides, and the coil will not read Rarity at all.

A forged title token is another order of crime. A stamp lies about a sword. A token lies about a person, and the whole coalition runs on a stranger being able to hand you the goods because the silver at your throat says so. Every hall in every realm hangs for it, including halls that are at law with each other, and a forged token is one of the few subjects on which the Accord has ever been observed to agree with itself.

Marks, Names and Past Owners

Two things ride on a good piece besides its Grade, and a buyer who ignores either one has already met the people above.

The guild mark. A stamp under the Accord’s stamp, naming the maker and the shop. Craftsmen shout their marks in the street and brag about them at table, a Marked knife from a loud shop outsells a Proofed one from a quiet shop, and the Accord’s stamp is the only part of the arrangement anybody can sue over. A guild mark tells you who to blame. It changes nothing on the ladder.

The name. Top gear has a name, a reputation and a list of past owners that the public knows, and heralds and minstrels carry all three, wrongly. A piece does not need a Hallowed stamp to get a name; it needs one famous evening. By the Storied Tier a piece has usually had several names, the most famous one winning, and a buyer pays for the list of who died holding it as readily as for the Grade. The oldest and worst of them come off the dead battlefields, and the people who sell those generally cannot say who made them or what the maker’s ceiling was, which the next owner finds out.

The name sits on the same ladder as everything else. Grandmother’s Patience has the second-best-known name in its valley and no Tier at all.

On a Card

Name · Kind, stamp (Grade X), Rarity, material Tn. One line of what it does. For example: Slyvine’s Scarf · Relic, Proofed (Grade D), Mythic, T2 or above. Can stretch, tighten, or harden to act as a rope or a weak shield.
