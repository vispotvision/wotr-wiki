Worn Power — Gear on the Sheet

Worn Power — Gear on the Sheet

Nov 17, 202619 min read

A sword tells you what it can do. A coat tells you what you can do in it, and charges you by the hour for the news.

Said at the Ambleford assay counter, usually to a young man holding his father’s purse

The Vance twins rode into the Kessen brackets in the same summer with the same inheritance, and the bookies priced them as one fighter. Aubrey Vance spent his half the way the minstrels would have advised. He walked onto the sand in the Larkspur Harness (Legendary, sky-blue enamel over Grimbarite, the plate three dead champions had worn), with the Legendary helm Widow’s Lantern on his head and the Legendary gauntlets called the Tax Collectors on his hands, and for the first minute he was the most beautiful thing in the county and very nearly the strongest. The crowd could see the blue of his aura standing off the plate like noon on a lake. What the crowd could not see was his window, where three lines each read Shell draw: heavy and the bar under them was going down like a tide. He was at half before he had been hit. At a quarter his hands were shaking inside the Tax Collectors, the harness went dark piece by piece as his reserve could no longer keep it lit, and a caravan guard in mended Common mail walked in and knocked him flat with the boss of a borrowed shield.

His sister Odile sold two of her three pieces before the season opened, kept a single Very Rare brigandine she could afford to feed, and spent the rest on a trainer and on dinner. She won the Kessen bracket four years running. Asked about her brother, she said he had been wearing more than he could eat.

Both halves of that story are the law. Good gear makes a strong fighter stronger than anything else money can buy, and every piece of it has to be paid for twice, once at the counter and again out of the wearer’s own reserve for as long as it is worn.

What a Worked Piece Does

Grade and Rarity says what a piece is. A blade still cuts at its own Grade, a harness still stops what its proof says it stops, and none of that changes here. This page is about the other thing a worked piece does, which is change the person inside it.

A worked piece multiplies a Sub-Stat. Its line names one or two of the sixty-four single measures (Perception, Fortitude, Celerity and the rest, set out in the Eight Primaries and the Sixty-Four Sub-Stats) and a figure, and while the piece is worn and lit the wearer’s own number in that Sub-Stat is multiplied by it. The Sub-Stat’s Grade is read off the new number. The Primary above it is then added up again from its eight entries, and its Grade is read off the new mean.

Take a scout whose Perception stands at 160, a high C, and put a helm on her that reads Perception ×1.15. Her Perception is now 184, which is B, and in any contest that calls on Perception she is a B. Her Gnosis total has gone up by twenty-four and its mean by three, which will move the letter on the register only if she was standing at the edge of one. The helm has made her better at one thing by a clear margin and has barely touched her broad shape, and that is how gear generally behaves.

There are four kinds of gear, and the sheet treats each differently.

KindWhat it isWhat it does to the wearerOn the window
Plain gearIron, leather, wood and cloth with no working in itNothing. It performs as plain matter does, by the world’s old habits, and good plain steel is still good steelNo line
Worked gearRuned, Draft-tempered, or cut from monster partsMultiplies the Sub-Stats named on its line, for a Shell drawA Worn block, with the multipliers and the draw
Bonded gearA piece seated in its owner’s soulMultiplies as worked gear does, and its multipliers grow as the bearer climbsA Bonded block, with the figures moving
RelicsThe true works of the old wars and the walking EpochsWhatever it pleasesQuestion marks

Plain gear is most of what anybody owns, and nobody should be ashamed of it. A levy pikeman’s kettle hat multiplies nothing and has saved more lives than every Legendary on the continent together.

Worked gear is the trade. The three common ways of making it give three common flavors. Rune-cut pieces carry clean, narrow lines and are the easiest to read on a coil. Draft-tempered pieces, quenched or soaked in rendered stock, tend to carry bodily lines (Fortitude, Constitution, Tolerance) and a faint smell of the alchemist’s yard. Monster gear carries what the animal was good at, so a night-hunter’s hide sharpens Perception and a river beast’s plate thickens Fortitude, and it carries a little of the animal’s temper along with it.

Bonded gear begins as worked or even plain, and after the bond its line is the owner’s affair. The multipliers rise as the bearer rises, up to what the material can hold and no further.

Relics multiply, or do something that looks like multiplying, and no window, coil or appraiser has ever got a figure out of one. The wearer’s own sheet shows the piece’s name and a row of question marks. Holders report that the figure appears to depend on whether the relic approves of what they are doing.

Why It Favors the Strong

A multiplier is a share, and a share of a great deal is a great deal. This is the whole reason the wealthy fight better than their training deserves, and it deserves an example with the sums written out.

A field harness of worked plate reads Fortitude ×1.3. Buckle it on a levy sergeant whose Fortitude is 60, a middling D, and he stands at 78, still a D, tougher by eighteen points and no letter. Buckle the same harness on a sworn champion whose Fortitude is 250, a B, and she stands at 325, an A. One full Grade up decides an exchange outright. The sergeant got a better coat and the champion became a different kind of problem.

So the purse and the training compound. A house that can buy its heir a Stage, a trainer and a harness has bought three things that each make the other two worth more, and a poor fighter of equal talent meets that heir a letter behind in the two or three Sub-Stats that matter. Poor fighters do win. They win by making the rich one pay the draw, by getting past the harness to something it does not cover, and by not being where the strong Sub-Stat is pointing.

The Shell Draw

A worked piece is dark until its wearer lights it, out of the wearer’s own reserve, for as long as it is worn in earnest. Armor and weapons, which are most of what anybody means by gear, are lit through the Aether Shell, and the window calls this the Shell draw and grades it in three words.

DrawWhat it is likeTypical of
LightA trickle a fighter stops noticing. It can be worn all dayMost Uncommon and Rare pieces with one or two modest lines
SteadyA real, constant spend. Every working the wearer runs holds for less time from full than the card saysVery Rare pieces, and anything with a strong line
HeavyAs costly as holding a working of your own the whole time it is litLegendary and Mythic pieces, and nearly anything that carries three lines

One heavy piece is a decision a strong reserve can afford. Two is a short fight. Three is Aubrey Vance. The tells are the same ones any spending shows, the short breath at half and the tremor at a quarter, and an opponent who sees them on a fighter who has not yet thrown a working knows where the reserve is going. When the reserve cannot feed a piece it goes dark, its line drops off the window, and the wearer is suddenly standing in plain plate with the trade-off still attached.

The draw goes by the piece. A piece is lit through the layer of the soul it works on. Armor and weapons draw on the Shell. Soul-wards, charms and reliquaries draw on the Core, and the window prints Core draw. Oath-rings, luck-tokens and knot-cutters draw on the Attraction Layer, and the window prints Layer draw. There is still only the one reserve, and all three are paid out of it, so a Body fighter with a ward at his throat is spending the same store on his plate and his locket. What changes is where the strain shows: a heavy Shell draw runs the wearer hot, a heavy Core draw leaves them a little flat and far away, and a Layer draw shows as nothing at all until the small luck around them starts to sour.

A fighter may let a piece go dark on purpose to save the draw, and light it again when it is wanted. Lighting it takes a breath and shows, since the aura runs out over the piece as it wakes. Veterans do this constantly. It is the main skill of wearing good gear, and no shop teaches it.

The Cap

All the gear a person wears, taken together, multiplies any one Sub-Stat by ×1.5 at most. A harness at Fortitude ×1.3 and a tempered belt at Fortitude ×1.3 do not make their wearer nearly twice as hard to break. The soul reads ×1.5, stops, and goes on charging the draw for both. Two lines on one Sub-Stat multiply together (×1.2 and ×1.1 make ×1.32), and whatever the product comes to, the window holds it to ×1.5. Nor does any amount of gear lift a Sub-Stat past the ceiling its owner’s Stage allows: the line still prints, and the stat stops where the Stage stops it, which is the whole reason a rich nobody in Mythic plate is still a nobody, only a better-dressed one.

This cap is gear’s own. It stands apart from the cap on a practitioner’s own power-ups, the two Grades over sustainable that Stacking and Spending sets out, and gear does not count toward that one. The sustainable Grade is read bare, with every piece off and dark, and it is the bare Grade the two are counted from. Gear multiplies on top, and it can move the letter the sheet shows, as the champion’s harness moved her Fortitude from B to A. Her boosts still stop two Grades above the B she owns, and the harness then multiplies whatever number that comes to, under its own ×1.5. So a fighter in a good coat may still focus, vow, call and release to her full two Grades, and no coat has ever bought anyone a third. Gear adds no fracture risk either. What it adds is the draw, and the draw is why a fighter in three Legendaries has very little reserve left to stack anything with.

Gear Above Your Stage

A piece can be too much for the person wearing it. When its Grade stands above the highest letter the wearer’s Stage lets their own Sub-Stats reach, the Shell cannot carry the whole working, and one of two things happens.

It works at half. The piece gives half its gain, so a line of ×1.3 reads ×1.15, at the full draw. Most pieces do this, and most heirs never notice, because nobody has told them what the line ought to say.

It fights them. A proud piece, a monster piece with a strong temper, or anything bonded to somebody else for a long time may refuse outright. The line reads below one. The plate drags, the helm narrows the eyes it was cut to open, and the draw climbs. Every armorer has a story about a boy who could not get his grandfather’s gauntlets off.

A bought Stage helps with this exactly as much as it helps with anything, which is to say that it opens the ceiling and the fit follows.

Trade-offs

Lines below one are honest, and nearly all heavy gear carries one. Heavy worked plate reads Fortitude ×1.3 and Celerity ×0.85, and no rune yet cut has made forty pounds of steel weigh nothing. On the champion above, with Celerity at 240, that is 204, still a B but a slower B, and against a fighter who was level with her on speed it is now a contest she loses on the first step. On the levy sergeant the same line takes him from 80 to 68.

The common bargains are well known to the trade. Plate gives Fortitude and takes Celerity. A deep hood or a closed helm cut for Perception often takes a little off Empathy, since it reads threats and stops reading faces. Draft-tempered pieces that thicken Constitution tend to dull Threshold the wrong way, so the wearer feels every hour of the march. A smith who tells you a heavy piece has no second line is either a Master Work’s maker or a liar, and the first kind are all dead.

Multipliers below one are not softened by the cap. Nothing in the law stops a fool from stacking three slow pieces and standing still.

Trouble

Forged lines. A forger who cannot fake a working can dress a low one to run bright for a month, and a line that reads Perception ×1.15 at the counter may be a true ×1.03 with a bright coat of paint. The buyer’s own window reports what the working is doing on the day, so it agrees with the forgery until the dressing burns off, and it burns off faster under load. The usual moment is the first hard exchange of the first real fight. An Appraisal at a Grade above the forger’s skill catches it, and so does a coil. The stamp does not, since the stamp is the thing that was forged.

Cursed gear. A curse on a piece is nearly always a line with terms, written by whoever made it, and the window shows the terms only as the maker chose to write them (see Blessings and Curses). Fortitude ×1.4 while the bearer has not retreated is a fine line until the day the bearer needs to retreat and discovers what the rest of the sentence was. The well-known kinds are the piece that will not come off, the piece whose draw doubles at night, and the piece that is generous to its wearer and takes the difference out of whoever stands nearest.

The flex. Gear is worn to be seen, and its lines are said out loud by everybody. Owners announce their multipliers at dinner. Rivals halve them in the retelling. A smith will price a stranger’s coat across a street to within ten gold, loudly, as a courtesy to the trade. An appraiser may read the line off a piece without asking, if the skill is good enough, and at a bracket ground there is always one sitting at the rail calling the figures to the bookies. All of this brings what it has always brought: thieves, challengers, creditors, and the man who has heard exactly what your harness does and has come dressed for the Sub-Stat it leaves alone.

The Window

The wearer’s soul reports a worked piece in a short block, unseen by anyone else, when the piece is lit and whenever its state changes:

[Worn: Ashjaw Helm · Rare · Grade B]
[Perception ×1.15 · Threshold ×1.1]
[Shell draw: light]

A piece working at half says so, and a piece that has gone dark drops to its name alone:

[Worn: Larkspur Harness · Legendary · Grade S]
[Fortitude ×1.15 (half) · Tolerance ×1.1 (half) · Celerity ×0.85]
[Shell draw: heavy]

A Sub-Stat that gear has moved shows its shifted Grade on the sheet, with the piece named beside it, in the manner Windows and the Sheet gives for any shifted figure. A bonded piece shows its multipliers ticking up as its owner does. A relic shows this, and experienced holders have stopped asking:

[Worn: ???]
[??? ×???]
[Shell draw: ???]

Soul-Wards and Thread-Pieces

Plate covers the body. Two older trades cover the rest of a person, and neither looks like much on a rack.

Spirit gear is worn against hands that reach the soul. Soul-wards, charms and reliquaries multiply Resilience Ward and the entries of Harmonics, most often Stability and Attunement. A soul-ward is a cut or consecrated thing worn against the skin, a disc, a cord, a stitched band inside a collar. A charm is its cheap cousin, sold at every shrine gate, and the honest ones carry a small true line. A reliquary holds something of a person who was hard to move in life (a finger bone, a braid, the ash of a letter), and it lends the wearer a little of that steadiness. Their draw is usually light, it is a Core draw and not a Shell one, and they show nothing to the eye when lit, which is half their value. No plate does this work at any Grade, and a fighter in a Legendary harness with nothing at his throat is a strongbox with the lid off as far as a Spirit hand is concerned.

Fate gear is rarer, dearer and harder to prove. Oath-rings, luck-tokens and knot-cutters multiply Dominion and Resilience Continuity. An oath-ring is cut when a vow is sworn and strengthens the standing of the one who swore it, Command and Pressure most often, for exactly as long as the vow is kept. A luck-token steadies its bearer against being moved, displaced or made the one the accident happens to, which is Continuity’s business. Fate gear is lit through the Attraction Layer, out of the same reserve as everything else. Counterfeits outnumber true ones perhaps fifty to one, since a false luck-token is very hard to tell from a true one on a quiet day.

A knot-cutter is the strange one. Every pull on Thread leaves a knot, a debt the world collects from the puller later and in its own coin. A knot-cutter carries a small Continuity line while it is whole, and it can do one further thing, once: it takes a single knot’s debt onto itself and breaks. The bearer chooses the moment by cutting, snapping or unpicking it. The misfortune that was coming lands on the piece, and the piece is gone, generally in a manner that makes clear what it spared its owner. Fate houses buy them by the case and never admit to it. Nobody has made one that takes two.

Some Pieces on the Roll

Each entry gives the kind, the stamp with its Grade, the Rarity and the item Tier. The T on a piece is the item Tier, the rung of material and maker’s standing set out in Grade and Rarity, and has nothing to do with the Danger Tier posted on a beast. The soul-wards and rings below draw on the Core and the Layer, as above.

The Ashjaw Helm · Helm, Instrument (Grade B), Rare, T4. Perception ×1.15, Threshold ×1.1, light draw. Cut by Adept Brannoch Sele of the Kessen road from the lower jaw of an Ashjaw, a gray tunnel-hunter that finds its prey by the heat of breath, with the teeth left in along the brow and the bone smoked the color of slate. Sele’s mark is a hooked tooth under the left cheek-plate. It has had three owners. The first was the crew captain Dunmore Halse, who paid for the kill, and the second was his caller, who took it off him when he did not come up. It last sold at Kessen for 240 gold to a woman who would not give a name, and the trade considers the price fair and the buyer’s silence worrying.

The Larkspur Harness · Full plate, Storied (Grade S), Legendary, T6. Fortitude ×1.3, Celerity ×0.85, Tolerance ×1.2, heavy draw. Sky-blue enamel over Grimbarite plate with a Frostwool arming coat, made by Master Ysolde Carrow of Altherion for the champion Ser Edrik Lark, who died in it of old age, to general surprise. Two champions wore it after him and one of those did not die of old age. House Vance bought it at the Lark estate sale for 11,000 gold. It hangs in their hall now with the straps let out, because Odile Vance bought it back from her brother’s creditors and has never worn it.

Sister Maud’s Knuckle · Reliquary, Proofed (Grade D), Uncommon, T2. Ward ×1.1, Stability ×1.05, light draw. A pewter locket the size of a walnut holding one finger bone of a spital sister who is said to have sat up forty years with the dying and never once been frightened by what came for them. The spital at Marchbank had the bone cut into eleven lockets by a Journeyman it will not name, and sells one when the roof needs doing, at 9 gold. Soldiers going north buy them. The bone in at least four is mutton.

The Tollkeeper’s Promise · Oath-ring, Tempered (Grade C), Rare, T3. Command ×1.15, Continuity ×1.1 while the oath on it is kept, light draw. A plain Ghoststeel band cut by the Adept ringwright Hale Morrow for a bridge-warden who swore that nobody would cross his bridge unpaid. It worked for thirty-one years. The warden’s son inherited the ring without inheriting the oath, wore it to a bracket, and found that it reads ×1.0 on a hand that has promised nothing. It is in a pawnshop at Ostrey at 85 gold, and the pawnbroker tells every customer the story, since he would rather not be sued.

A Kessen knot-cutter · Knot-cutter, Instrument (Grade B), Very Rare, T4. Continuity ×1.05 while whole, light draw, and the one cut. A finger’s length of red cord with nine knots tied by a cordwright who walks the Fate road, sealed in wax and usually worn sewn inside a cuff. From the two houses known to make them the price is 600 gold, by introduction, and the houses do not give receipts. The best-known use is that of the factor Peregrine Stroud, who pulled once on a grain auction, unpicked his cord that evening, and watched his own warehouse roof come down an hour later on nobody. He bought another the following morning.
