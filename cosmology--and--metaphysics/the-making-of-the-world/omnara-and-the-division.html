Omnara and the Division

Omnara and the Division

Nov 17, 202610 min read

“The Apeiron does not remember the Division, for it was not conscious in any way the post-Division cosmos would recognize. To remember requires sequence. Omnara contained all sequences simultaneously, and therefore remembered nothing, and therefore forgot nothing, and therefore was everything that memory and forgetting would later describe.”

Lattice of Names, Fragment Zero

On the morning of the spring equinox, three hundred singers of the Sect of the One Note climbed the green hill above Ossery in robes of cloth-of-gold, with some four thousand people behind them carrying picnics, to sing the world back to where it started. Their abbess, Iseult Lisle, had spent thirty years on the theory: a chord holding every pitch at once, in which no voice could be told from any other, would be the Apeiron itself made audible, and whoever stood inside it would know what the cosmos knew before it had parts. At sunrise she brought her hand down. Three hundred coats came up at once, ember and teal and arterial red and every other hue the sect had recruited, and as the chord closed the colors ran together up the hillside into a white that no Wellspring owns and nobody present could look at. The noise had no pitch at all. People a mile off said it was like standing inside every bell foundry in the world. Glass broke in three parishes, the cows in the low meadow dropped their calves early, the river at Ossery stood up in small square waves, and thirty-seven of the singers never heard another thing as long as they lived. It lasted until they ran out of breath, which was a little under a minute. The Guild’s observer, eating an onion on the far slope with wool in his ears, entered the event as a complete success at producing noise, and added that a totality does not contain partitions and that the abbess had used three hundred of them to build it.

They were wrong in a very grand way, and I love them for it. Every school that has ever gone looking for what came before the first thing has been wrong in some way, and here is what they were all looking for.

Omnara: The Apeiron

The Formless Totality · The Womb Before Naming

Before causality drew its first breath, before weight existed to anchor thought, before awareness discovered itself in the dark between uncounted infinities, there was Omnara.

Omnara is the name given by the earliest Parunic archivists to the state of absolute undifferentiated unity that preceded all existence. The word itself is borrowed from the Proto-Parunic root omn, meaning “completeness without boundary,” and ara, meaning “womb” or “the place from which emergence is permitted.” Together, Omnara translates roughly as The Womb of All Completion, a title the archivists used with visible reluctance, aware that naming the unnameable was itself a kind of transgression.

The archivists refuse Omnara the name of being and the name of force alike. Omnara was the condition of totality before totality required compartments.

Every law that would later govern reality, every Archon that would later embody a principle, every Wellspring that would later pool in the ground of the world and turn a Crystal to its own nature, every soul that would later struggle toward Temperance, all of it existed within Omnara as potential without partition. Fate was not yet separate from Body. Spirit had no boundary against Matter. Attraction and Obsession were the same current flowing in a direction that did not yet exist.

To call Omnara a god would be to diminish it by suggesting it had intention prior to the mechanisms of intention. To call it a place would be to impose space upon something that predated the geometry of placement. Omnara simply was, and within that was lay everything the cosmos would ever become, compressed into a singularity of undifferentiated meaning.

The Division

The Division is the cosmological event that ended the Apeiron and birthed the tripartite architecture of existence. No surviving record describes why it occurred. The oldest fragments treat it as an inevitability encoded within Omnara’s own totality, with no choice anywhere in it: a unity that contains all possibilities must eventually contain the possibility of its own separation.

The Measurewrights, who cannot leave anything alone, have an account of their own. Take a pail of very still water on a very cold night, they say, and it can sink well past freezing and stay liquid, perfectly even, with no reason in it to become one thing rather than another, until a single nudge sets the whole pail to ice along the lines the water’s own nature allows. They call this the Pail, and they say totality was the pail. The archivists answer that a pail implies a shelf and a nudge implies a neighbor. Neither side has ever given an inch, and the record says nothing either way.

From Omnara’s singularity, three primal currents unfurled, each carrying a distinct axis of what existence would require to function as a coherent system.

Two Realities, and What Stands Above Them

The three planes are not three of a kind, and no stair runs between them. The Physical Plane is physical reality under the epoch Aegor, who rose from its current: matter, body and the world’s settled habits, where truth becomes event, ruin and mountain range. Those habits were settled in the Physical Epochs and have never been overturned, by any working, at any Stage. The Soul Plane is Spiritual Reality, the stratum through which abstract law becomes a soul’s felt truth, and Ashura rose from its current. Those two are the lower realities, pressed so close against each other that a person lives in both at once, and neither stands over the other. The Plane of Fate, from which Bara rose, is above them, and it is no reality in any careful sense. It is everything that flows, every possibility, idea and concept, anything that can happen in the two below before it has, the lattice all of it hangs from and the law and construction of the world, and “plane” is a courtesy.

“Reality is the shape that possibility chooses to wear for a little while. The Plane of Fate is the wardrobe.”

Fragment of the Lattice of Names

Wherever distinct planar laws border each other, a transition zone must exist. That transition zone is the Veil, a structural necessity generated by the three-plane cosmology rather than a fourth plane in its own right. It is the skin of the lower realities: both of them lie inside it, its inner fold runs between them, and its outer face is where they meet Fate. The Veil has the whole account.

Students will meet an older picture of all this, because every field surveyor still carries it. “Depth, the nested plane model. Fate deepest, Soul between, Physical at the surface. A Well is not a hole in the ground. It is a place where the surface has thinned enough that the middle layer reaches through.” It is a good rule for the job it does. It gives the order in which a Well opens to somebody standing on one with a rope around their waist: the ground first, the soul’s country next, and last of all the thing that makes no sense down there. It says nothing about where that thing stands, and the word deepest has been misleading students for as long as there have been students. Fate is what the other two depend on. It is above them, and a surveyor who reaches it from below has only come at it from the one side a surveyor can.

Bara rose from the First Current and wrote the Works of Hataraki inside that plane before there was a before to write them in. The Fourteen Archons came out of the tension among the three who rose from these currents, and what came was those Works crowned: a law with somebody on the throne. Most if not all of them are Sovereign on all three planes, and on the Plane of Fate they are the laws as well as the powers, with Uurgath and Urion unparalleled there among the rest because what they are cannot be conceived. Bara stands above every one of them. They are the most powerful things that still act up there, which is a smaller claim than it sounds. The Titans descend from the Archons in their turn, and everything else on these shelves comes down from there.

What the Division Left Over

A cut leaves offcuts, as any dyer at Wenlock Cross will tell you, and the most useful thing in the world turns out to be the part of Omnara nobody used.

Aether (the rest of the world’s books call it mana) is the world’s own substance, what remains of Omnara undivided. The three currents carried off sequence, substance and awareness, and what they left behind still holds every possibility with no partition in it. That is why it can carry any idea. A single medium burns in one valley and grieves in the next because it has no nature of its own to get in the way, and Aegor compacted it into the first strata of matter for the same reason a mason likes clean sand.

Only a body can pull it. An Echo cannot, a thought cannot, and a soul with its body left behind in a chair goes on spending what it walked in with. The medium is endless, and any one ground’s supply is not, since a stretch of country fills at its own rate and can be drawn down faster than it fills. Fighters call the two ends fat ground and lean ground, and the Measurewrights have a table.

That ground is a sore subject. An auditor of the Guild once set the shortfall described in The Crossing, what the rendering houses take from the Wellsprings in transit, beside the seventy-year rate of the Withering, and observed that the two columns looked very much like one question. She was told that two columns that look alike are two columns. The Guild cannot prove otherwise, and I notice that it still has her figures.

The Wellsprings did not come out of Aether. They ride in it. The Sixty come from the Archons by way of the Titans: Titans are the Wellsprings themselves, and each of the Sixty is one way a Titan’s law shows up, carried by this medium that will carry anything.

The soul came last and owes all three who rose. The Three Epochs says who gave it what, and From Omnara to a Blow walks the whole road from this page down to somebody’s fist.
