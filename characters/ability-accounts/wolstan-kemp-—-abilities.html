Wolstan Kemp — Abilities in full

Wolstan Kemp — Abilities in full

Nov 28, 20267 min read

Card: Wolstan Kemp

Techniques

GOING ONCE! GOING TWICE! GONE! · Signature Art

GOING ONCE! GOING TWICE! GONE! is basically resonance striking: the power to load a thing with its own shiver until it cannot hold, and then finish it on a plane he chose before he began. It is named in plain Midmarch English for a roup-crier’s three calls. He sells the post three times, and on the third it is gone. He has no Traits; what the yard has seen is one art and twenty years of selling it over his own flags.

Capabilities

It is an auction, and he means every part of the word. A lot is put up, the bids are taken in order, each bid stands on the book until the next one tops it, and the hammer falls once. The art does the same thing to a body of wood or iron.

The charge is open and slow and anyone can read it. He rolls his sleeves, left and then right, plants his feet wide on the flags with the right a little behind and the knees soft, and settles his whole bulk onto the stone like a laden wagon onto its axles. He looks at the thing he means to break and finds the flaw in it before he touches it; on the yard’s pell that was the drying check across the crown, the crack oak opens along its rays as it seasons. He lays his left hand flat on it, almost gently, and breathes in, and his crown comes down on the yard. As Ned saw it, he seems to drink the yard, pulling up through his boots the raw stuff of the ground (Aether to the scholars, mana to the carters), and the flags go pale and dry. It is packed raw in his Shell, the body-side layer of the soul, and pushed out through his skin into his hands, where it shows as beaten copper climbing from the fingertips. By his own mouth more than half of what he puts in reaches the target, near two parts in three on a good day.

Then the three calls, and each one is a bid. They are three short palm-heel pushes from the hips, each at the instant the post’s shiver comes back to him, and a bid once called stands on the book: each push stays in the wood until the next one tops it, and none of them is allowed to lapse. Every solid thing has a pace it swings at when it is struck, the bell-founders’ note, and a push given at that pace adds to the swing already there; it is how the Minster ringers get a tenor bell that weighs as much as an ox up from dead still with small pulls on a rope, each taken at the top of the swing. No bell-ringer could do it to oak, because oak swallows its own ring and goes dead in a heartbeat. This is the woken half of the art. His copper sheathes the post and keeps each push in the wood like money on account, so oak that should go dead keeps ringing, the shiver running down to the socket and back to the crown and losing nothing on the way, because a bid on the book does not lapse. The Measurewrights call the pace the natural frequency, the adding resonance and the swallowing damping, the rubbing inside the wood that turns a shiver to a little warmth; their sums for a bell are good, and they cannot say why a man’s copper should keep oak from going dead. The second call therefore comes down on the whole of the first, and the post stops shivering and starts to sway, its crown moving like a mast and the check in its end-grain yawning and shutting with every swing.

The third call is the edge of the right hand held stiff as a froe. A froe is the cleaving blade a cooper sets in the end of a log to split staves, and it works because wood is strong along its grain and weak between its rays. The hand does the froe’s work at the one instant the swing has already pulled that weak plane open as wide as it will go, so the blow has only to finish what the post is doing to itself. It is the auction’s rule and nothing stranger: three small pushes, none of which could mark the oak, add up to a break because none of them was allowed to die.

Applications

  • Riving the yard’s pell, twenty years in its socket, from top to bottom
  • Breaking a thing along a flaw he found before he touched it
  • By his own boast it works on a man (“You want to know what I pay on a man, you’ll have to hire me”), though nobody has seen that

Techniques

  • GOING ONCE! - a short palm-heel push from the hips, the kind a man gives a stuck door, and a ring of copper runs round the struck thing and sheathes it top to bottom while it starts to shiver
  • GOING TWICE! - the same push in the same place at the instant the shiver comes back to his hand, so it lands on top of the first and the thing begins to sing and sway
  • GONE! - the right hand brought down edge-on, the copper run together into one bright line, along the plane he picked before he began, at the top of the swing, and the thing rives top to bottom

Associations

  • No Wellspring is named for it; the smell of a bell-founder’s yard is all Ned has to go on
  • The Body Path, in the Form of a Strike
  • He came by it living, in a dead king’s tourney ring and twenty years of his own yard
  • Reading the grain, his habit of finding the flaw in a post, a blade or a man before he touches it
  • His colors are beaten copper-orange, like a new copper pan held to a fire, with a heat-haze over them and a smell of hot pennies
  • The yard cries his calls with him like a crowd at a roup
  • No Release has been shown; if he has one he did not spend it on a post

Limitations

  • A bid called is a debt: once called, it is on the book, and he may not withdraw it
  • Whatever he has banked in the thing by the second call has to go somewhere, and if the third call does not spend it down the plane he chose, it comes back up the hand that is still on the wood
  • So he cannot stop at twice; by Bett’s account he broke his wrist so at twenty-six
  • It has no single-blow form, and the calls are three, never one or two
  • He needs both pushes banked in the same thing, and his left hand on it to feel the shiver come back
  • What does not reach the wood burns in his Shell’s furnace and comes off him as heat: scalp streaming and steaming, face red, breath hard for two or three breaths
  • Riving the pell split the edge of his right hand from the little finger to the wrist
  • Where he stands matters: on rich ground with nobody else on it the breath would be short, and in a crowded yard, with forty people pulling on the same stone, it runs long, which gives anyone watching more time
  • On a man it would want two touches landed in time on the same body before the third could do anything at all, and a man does not stand still in a socket the way a pell does; that is the yard’s guess and Bett’s, and it has not been tested
  • Its tell is the three calls, with the copper gathering to one bright line on the right hand by the third, and the second call’s ring louder than the first by more than twice
  • It is beaten by getting between the calls: moving, breaking his touch or striking him after the second call turns his own stored swing on his arm
  • The long breath before the first call is the other opening, longest on crowded or poor ground
