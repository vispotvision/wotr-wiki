Ned Chapman — Abilities in full

Ned Chapman — Abilities in full

Nov 28, 20268 min read

Card: Ned Chapman

Techniques

GUESSING WHOSE IT WAS · the knack, a skill on his sheet, Adept

GUESSING WHOSE IT WAS is basically psychometry: the power to hold a used thing for a minute and have his hands learn how its last owner held it. It is a knack he cannot name and nobody has explained. He is unwoken and has no Trait slot, so his sheet lists it as a trade skill, and the name is the sheet’s own, which he first saw as a boy and has never stopped finding funny.

Capabilities

Half of it is the trade and needs no knack. A used thing keeps a record of how it was used whether or not anybody ever reads it. Brass and latten wear a grain at a time where skin rides them, as a step wears where feet fall. An edge keeps the lie of the hone’s strokes, and a stem bent once and bent back keeps a frosted look where the metal was worked. An appraiser reads that with his eyes and thumb, and Ned is a good one.

The knack starts where the eyes stop. The wear is the track of one body’s habit, the same motion made ten thousand times until the hand does it without being asked. What comes to him in the minute is that habit itself, laid into his own hands and forearms: the grip, the angle of the wrist, the tucked elbow, the stroke the thing knew best and the one time it was loaded hardest. It gives a how and never a how hard. The steel remembers the shape of the cut and does not remember the arm that drove it, so a dead trooper’s falling stroke in a peddler’s hand is exact and could not go through a loaf.

To anyone watching it is a buyer’s look and nothing else. He turns the thing up to the light and lays his thumb where the last thumb lay: the worn seat on a spoon’s knop, the blunt of a blade above the cross, a buckler’s grip bar. His fist closes, his breath holds, and his eyes go to the middle distance for the length of a slow minute. In his head there is a dry riffle and a faint smell of old paper and attic dust, and both are his alone. No light comes on his hands and no color into the air, because an unwoken man has none to show.

In a fight he has no Essence, so nothing rides the blow but the borrowed shape, and the shape is the arms’ alone. On the Furlong the messer’s dead owner gave him a twitch out of a bind that split Hob Stile’s scalp and stopped on the bone, because the placing was a veteran’s and the force was a walking man’s. On the Hem road Alan Thwaite’s spear gave him a levy’s short hard thrust from the right hip while his own hips stayed square, so the wolf read his shoulders and the point skidded.

Why it is slow is his own reckoning, worked out from the outside the way he works out a price. Every soul has a hard bright knot under the breastbone that the woken pull the world’s own magic (Aether, mana in plain speech) through and turn into their own. His has sealed over like a well with a stone on it, and Kemp’s finger named it Capped. A capped soul still seeps, he thinks, and the knack runs on the seep. A seep fills a cup slowly, which is why a read takes a minute. That is a chapman’s guess built from tongs and cartwheels. It is unconfirmed, and no Measurewright has looked at him.

Applications

  • Learning a thing’s last owner: the hand, the height, the reach, how long and how carefully it was used, whether it bit flesh, how the last hand left it, and its hardest load
  • Proving goods false, as with forty “came-home” bucklers at Tollgarth, proved a week from the forge and aged in cow’s piss
  • Borrowing a dead owner’s grip, guard and best stroke for a fight
  • Reading his father’s purse in the loft
  • Reading the deserter’s messer, whose owner’s low guard he calls the Boar
  • Reading Alan Thwaite’s levy spear: five musters in a file, the short hard thrust from the right hip off the back foot, the head always coming round to the left shoulder after, and the hardest load a great weight (a horse, he thinks) stopped on the set spear

Techniques

  • THE SLOW MINUTE - skin on a used thing, turned to the light like any buyer pricing it, and in a minute he has the hand, the height, the reach, the years and the care, whether it bit flesh, its hardest load, and how the last hand left it; never called aloud, and to the whole bridge it is a chapman making up his mind
  • THE BORROWED HOLD - his hands settle into the dead owner’s grip and guard and keep the shape a quarter of an hour, perfectly drawn and with nothing behind it; a picture of a cut, he calls it

Associations

  • No Path, no Wellspring and no Form: he is unwoken and Capped, and the sheet cannot see the knack’s root
  • The appraiser’s trade, which reads wear by eye and thumb and supplies the why from nine years of pricing dead men’s goods
  • The same trade gives him what else he has in a fight: taking a length with the staff and reading it at the ell notch (forty-five inches), which his father taught him at nine; the measuring slide (Staff, Initiate); and setting a spear’s butt, which needs no force (Spear, Novice)
  • On the Hem’s hard crown his hobnails slid, the butt skated and the lugless head let the bitch come up the shaft
  • The crier’s voice: he read two wolves’ shared light as two dealers working a farmer and cried it, and crying boots at the wolves backed one off
  • A dark, quiet loft or an empty field at dusk makes a read loud and clean

Limitations

  • It gives only what a hand did, so there is never a name, a face or a thought in it, and the why of anything is his own inference
  • It gives a how and never a how hard: the borrowed stroke lands at his own force, which is Swordwork F and Spear F
  • He has no reserve, no Essence and no light to show
  • A read costs a slow minute, and most of a second minute in a thick crowd
  • By the fourth read on a dead men’s morning there is a first ache behind his eyes, and a headache by dusk; two loud reads in a day bring the ache early
  • Its tell is skin on the thing for a slow minute with his eyes going far, and then a quarter of an hour of the dead owner’s hold in his hands: a scullion’s flat scouring grip on a buckler, a left-handed man’s two fingers into the mouth of a purse, a horseman’s high guard on a saber
  • A trained eye across the yard has nothing else to read but a peddler holding a thing a little oddly
  • Only one hand’s story comes at a time, the hand that held the thing the way his own holds it, so a thing with many owners gives him the one his grip happens to match
  • The record has to be there to be read: fire wipes it, which he has only as Measurewrights’ hearsay, and scouring or a long soak leaves motions and loads and little else
  • Cloth between skin and thing stops it dead, which is why he bound Bett’s grip through a rag
  • A thick crowd on the ground he stands on muddies a read like a voice heard through a wall
  • A fighter who closes inside the minute beats it
  • It fails in its own way, and no Wellspring’s: the hold is somebody else’s whole habit, the bad half with the good, set on a body with the wrong feet under it, and it will not be argued with for a quarter of an hour
  • On the Hem road the dead man’s drilled head-turn to the left shoulder came with the thrust and took his eyes off the bitch at the worst moment
  • A shock can knock the hold out of the arm for a breath, and then it takes the arm back
  • A loud read leaves a habit behind for a day or two; the messer’s left his right thumb hooking for a nail
  • A thing held to the end reads that way too, and the hold clenches on it, so he has to open his fingers one at a time
  • Some things he will not read: he will not hold Tizz’s Long Arm to price it, and at Kemp’s he made no read at all, his hand going toward a yard-iron and coming back
